An put two folders on her desk and wrote different names across the covers.

The first read Employer obligations. The second read Missing-person investigation.

Luo had offered to arrange another joint call involving company representatives, the local partner, and the family's advisers. An declined the proposed format. The employer could answer questions about its staff, records, assistance, and expenses. The case investigators could decide what evidence to seek and what information to disclose. A company meeting would not become a substitute for the official process.

She wrote the distinction in an email, then read it to Yanchuan before sending.

“Too severe?” she asked.

“No.”

“Then why do you sound worried?”

“I'm used to asking them for help.”

“We still need their help. I don't want them to define what counts as a completed search.”

“And if they stop paying the expenses?” he asked.

“Then I will ask why they stopped. I won't exchange the word completed for another month of assistance.” She looked at the sheet of reimbursements. “I'm frightened of having to say that. Don't make me say it alone.”

The email requested an updated account of support offered, reimbursements still owed, travel records corrected, and company files preserved. It also asked for a named person to handle practical assistance without making the family repeat the disappearance to each new manager.

The missing-person case remained with the established investigators and counsel. No company declaration about an employee's plans would decide whether that inquiry ended.

Wanjiru reviewed the separation and suggested a few changes to avoid implying legal duties whose scope had not been established. An accepted them. What she wanted was a workable boundary, not an accusation disguised as a template.

On Friday Luo called and spoke more carefully than he had in November.

“We can name a coordinator,” he said. “We can account for the expenses. But there are questions we cannot answer.”

“Then say which questions,” An replied. “Don't answer a different one and call it closure.”

Yanchuan listened without interrupting. Months earlier he would have pushed the conversation toward the fourteen minutes and demanded to know who changed the document. An kept the company on the subject it could actually address.

When the call ended, she handed him the reimbursement spreadsheet to check. Three items lacked receipts because local transport companies had never issued them in a form accepted by the employer. She suggested alternatives: contemporaneous records, payment confirmations, and a clear explanation, without pretending a missing receipt existed.

Yanchuan completed the evidence column. It was unglamorous and worth doing.

In Kenya, Amina was relieved when she received the new contact protocol. She could direct technical questions to the operator and requests about a missing person to the case team. She would no longer be asked to infer which kind of inquiry a hurried email belonged to.

Daniel's workplace case continued on its own terms. Rose told An that the corrected payment statement had helped, but treatment appointments were still costly. An asked whether she wanted another reconciliation. Rose did, and the women set a time without discussing the Lin family's next lead.

There were now two families whose needs had become visible to one another without being made conditional on exchanging useful testimony.

At work, the first paid pilot contract was ready for review. Chen placed it before Yanchuan and the other engineer, a woman named Ning who had spent years working with factory control systems. The project would build a forecasting layer for an industrial client, but operational changes would remain subject to staff approval. The client could stop the pilot without being forced to certify a result it had not seen.

“Who gets the unflattering data?” Ning asked.

“The client and the engineering team,” Chen said.

“Who decides whether a bad week is excluded from the report?”

“Not the marketing department.”

They wrote the rule down.

Yanchuan noticed how much this sounded like the argument he had made with his brother's file. He was not trying to turn a tragedy into a product. He was trying to keep ordinary errors from surviving behind a neat chart.

When he told An about the contract, she asked whether it would take him away from the case.

“I don't think so. The team has other people. And we've stopped making every request depend on me.”

“That was the point.”

She did not congratulate him. She told him their son wanted to speak about his bridge project.

The boy showed his revised drawing. The bridge now had a wider base and an explicit note: holds the bus, not the truck. His teacher had asked why he included the warning.

“Because someone will try the truck,” he explained.

After the call, Yanchuan added one final item to the family protocol. If he had to travel again, another person would know how to continue the search without waiting for his return. An had already arranged most of that. He wrote it so that neither of them would have to rely on memory.

The employer replied with a named coordinator and a schedule for the outstanding expense review. It did not promise to find Yansheng. The official case contact acknowledged the corrected movement records and the technical finding. It did not promise an answer either.

For a while Yanchuan was angry at how little those two messages contained. He wanted to reply to both, but neither required another question.

A company had responsibilities. An investigation had authority. A family had a life that both could help or damage, but neither could take over.

He filed the two acknowledgments in separate folders.

On the train to Shenzhen, Yanchuan opened the new pilot specification. It began with a list of conditions under which the model must not make a recommendation.

He read it slowly. Something about the order felt right.

Before leaving, he sent An the project schedule and asked whether the next family call conflicted with her work. Her reply arrived first: Thursday, eight. Don't be late.
