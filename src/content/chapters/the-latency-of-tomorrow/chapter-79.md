The new inspection station saved twenty-seven minutes per batch on the tasks it actually measured.

The pilot report was correct. Images were captured faster, suspicious parts were identified earlier, and the operator no longer had to repeat several routine comparisons by hand. Arjun approved the figure after checking the baseline.

Then a finished batch waited an additional forty minutes at the loading door.

The new workflow had removed the manual inspection step in which a worker also informed dispatch that the batch was ready. The scanning program updated quality status. It did not send the message the warehouse expected, and nobody had assigned that small coordination task elsewhere.

“It's not an inspection task,” the manager said.

“It was done during inspection,” Leela replied.

They walked through the process with the people who performed it. Every time a worker paused to check a part, there were other actions: turning it for light, noticing the packaging condition, calling a supervisor about a mismatch, telling dispatch whether to prepare a pallet. The model supported one decision among many.

Yanchuan asked them to draw the tasks as actions rather than job titles. The first diagram had arrows crossing everywhere. It looked untidy and was immediately more useful than the neat list of functions in the sales contract.

A warehouse worker pointed to a place where everyone thought someone else would send the release message. They assigned a named step in the system, with an acknowledgment from dispatch. The delay disappeared in the next measured cycle.

Guo wanted to add the recovered time to the success metric.

“Only after we count the work required to do it,” Ning said.

The software could carry the message reliably once its content and authority were defined. The team recorded the implementation time and checked that the information could be corrected if a batch was recalled.

It was a small technical change and a large improvement in the usefulness of the workflow.

At Henglan the product team began mapping tasks that were routinely bundled together by experienced employees. Some could be automated without harm. Others needed a human judgement, a physical act or a relationship with another team. Still others were not essential and could be stopped openly instead of being silently lost.

The employment-impact reviews became more interesting when workers were asked what they actually did rather than which part of the software they used.

A supervisor complained that the conversations were expensive.

“Then the job was more complicated than the proposal assumed,” Yanchuan said.

He remembered Zhou's empty desk and the missing courier contact. The task of typing an invoice had been easy to count. The work of making sure an unusual shipment reached the right place had not.

An listened to the story that evening while preparing a reconciliation for one of her clients. She said her best employee often noticed a payment delay before it appeared in the official aging report, because she knew a customer who always called when a bank transfer failed.

“Could software do that?” Yanchuan asked.

“Perhaps, with a different process. But first someone would have to notice the call is part of the job.”

Her son appeared behind her with an unfinished model bridge. He had made the supports stronger but forgotten that someone had to lift the barrier before the toy bus could cross.

“Who does that?” Yanchuan asked.

“I do,” the boy said.

“Then it's part of operating your bridge.”

“It's the fun part.”

Yanchuan laughed. Adults often treated the unmeasured parts of a job as waste. Sometimes they were the parts that made the job bearable.

At the next factory trial the automatic inspection, release notice and warehouse acknowledgment worked together. The measured throughput improved. Staff still reviewed uncertain marks and handled physical exceptions. Their work changed, but it did not disappear into a number.

Arjun asked the team to describe the remaining responsibilities before calculating potential staffing changes. He did not promise every position would stay exactly the same. He required decisions about people to be made by the employer through its own obligations, using a more truthful account of the work.

The final process map filled a wall. It was too large for Guo's brochure.

He photographed it for his own reference and asked the team to prepare a smaller explanation without removing the human handoffs.

Yanchuan wrote at the bottom: A task can end without ending the work around it.

Leela read the sentence and crossed out can.

“Sometimes,” she wrote instead.

He kept her correction.
