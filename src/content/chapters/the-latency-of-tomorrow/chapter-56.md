The clause occupied four lines at the bottom of page nineteen.

If the measured service fell below the guaranteed range, the equipment provider would receive a grace period before penalties applied. The customer would continue paying the availability fee during that period, subject to a separate credit calculated later.

Guo's lawyer called it an ordinary cure provision. Yanchuan called Chen before signing his technical review.

“What is the fee buying during the grace period?” he asked.

“Availability,” Chen answered.

“If the service isn't available?”

“Then we need to see what the credit does.”

They built a small example. A factory might pay for standby capacity that the provider could not deliver during a hot week. The contract would postpone the penalty while requiring the factory to arrange its own replacement supply. The eventual credit might cover the availability fee but not the operational costs of the interruption.

Guo's team had priced the investor's risk as though the grace period were protection against an expensive failure. The clause had not reduced the failure's consequences. It had shifted some of them onto the customer.

“Who drafted it?” Yanchuan asked.

“The legal team adapted a template,” Guo said. “That's what templates are for.”

“Then this template has a price.”

Guo asked him to calculate it.

The calculation depended on the probability of failure, the factory's cost of obtaining replacement service, and how long a grace period could realistically last. They had too little information for a precise expected loss. But they could show a range and identify the missing terms required for a fair comparison.

A commercial director said every contract assigned risk somewhere. Yanchuan agreed. He wanted the assignment to be visible before it became a dispute.

Ning examined the technical schedule. The equipment would remain advisory in some modes and under operator control in others. A performance guarantee could not rely on the system deciding when to intervene if it had no authority to do so.

“We cannot guarantee the outcome of a decision we aren't allowed to make,” she said.

The lawyers went back to the clause. The revised provision identified response times, the evidence needed to establish an outage, who would arrange substitute capacity and which costs each party would bear. It also allowed the customer to refuse a remedial operation that violated safety limits without losing the contractual credit.

Guo called the revision expensive.

“It makes the expense visible,” Chen replied.

Yanchuan had begun to enjoy watching the two men conduct this argument. Neither was wholly wrong. Guo needed agreements that could finance machinery. Chen needed the engineering promises to remain defensible. The difficulty was not that one wanted money and the other wanted truth. It was that a poorly designed contract could make both parties believe they had purchased different things.

That night An asked him to look at an agreement from a supplier who offered a discount in exchange for faster payment. The discount was genuine, but an additional clause limited the supplier's responsibility for goods that arrived damaged.

“Does the discount cover the risk?” she asked.

He asked how often damage occurred, how quickly replacements could arrive and whether her customers would bear the delay. She had most of the figures in her own records.

They compared them without pretending the calculation made the decision for her. She chose a smaller discount under terms that retained the supplier's ordinary responsibility for damage.

“I could have guessed that,” she said.

“You probably did.”

“It's nice to know what the guess costs.”

At the family review the employer proposed closing the travel-expense reconciliation after the final payment. An agreed that the specific accounting task could be completed, provided it did not alter the missing-person case or waive unrelated rights. Wanjiru reviewed the wording.

Yanchuan noticed how similar the question was to Guo's grace clause. A narrow administrative completion could become a broad release if someone wrote it that way. An had learned to check what a signature would mean outside the line she intended to settle.

Later Mei asked why Guo kept returning with new financing proposals.

“Because he thinks the service can grow,” Yanchuan said.

“And because you keep telling him where the numbers aren't stable.”

“That's not a flattering reason to employ us.”

“It can be valuable if he listens. It can be useful camouflage if he doesn't.”

He thought about that for a long time.

The final margin clause was longer than the original, with fewer opportunities for either party to say later that it had expected something else. Guo signed off on the extra cost for a limited pilot, retaining a separate decision about broader investment.

As Yanchuan filed the agreement, a message from Amina arrived. The operator had approved the work plan for the corrected acceptance test, but the schedule depended on replacement equipment and worker availability. It was not yet a date on which anyone could promise a passing result.

He replied that the distinction was understood.

In his notebook he wrote: A margin can be a buffer, a price, or a way of moving a cost out of view. The word alone doesn't tell you which.

He closed the notebook and looked at the empty place on his calendar where another trip to Kenya might eventually fit. There was still no specific appointment requiring him there.

The next morning Chen returned the draft with the fee formula highlighted. Yanchuan opened the spreadsheet again rather than answering the investor's congratulatory message.
