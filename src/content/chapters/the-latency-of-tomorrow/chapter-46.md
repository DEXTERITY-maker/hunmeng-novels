The priority column had four numbers and no explanation.

One was assigned to the packaging line. Two to the chilled storage room. Three to an office air conditioner. Four to the rest-area ventilation. The proposed energy-control plan would preserve lower-numbered loads first when capacity was constrained.

Yanchuan asked the analyst who had made the list.

“Operations supplied it,” she said.

“Which person in operations?”

The analyst checked the filename. There was no name attached, only a version date. The row for rest-area ventilation had been added after the first model was trained.

Gao looked at the ranking and shook his head.

“The office unit can stop before the rest-area fan,” he said.

The commercial manager explained that the office unit served a room with network equipment. The air conditioner had been labeled office because that was where its breaker was located.

“Then the label is wrong,” Gao said.

The four numbers were not merely priorities. They were a compressed account of what the system thought each circuit did. Several people in the room had accepted the table without noticing that the names could conceal different functions.

Ning proposed a review with the owners of the loads. The office manager could describe its equipment. The safety officer could explain ventilation limits. Maintenance could identify what actually shared each circuit.

Guo asked whether the priority order could change with time.

“It must,” the safety officer said. “A rest area can be empty at night and crowded during the afternoon heat. The network room has a limit at every hour. Don't ask us to choose a permanent winner from four lines.”

They replaced the single ranking with constraints and a documented escalation process. Some loads were essential under particular conditions. Others could be shifted after a named person confirmed it. The model would be allowed to suggest a sequence, not silently decide whose requirement mattered more.

At the hospital Qiao faced a different priority list. The ward had received fewer transport slots than requested, and an administrator wanted to sort patients by a simple measure of readiness. Qiao had a patient who could leave only when a relative with a suitable vehicle arrived, another who needed a different care arrangement, and several whose transfers could happen at ordinary times.

“The system thinks a high score makes the patient more movable,” she told Yanchuan.

“Doesn't it reflect some of the constraints?”

“Yes. It doesn't reflect all of them. And those that are missing aren't always small.”

She had no interest in importing the factory model to the ward. But she wanted her brother to understand why an apparently objective ordering could become an excuse not to listen to the person responsible for the decision.

He told her about the mislabeled office cooler.

“A label that hides the equipment,” she said. “We've got those. Ours hide people.”

Yanchuan thought of the missing-person chronology. In its early versions he had given every lead an urgency rating. A report discrepancy, a hotel receipt, a transport charge. He had once moved a lead higher because it felt like a way to reach his brother, not because its source was stronger.

An had made him replace the ratings with source, status and next action.

At the scheduled review he thanked her for it.

“Which change?” she asked.

“The priorities.”

“You thanked me at the time.”

“I don't think I understood why.”

She turned the camera to show their son building a road network from strips of paper. The boy was trying to make the school bus reach a bridge without crossing a toy railway at the wrong moment.

“Who goes first?” Yanchuan asked.

“The train,” the boy said. “Unless it's not coming.”

“Who knows whether it's coming?”

The boy pointed to himself.

“Then you have the job.”

The simple answer made him laugh. The boy had not created an objective rule outside himself. He had appointed a person to watch and decide.

At the factory the revised priority method came back from review. It was longer and less elegant. It included a place to record reasons for overrides and a named duty holder for unusual conditions. The commercial manager asked whether it could be shortened for a presentation.

“Yes,” Ning said. “We can summarize the process. We cannot replace it with the summary.”

Guo asked the team to calculate how much extra savings the original ranking would have promised. They did. Most of the difference came from treating the rest-area ventilation as freely interruptible during the hottest hours.

“Then the bigger number wasn't available to buy,” he said.

Yanchuan was surprised. Guo did not always choose the more defensible figure. He was a businessman, and Yanchuan had seen him negotiate hard for favorable terms. But he seemed to understand that a contract built on a load no one could safely turn off was not an asset simply because a model assigned it a number.

The revised pilot report listed the constraints first and the savings second.

After the meeting Gao walked Yanchuan to the gate. Workers were changing shifts. Some paused to buy drinks from a cart; others hurried toward buses.

“When people see the priority column,” Gao said, “they think somebody has decided what matters.”

“Somebody has.”

“Then put the somebody beside it.”

Yanchuan promised to propose the change. He had already learned that decisions survived their makers when the record preserved only the result.

He added a column for authority, and another for the reason it had been exercised. The table no longer fit on one screen. Gao asked for a printout large enough to read at the shift briefing; Yanchuan sent it before anybody reduced the font.
