The reserve pump had not run for thirty-six days. It still appeared on the factory's expense sheet every week.

Guo's analyst wanted to know why.

Gao led him to the equipment room, where the standby pump sat beside a working unit. Its casing was clean. A paper label identified the last inspection and the technician responsible for the next. It had not supplied the main system that month. Its purpose was to be available if the working pump failed.

“We could reduce the maintenance schedule,” the analyst suggested.

“You could,” Gao replied. “Then you would have a different reserve.”

Yanchuan stood near the door with Ning. They had been asked to extend their pilot report from energy savings to whole-service cost. The proposal sounded modest until the first spreadsheet arrived. It counted electricity used by equipment while idle but not the inspections, replacement parts, occupied space or staff time needed to keep it ready.

“Your model treats readiness as free,” Ning said.

“It's a forecasting model,” the analyst answered.

“And now you're trying to use it to price a contract.”

Guo listened without intervening. He had invited the disagreement, but Yanchuan wondered whether he would still welcome it after he saw the revised estimate.

They built the cost from actual maintenance records. Testing consumed power. Spare seals expired. The pump's availability depended on someone noticing a fault before the primary unit broke. There were months when the reserve was not used, and moments when its existence could determine whether the factory completed an order.

The analyst objected that a rare failure should be averaged across years.

“Average the cost of an event if you need a budget,” Gao said. “But an average pump will not appear when this one stops.”

They agreed to represent two separate quantities: expected expenditure over time and the minimum reserve required for the factory's agreed service. One could support a financial decision. The other could not be canceled because an average looked small.

On Friday Chen presented the revised analysis. The forecast-driven schedule offered genuine savings, but less than the first estimate suggested once reserve inspections and operating constraints were included. A sales director asked whether they could publish the earlier figure and note the other costs elsewhere.

“No,” Chen said.

It was the shortest answer of the meeting.

Guo asked for a comparison between maintaining the current reserve and accepting a lower service commitment. They could prepare one, Yanchuan said. It would make the trade-off visible rather than pretend the same level of reliability could be purchased more cheaply by changing a cell.

Outside the factory a contractor was replacing a worn pipe support. Yanchuan watched the work for a few minutes. The worker had laid out several bolts before taking down the old bracket, knowing a replacement could be the wrong size. The job seemed slow only if the preparation was ignored.

He told his father about it that night.

“Your brother used to do that,” Jianming said. “Put the new part beside the old before touching the screws.”

“You taught him.”

“I taught both of you. One of you listened earlier.”

Yanchuan smiled. He could hear a lamp being switched on and off in Wuhan, his father's habitual test after a repair.

Jianming asked about the investigation. Yanchuan said that the technical record was being corrected but the police still had no confirmed account of his brother's movements after the annex visit. His father asked whether the family needed more money for another journey.

“Not until there's a reason to travel,” Yanchuan answered. “Keep it for your repairs.”

“You think you can budget a missing son?”

“No. I'm trying not to spend what we'll need if a real opportunity comes.”

His father was quiet. Then he said that he had put aside a small sum anyway. He would not empty his emergency savings. Qiao had made him promise.

Later An sent her monthly household account. She had included a line for sudden travel and another for ordinary things their son would need whether his father came home tomorrow or not. Yanchuan noticed she had not labeled the first line rescue. It was money held ready, without a guarantee it would be used.

He thought of the pump.

At the next pilot review Guo approved the smaller savings claim and the separate reserve estimate. His analyst asked whether this would make their business less competitive.

“It might,” Guo said. “Don't tell the customer otherwise.” The analyst had spent three evenings preparing the first figure. He left with both versions of the spreadsheet and no permission to use the better-looking one. Gao caught up with him by the lift and invited him to the next pump inspection. The analyst said he had another meeting. Gao gave him the date anyway.

“Only if our competitors are promising the same service without paying to keep it available,” Guo said.

“That happens,” Ning replied.

“Then we need to decide whether we want to compete that way.”

There was no applause. The contract still had to pay wages, satisfy the client and leave enough for future maintenance. The honest figure did not solve the business problem. It showed where the problem was.

Yanchuan updated the pilot handbook with a test that had nothing to do with prediction error. Before calling an unused asset unnecessary, identify the event it is meant to survive, the person accountable for keeping it available, and the consequence of removing it.

Gao read the sentence, then crossed out survive.

“An asset doesn't survive an event,” he said. “The people and the work do.”

Yanchuan changed the wording.

On the way home he passed a fire station. The vehicles were parked, gleaming beneath bright lights. The station did not look productive in the way a factory line did. He could imagine a spreadsheet treating its silence as waste.

A firefighter stood at the entrance rolling a hose back onto its rack. From the street the station looked idle. The firefighter did not.
