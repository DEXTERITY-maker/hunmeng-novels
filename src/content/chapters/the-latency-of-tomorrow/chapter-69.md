The contract did not say what would happen to the time the factory saved.

Arjun had agreed to pay for a system that reduced unplanned inspection delays. The pilot was producing a modest benefit. Fewer batches required repeat checks, and maintenance staff received some warnings early enough to arrange work without stopping the line unexpectedly.

A production supervisor proposed increasing the output target accordingly.

Leela asked whether the workers who performed the extra work would receive additional training, staffing or compensation. The supervisor said the contract with the model provider did not cover labor arrangements.

“Then I'm asking the factory,” she replied.

Yanchuan had come to review the pilot's second milestone. He had expected a technical meeting. Instead the team was discussing who owned the benefit of being less surprised by machinery.

Arjun listened and said he would not decide the production target in that room. The improvement had been measured over a limited period, and the maintenance burden had changed. Before adding work, the factory needed to understand whether the benefit was stable and what staff would be asked to do with it.

Guo wanted to know whether that would reduce the value of his investment.

“It might change who receives the value,” Chen answered.

The commercial agreement described data access, model support and service fees. It did not assign the factory's productivity gains to a particular group of people. That decision belonged to local management and the workforce under their own arrangements. Treating the silence as permission for any outcome would be another way of moving cost beyond the chart.

At lunch Leela told Yanchuan she did not oppose the model. She had helped make it useful. She opposed turning a reduced burden in one task into an invisible requirement to do more of everything else.

“A machine makes one part easier,” she said. “Then a manager says we were never working hard enough.”

He asked whether she had discussed it with Arjun before.

“Yes. He's listening. That's more than nothing.”

She turned her cup in her hands. “Last year we stayed late for a rush order and they thanked us with a speech. Then the extra hours became the starting schedule. I don't intend to watch that happen twice.”

Yanchuan had come expecting to measure minutes saved. He asked what the old rush order had cost in overtime. Leela said the payroll office would have the records, and she wanted the workers to see any comparison before it appeared in an investor's presentation.

The next month the factory agreed to a limited productivity trial with clear workload and safety measures. Staff representatives were included in deciding how the changed process would work. Training time was counted. The result would be reviewed rather than made permanent on the strength of a single favorable quarter.

Guo asked Yanchuan to draft a generic method for estimating the economic value of such a pilot. Yanchuan included direct operating savings, maintenance costs, training costs, and a section identifying distributional questions that the model could not settle on behalf of the customer.

“Investors don't like questions without answers,” Guo said.

“Then investors should know which answers they are paying someone else to make.”

The remark annoyed Guo. He called back the next day and asked for a shorter explanation suitable for a financing discussion. They agreed on one: a system's measured benefit does not decide how its benefit should be shared.

That evening An was completing her accounts. A supplier had offered faster software for reconciling payments, but the proposal assumed the same number of staff would process twice as many transactions. Her colleagues had asked whether the faster system would reduce overtime or simply raise daily targets.

“What did you tell them?” Yanchuan asked.

“That we should test what it saves before promising where the savings go.”

He smiled. Their conversations had become repetitive in a way he no longer disliked.

At the family review Wanjiru reported no decisive development in Yansheng's case. A request for further documentary clarification remained active. An asked whether there was a specific action the family needed to take; there was not.

For the first time, Yanchuan had an evening free from both a new lead and a project emergency. He called his father and listened to a long account of a neighbour's faulty electric kettle. Jianming had refused to repair it with an unsuitable part. The neighbour bought a new kettle.

“You didn't save him any money,” Yanchuan said.

“I saved him a false repair.”

They spoke about the weather afterward.

The factory's productivity trial began with employees signing the revised work instructions through the established process. Leela checked that the maintenance team's new tasks were included and that someone would evaluate the added workload. The pilot model continued its narrow job, marking parts that deserved inspection.

One afternoon Arjun stood beside Yanchuan and watched a warning appear on the screen. The operator examined the part and carried on after deciding it was acceptable.

“That's a refusal,” Arjun said.

“An informed one.”

“And we've learned something about the process.”

They had. The model's value was not simply the count of warnings accepted. It was the way it helped the factory direct attention while keeping decisions with the people responsible for the work.

The commercial contract had purchased a tool. The workshop still had to decide what kind of workplace that tool would help create.

Leela asked for the staffing discussion to be attached to the technical sign-off. Arjun agreed, though it meant the pilot could not be called complete that afternoon.
