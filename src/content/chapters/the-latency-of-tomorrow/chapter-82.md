An took the morning off because she remembered the date without opening a calendar.

For weeks people had asked whether she wanted to arrange something on the anniversary. She had said no to a public gathering, no to a statement for the company and no to an interview with a reporter who promised to treat the case gently. On the morning itself she still did not know whether she wanted anyone at the apartment.

Her son made a decision for her by putting three bowls on the table.

“Uncle Yanchuan is coming,” he announced.

“He's coming after lunch.”

“Then I will keep his bowl clean.”

She watched him move a hand-painted bowl away from the breakfast dishes. It had a small chip on the edge. Yansheng had brought it back from a journey before Kenya, wrapped inside a shirt because he had forgotten to buy proper packing material.

The boy had asked whether he could use it. She had said yes. Objects did not have to be left untouched to be remembered.

In Shenzhen, Yanchuan was supposed to give Chen a revised feasibility estimate before boarding the train. He completed the calculation and sent it without adding a message about the day. Chen replied that the review could wait until Monday and told him to take care on the journey.

Outside the station, people queued for taxis under a hard blue sky. On his phone the family chronology still began with the October call and a number of records that had failed to establish what happened afterward. The technical acceptance dispute had produced a corrected finding. The missing-person inquiry remained open.

He did not open the chronology on the train.

An met him near the market because she wanted help buying vegetables, not because she could not carry them. They stood beside a stall choosing tomatoes. She rejected one bag for bruises the seller could see and another for its unnecessary plastic wrapping.

“You're staring,” she said.

“I was trying to think what to say.”

“You can tell me if these are too ripe.”

He picked up a tomato. She laughed, and he was grateful that she did not need the laugh explained.

Their son returned from school carrying a drawing of a bridge. This one had two spans, with a walkway beneath the upper deck. He said his teacher had asked the class to draw something people could use.

“Who uses the lower path?” Yanchuan asked.

“People who aren't in cars.”

“And if it floods?”

“I put the path higher than the river.”

The child's attention had moved to ordinary questions about space, weight and people. Yanchuan listened instead of searching the drawing for a metaphor his nephew had never intended.

That evening Jianming called from Wuhan. Qiao had visited him after her shift and was sitting near the window with a cup of tea. Their father had wanted to travel but had been advised to rest his wrist and keep a follow-up appointment.

“I can come next month,” he said.

“We know,” An replied. “You don't have to make a journey to prove you're thinking about him.”

Jianming nodded as though she could see him. He asked his grandson about school, listened to the bridge explanation and told a story about Yansheng as a boy.

Yansheng had once dismantled the family's alarm clock to discover why the second hand caught every minute. He had lost one small spring, spent two afternoons looking for it and finally found it inside his own sleeve. Their mother had been furious because everyone was late for school.

“You've told that one,” Qiao said.

“Yes,” Jianming replied. “I remember it.”

The boy asked whether the clock had worked afterward.

“For a while.”

“That's not much.”

“It was enough to get us to school the next morning.”

After the call An put a small plate of sliced fruit on the table. She did not arrange a place for Yansheng or turn the meal into a ceremony. She told the boy that his father had been gone for a long time and that the adults were still trying to find out what happened. He could ask questions whenever he wanted, and he did not have to ask any tonight.

He asked whether the police had stopped.

“No,” she said. “They still have the case. We don't know when there will be an answer.”

“Would Dad remember my new bridge?”

“He would want to see it.”

“That's different.”

“Yes,” she said. “It is.”

The three of them sat quietly. The boy picked up his drawing and explained the lower path again, this time making Yanchuan draw in a ramp.

Later, while washing dishes, An said she had received a message from Luo asking whether the company could send a commemorative gift. She had declined. There was no need to debate his motives. A gift arriving with the employer's name would have made an already difficult day carry an additional obligation.

“Did he say anything about the investigation?” Yanchuan asked.

“No. He said he hoped we were well.”

“I don't know what that means.”

“I don't need to know.”

She handed him the chipped bowl to dry.

The following morning Yanchuan called Wanjiru during their agreed case-update window. He asked whether any material development had occurred since the last official notice. She said no new verified movement evidence had been released, and the inquiry remained active. She expressed care for the family without suggesting an anniversary changed the evidentiary standard.

He thanked her and ended the call. For once he did not ask her to repeat the questions already on record.

On his return journey he found a photograph An had sent: her son kneeling beside the bridge drawing, holding the repaired toy bus. The picture was slightly blurred because the boy had tried to make the bus move during the exposure.

He saved it in the family folder, not the case archive.

That night Jianming sent a message to both his children. He had found the old clock, now broken beyond any worthwhile repair. He would not throw it away yet. He had put it in a drawer where he could find it again.

Yanchuan wrote back that he would look at it on his next visit, if their father wanted.

He understood that some dates belonged to a chronology and some belonged to the people who survived them. October twenty-ninth was both. It would remain both even if the inquiry one day reached a conclusion.

He placed his phone beside the bed and left the calendar open to the next ordinary day.
