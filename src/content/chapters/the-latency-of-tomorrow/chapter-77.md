The desk was gone when Ning returned to the client's office.

A cabinet and two chairs occupied the place where a production clerk named Ms. Zhou had spent eight years tracking late delivery paperwork. The factory had deployed a document intake tool. Managers celebrated the removal of duplicate typing, and a space-planning contractor had cleared the furniture before the next monthly review.

“Where is Zhou?” Ning asked.

“Transferred,” a supervisor said.

“To what?”

He did not know.

The system could extract invoice numbers, compare them with orders and route discrepancies to an authorized reviewer. It did those tasks reasonably well. It did not know which courier routinely delivered to the wrong entrance or which warehouse gate was closed during school pickup hours, facts Zhou had learned through ordinary work and used to prevent delays.

A shipment arrived while Ning was in the office. The software flagged a mismatch, and the document remained unresolved until someone called the driver. Nobody knew which number to use. Zhou had usually known.

The supervisor contacted her. She was now in a temporary administrative pool, waiting for assignment. She answered the question in less than a minute.

“She shouldn't have to support the old job for free,” Ning said.

The supervisor looked uncomfortable. The automation had been justified by removing one position. It had not included a plan to transfer the knowledge that made the old process work.

Guo was furious when he heard the story. Henglan's agreement described an assisted trial and required the customer to approve changes to staffing separately. The client had treated the tool's successful demonstration as permission to reorganize work before the consequences were assessed.

“We don't control their employment decisions,” Guo said.

“No,” Ning answered. “We do control what we claimed the tool could replace.”

They reviewed the sales material. One slide described the reduction in repetitive clerical effort; another, added later, referred to full position equivalents. The qualifier that other exception-handling work remained had been moved into a footnote.

Henglan issued a correction and offered to document the remaining tasks. The client resisted, saying the change was an internal staffing decision. Ning agreed that it was. She also insisted that the technical report must not imply the system performed work it did not.

Yanchuan called Zhou with her permission to ask about the process, not to investigate her employment. She described the relationships between couriers, loading docks, warehouse staff and a few customers who never filled forms correctly.

“None of that was in the screen,” he said.

“It was in my notebook,” she replied. “Nobody asked for the notebook until my desk was gone.”

She did not want her private notes copied into a vendor's training database. She did want the company to understand the jobs it still needed someone to do.

The factory eventually assigned a new worker to exception handling and invited Zhou to train the person during paid hours. Whether her transfer could be reversed was a matter between her and the employer. Henglan documented the process boundaries so future customers would not be sold an exaggerated claim of replacement.

At home An listened to Yanchuan describe the empty space.

“They moved the furniture before deciding who would handle the problems,” she said.

“Apparently.”

“Then they thought the job was the desk.”

He remembered how the family's first account of Yansheng had reduced his work to travelling and signing forms. Only after he disappeared had Yanchuan learned about maintenance tickets, acceptance boundaries and the people who relied on his questions.

A job could disappear from a chart long before its work disappeared from the world.

At the next Henglan meeting Ning required an employment-impact section in every proposed automation study. It did not give the vendor authority over staffing. It required the customer to identify which tasks would remain, which knowledge needed transfer and who would carry the new work.

Guo worried the requirement would slow sales.

“It should slow promises we can't support,” she said.

A month later Zhou wrote to say the new exception-handling process was working better. She had accepted a different permanent role with a clearer schedule. She did not thank the software.

Yanchuan didn't ask her to.

The empty desk remained in his mind longer than the polished demonstration. He had seen machines complete tasks. He was learning how many tasks had never been named.
