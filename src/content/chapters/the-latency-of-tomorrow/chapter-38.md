The first invoice was smaller than the cost of Yanchuan's journey to Kenya.

Ning held it up beside the pilot contract and said, “Don't spend our success before we deliver anything.”

She was responsible for controls integration. Yanchuan would evaluate demand and reserve assumptions. A factory maintenance supervisor named Gao had authority to reject recommendations that interfered with safe operation. Guo's firm would finance the study, while the factory retained ownership of its operational data and the right to stop the trial.

The arrangement had taken six revisions and more patience than any of them had expected.

The pilot did not move switches. It read approved measurements, predicted near-term demand, and proposed opportunities to shift flexible loads. Operators could accept or decline suggestions. Every recommendation would keep a record of its inputs and assumptions.

The commercial manager wanted to call it intelligent optimization. Gao called it another screen.

“Give it a month,” he said. “Then we'll decide what it is.”

Their first day went badly in an ordinary way. A temperature sensor reported a room cooler than anyone inside believed. Ning checked its calibration and found it had been installed near a draft from an open loading bay. The predicted demand looked wonderful because the software thought the cooling load would remain small.

“Should we replace the sensor?” a manager asked.

“We should fix the measurement,” Ning said. “Then establish whether the old readings can be used for anything.”

The manager wanted the first week's dashboard by Friday. Ning said he could have a dashboard that showed incomplete data. He could not have a credible savings estimate based on a known bad measurement.

Yanchuan watched the exchange without adding a speech. The technician who climbed the ladder to inspect the sensor knew more about its location than anyone at the meeting. They asked her to mark the mounting point on the drawing.

In the second week the model predicted a demand peak at four. A production shift had been moved because a machine needed repair. The model had no record of the change and recommended delaying a load that was already off.

Gao laughed when he read the suggestion.

“It's telling me not to use a machine that's in pieces.”

Ning added a status input from the approved maintenance log. She did not make the model guess whether a machine was under repair from indirect patterns. A human entry about an actual repair carried information the sensors did not.

The next recommendation was better. It proposed a narrow adjustment in a period when the production team had already allowed flexibility. Gao accepted it after checking the limit on his own screen.

The result was modest: a measurable reduction in a peak that had not yet become dangerous. The team recorded the before-and-after conditions. Nobody claimed the adjustment had saved the entire facility.

Guo visited at the end of the week. He watched the operator decline two suggestions and accept one.

“Do the refusals count against your success rate?” he asked.

“Yes,” Ning said. “But they also tell us whether the system understands the workplace.”

Guo studied the audit log. One refusal was labelled insufficient maintenance margin. Another said production schedule changed. He asked whether those reasons could train future recommendations.

“Possibly,” Ning replied, “with permission and a review of what they mean. A refusal isn't always proof the recommendation was wrong. Sometimes it says the operator knows something the system doesn't.”

That night Yanchuan explained the pilot to An. She had spent the day chasing an unpaid invoice for her own business. When he said the first recommendations had been rejected, she laughed.

“Then they're honest customers.”

“We're paying attention to their reasons.”

“Good. I wish our suppliers did that.”

She asked whether he had heard anything new about Yansheng. There had been no change in the case since the last official update. He said so without dressing the silence as progress.

Their son appeared on the call with his bridge, now repaired so many times that the original cardboard was barely visible. He asked whether Yanchuan's computer could design a stronger one.

“It could suggest a design,” Yanchuan said. “We'd still have to build and test it.”

“Can it make the truck cross?”

“Maybe. It would need to know how heavy the truck is.”

The boy fetched the metal toy and held it close to the camera as if its weight could be transmitted that way.

Yanchuan laughed, then asked him to place it on a scale when An had time. He did not promise a bridge from a picture.

By the end of the first month the pilot had produced a series of recommendations with clear outcomes. Some saved money. Some were declined. Others could not be evaluated because a schedule changed or a sensor failed. The report kept all three categories.

A commercial slide showed the savings from accepted recommendations. Yanchuan added the proportion of suggestions that had not been acted on and the conditions in which the result had been measured.

Guo looked at the finished page.

“It will be harder to sell,” he said.

“It will be easier to defend,” Chen replied.

Guo allowed the page to remain.

The contract's first milestone payment arrived the following week. It covered staff time and equipment checks with little left over. Yanchuan felt relief disproportionate to the amount. For months every expenditure had seemed to compete with finding his brother. This was work that paid for itself and could be repeated if it proved useful.

He sent his father a photograph of the factory team after the review. Gao stood at the edge, refusing to smile for a company picture. Ning had grease on one sleeve. Yanchuan looked tired but less hollow than in the photograph An had taken at the airport.

Jianming replied, Your brother would ask what was missing from the photograph.

Yanchuan looked again. The night-shift operator had already left before it was taken.

He asked Ning whether they could take a second photograph with the full team at a time that suited them. She said to ask the people first.

He did.

The missing-person case remained open in its separate folder. The pilot was not an answer to it, a memorial, or a promise that technology would spare anyone uncertainty.

It was the first time Yanchuan had been paid to build a system whose limitations were part of what the customer was buying.

He considered that a beginning.
