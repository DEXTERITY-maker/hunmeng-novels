The replacement fan assembly arrived six days after the date printed on the shipping estimate.

Its delay was not a disaster. The battery remained out of the proposed service, and its owner was paying for capacity that had not yet begun to earn revenue. The difference mattered to the financing model, which still assumed a restoration date from the supplier's first estimate.

Feng called Yanchuan when the crate reached the workshop.

“Would you like to see the part?” he asked.

“I'd like to know whether it's the approved part.”

Feng laughed. “That's why I'm calling.”

The serial information matched the updated documentation. The installation would take place under the manufacturer's procedure, followed by inspection and testing at the conditions the service contract required. The technician refused a suggestion that the unit could begin limited dispatch immediately after the mechanical replacement.

“We haven't checked the system,” he said.

Guo's analyst revised the revenue start date. The investment committee complained that another delay would make the opportunity less attractive.

“Then the opportunity has changed,” Guo replied.

The team prepared a staged acceptance plan with clearly defined milestones. Each could be paid for when completed: delivery, correct installation, witnessed testing and formal availability for service. The owner agreed after calculating the cost of another premature claim.

At the same time Asha's group in Kenya was designing its small maintenance-planning study. Their first demonstration concerned a common replacement component that had repeatedly been ordered after a fault instead of being stored where it could be reached. An imported planning package had proposed a single central inventory to minimize carrying costs.

Asha's map showed why that was inadequate during the rainy season.

“Can we calculate the right place for one spare?” she asked.

Yanchuan and Ning worked from permitted aggregate information. They compared the cost of storing the part locally with the delay caused when roads were unusable. The calculation did not require them to identify individual workers or customers. It did require the local team to define which failures could safely wait.

In the first version, the apparent best location was a depot with inexpensive space. Mutiso pointed out that the depot's access road flooded during the same conditions that strained part of the network.

They changed the scenario, not the facts.

Asha's team chose a more accessible store for the spare, despite its higher carrying cost. The result was not an optimization trophy. It was a decision backed by a map, a budget and a person responsible for checking that the part remained available.

Guo asked whether they could call the result a cross-border pilot.

“Only after the local engineers say it's a pilot,” Yanchuan replied.

Asha approved a short description. It credited the team that supplied the operational knowledge and stated that the calculations were advisory. No one had installed an automated network controller. They had improved one maintenance decision.

On a video call she showed them the shelf where the part would be kept once purchased. The shelf was empty.

“We're waiting for procurement,” she said.

The empty space was more honest than a photograph of a component the team did not yet possess.

Yanchuan had grown impatient with things that were not done. He still felt the impatience. But the report included the funding approval, purchase status, expected delivery range and the actual check that would make the spare available. A future auditor would not have to infer readiness from an intention.

A few days later Feng's battery passed its authorized post-repair tests. The engineer signed the record for the tested conditions, and the owner acknowledged the commissioning update. Guo's firm adjusted the financing arrangement to the actual date.

There was no hidden miracle in the completed test. The fan worked. The thermal system remained within its agreed limits. The battery could now be considered for the service its owner wanted to provide.

“That isn't a dramatic ending,” Mei said when Yanchuan told her.

“It's a real one.”

“Those can be more useful.”

In Guangzhou An had a different reason to wait. Their son wanted to fix the toy bus wheel himself. He had watched an instructional video and believed he understood the mechanism, but he needed a replacement pin of the right size. He refused to glue the wheel on permanently because the axle would no longer turn.

“That's your family's influence,” An said.

“It's his own bus.”

“He's making us all wait for a pin.”

They ordered the correct part. The boy put the bus back in its box with a note saying repair pending and went outside to play.

That night a case update arrived. The inquiry had not reached a final conclusion about Yansheng. The operator's technical correction had been documented, and the investigators continued to assess the remaining movement evidence. The family kept the case open through its established contacts.

Yanchuan told An, then returned to the maintenance study. He had begun to see the difference between waiting because nobody owned a task and waiting because the correct next action required time.

The following week Asha sent a photograph of the newly stocked spare, its record card fixed beside it. Mutiso had checked the location and the team had documented how to replenish it after use.

In his office Yanchuan pinned a copy of the permitted photograph beside the pilot report. One available component would not solve a whole network's problems. It might shorten one outage. The local engineers knew that better than anyone.

He thought of the months spent searching for a document that could explain his brother's last assignment. Waiting had sometimes been necessary and sometimes been an excuse. The work was to tell the difference without inventing certainty.

He checked the part number against Feng's commissioning note. Only then did he open the next project's budget, where the spare-parts allowance was still blank.
