The battery had been installed, commissioned and left almost untouched for eleven months.

Its owner wanted Guo to explain why a valuable asset was not earning money. The proposal was simple: add it to the demand-management pilot, charge when electricity was inexpensive, discharge during expensive hours, and calculate the saving.

Yanchuan visited with Ning and a local maintenance engineer named Feng. The enclosure stood behind a fenced service yard. Its paint still looked new. A warning sign carried a revision date older than the commissioning certificate.

“Why isn't it running?” Yanchuan asked.

Feng handed him an inspection record. During an early maintenance check, a cooling fan had shown intermittent faults. The equipment had passed a basic electrical test, but the operator had restricted it from regular cycling until a replacement assembly arrived and a thermal review was completed.

“Can it operate at low power?” Ning asked.

“Within a limited condition, according to the manufacturer,” Feng said. “The owner chose not to rely on it for contracted service.”

The owner's representative seemed embarrassed by the explanation. The battery had been purchased to provide flexibility, and its idle state looked like failure in quarterly reports.

Guo asked for the cost of returning it to service.

They counted the fan assembly, the engineering visit, the safety test and the time needed to obtain the final authorization. The new component had been delayed by a manufacturing change. A generic replacement could be purchased immediately, but it did not carry the required certification for that enclosure.

“Couldn't you test it yourself?” the representative asked Feng.

“I can test what I'm authorized to test. I can't make a nonapproved part approved by being confident.”

Yanchuan watched the man's face tighten. There was an entire business plan resting on a battery whose safe return to service depended on an unremarkable component.

The financial model had counted the asset's capacity but not the time required to make it available. Guo asked the analyst to prepare two forecasts: one assuming approved restoration occurred on the supplier's supported date, the other treating the battery as unavailable until evidence of completion.

The first had a profitable result. The second had none during the current contract window.

“That makes it look worthless,” the representative said.

“No,” Guo replied. “It tells us the current revenue claim needs a different date.”

They did not operate the equipment for their demonstration. Feng showed the documentation for the test he intended to perform after the part arrived, including the conditions under which he would stop it.

At home Yanchuan told Jianming about the battery. His father compared it to a neighbour's emergency generator that had sat unused until a storm, when its fuel had spoiled and the owner discovered the starter battery was dead.

“Ready isn't a label,” Jianming said. “Ready is maintenance.”

“That's almost exactly what Gao told us.”

“Then Gao is a sensible man.”

The next case message came while Yanchuan was adding the battery's availability boundary to the model. Wanjiru said the operator had located more of the original maintenance package from Kenya. It contained an acknowledgment that an office had received the restoration question. It did not contain a signed physical check. The question of Yansheng's whereabouts remained separate.

He looked at the unused battery's record and felt a familiar pull. Could a missing sign-off in one country tell him anything about an auditor missing in another? Only that a process might have been incomplete. It could not carry a man through a doorway.

He called An instead of writing a theory. She was reviewing invoices and asked whether he had a direct task for her.

“Not yet. I just wanted to tell you the update.”

“Then tell me how your day was.”

He described the battery, the fan and the owner's disappointment. She asked who would pay to repair it.

“The owner, unless the warranty covers the part.”

“Then there's your problem. Someone thought owning a thing meant it would always be ready.”

She paused.

“I sound like your father.”

“You do.”

They both laughed.

A week later Feng received written confirmation of the replacement's delivery window. The owner wanted Guo to sign a financing option immediately, before the repair cost changed. Guo agreed only to a conditional proposal, with no representation that the unit was available for current dispatch.

Yanchuan added a separate line for readiness verification, to be completed by the authorized engineers after the part was fitted.

At the factory Gao read the case study and nodded.

“Good. Now don't use it to tell my crew they can cut their spare parts.”

“The point is the opposite.”

“Then make sure the salespeople know.”

The battery remained idle when Yanchuan left the yard on his second visit. Its operator had tested the monitoring equipment and confirmed that the enclosure's environmental conditions were being maintained. There was no heroic improvisation, no sudden profitable dispatch.

In the report he wrote that the asset was installed but not available for the proposed service. It was a narrower statement than the owner wanted. It was also one a reader could act on.

Outside the gate a mechanic was washing his hands at a standpipe. He asked whether they had finally fixed the fan.

“Not yet,” Feng said.

The mechanic shrugged. “Then don't tell anyone it's fixed.”

Yanchuan thought of the paper flag Yansheng had once attached to a toy missing its wheel: awaiting part. He had not understood then how much dignity there was in refusing to call unfinished work complete.
