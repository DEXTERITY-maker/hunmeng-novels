The proposal said every displaced worker would receive training.

The appendix said the training would take place outside paid hours.

Ning found the contradiction in a draft customer agreement, tucked between a schedule for equipment installation and a description of productivity gains. The factory intended to replace routine checking at two stations with assisted inspection. Workers would be offered a twelve-week course in exception handling and maintenance support. Completion was described as a pathway to new roles.

“Who pays their wages during the course?” she asked.

A human-resources manager said the workers would remain employed.

“Then why does the timetable put the sessions after the end of their shifts?”

“We can't remove them from production before the new equipment arrives.”

“So the people doing both jobs are meant to work twelve additional hours a week.”

The manager corrected the phrase. Participation was voluntary, she said, and the course was a benefit.

Ning asked what happened to a worker who did not volunteer. The staffing forecast assumed every position at the old stations would be removed once the equipment was accepted. The manager looked at the spreadsheet as if seeing it for the first time.

Guo joined the review remotely. He said Henglan was a vendor, not the employer, and had no right to dictate wage policy. Yanchuan agreed about the boundary. He disagreed that the boundary made the cost irrelevant to their proposal.

“If the business case counts fewer labor hours after commissioning,” he said, “it must also count the labor required to make the transition possible.”

The analyst had modeled licensing, installation and equipment depreciation. Retraining appeared as a small fixed allowance. It did not include wages, supervised practice, assessment time or the extra support required while new employees were learning.

They paused the proposal and asked the factory to define the employment arrangements before presenting the savings forecast.

Leela joined from Pune because the customer wanted to learn from Arjun's earlier inspection pilot. She brought Ravi into the conversation with his permission. Ravi described the difference between watching a training video and identifying a fault while a supervisor checked his work.

“If you're learning after a long shift,” he said, “you notice less. And if every mistake is treated as lost production, you stop asking questions.”

The factory's union representative asked whether the new tasks would carry new salary grades. Management had promised opportunities but had not specified whether the skills needed to supervise a machine would be paid differently from the repetitive work it replaced.

An opportunity that required more responsibility without different terms was, the representative said, an assignment.

The negotiation moved slowly. Management could not guarantee an identical job to every employee if production needs changed. The union could not promise that all workers would qualify for every technical role. Henglan could only state the training requirements and the limits of its system.

What emerged was less elegant than the original paragraph. Workers selected for the transition would train for designated hours as part of their paid schedules. Assessment would be based on demonstrated skills, with additional supervised practice where appropriate. Job descriptions, pay bands and appeal procedures would be agreed by the employer and worker representatives. Employees not placed in a new role would not be described in the technical report as successfully redeployed.

Guo asked for the revised total.

The analyst showed him the number. The project remained viable under a cautious demand forecast, but its claimed payback period had grown.

“We may lose the sale,” Guo said after the call.

“Then the earlier sale depended on somebody working for free,” Ning answered.

He did not reply immediately. He was looking at the revised cash flow. Two investors had recently asked Henglan to demonstrate that its projects could scale. A longer payback period was an inconvenient story.

By the end of the day he approved the revised proposal for discussion. He asked that the report identify the negotiation as incomplete rather than calling the new employment terms agreed.

At dinner Yanchuan told An about the twelve weeks.

“Who takes care of children during evening classes?” she asked.

“That was one of the worker representative's questions.”

“And transport?”

“That too.”

“And older employees who haven't taken an exam in twenty years?”

“They're asking for supported practice.”

An stirred a pot and turned down the heat. Her bookkeeping clients often considered training an expense that vanished once a software subscription began. She told him about an employee who had learned a new payroll system after hours and then become the person everyone phoned when the software failed.

“She got more responsibility and the same salary,” An said. “She left after six months. The replacement was expensive.”

Yanchuan wrote the example in his private notebook without the employee's name. It belonged to a broader argument about costs that accounting could recognize if someone bothered to count them.

At the next project meeting, an older worker asked whether refusing a transfer would be used as a reason to remove her from the training list. The manager said no and offered a written explanation of the available routes. The worker asked to take it home before deciding.

Ning had expected enthusiasm when the new schedule showed paid instruction. Instead she heard questions about qualifications, night transport, childcare and whether the new support role would disappear once the first year of warranty ended.

Those questions were not opposition to technology. They were questions about how a person could plan a life.

Henglan completed the revised technical model with two scenarios. The first assumed a rapid launch after training that had yet to be staffed and scheduled. The second used the negotiated timetable, paid practice and a slower ramp-up with supervised operation.

The second produced a lower return in the first year and a clearer estimate of the capacity the site could actually provide.

Chen reviewed the figures and asked what was missing.

“Employee choices,” Yanchuan said. “We don't know how many people will take the places offered, or whether the employer will accept the terms.”

“Then write that uncertainty beside the result.”

He did.

Weeks later the factory confirmed a first group of volunteers, each with a named supervisor and paid training allocation. The program began with fewer workers than management had hoped. Some declined and kept their current assignments while further discussions continued.

Ravi sent a short recording from Pune in which he explained a mechanical wear pattern to a new apprentice without checking the model's label first. He had become a teacher for ten minutes of the afternoon. Leela had arranged for those minutes to count as work.

Ning forwarded it to Guo with permission.

He replied that ten minutes did not look important in a financing model.

“Multiply it by every person who must learn,” she wrote back, “and then ask what happens if no one is paid to teach.”

For once he did not ask her to shorten the explanation.
