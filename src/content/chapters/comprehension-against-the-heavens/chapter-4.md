Mrs. Tang was pulling down the bakery's metal shutter when he came back.

She looked him over—the dried blood under his nose, the rust from the fire escape on his palms, the knees of his jeans gray with dust—and her mouth opened for a lecture. Then she closed it again.

"You smell like a fire escape," she said finally.

"I was sitting on one."

"Why?"

Ye Xuan thought about it. *Because I jumped onto it from the street after copying a superpower with a breathing exercise.*

"The view," he said.

Mrs. Tang made a sound that meant she had raised children and grandchildren and knew exactly how much a young man's explanations were worth. She shoved a paper bag into his hands—two pork buns, still warm—and went back to the shutter.

"Friday," she said, without turning around.

"Friday," he agreed.

* * *

Upstairs, he locked the door, ate both pork buns in under a minute, and sat cross-legged on the bed.

"All right," he said to the rabbit-shaped water stain on the ceiling. "Let's do this properly."

He closed his eyes and breathed the way the old women had in the park—slowly, from the belly, letting the shoulders drop. Then he opened his other sight.

The room was almost empty.

That was the first surprise. In the park, the threads of qi had drifted through the air like pollen. Here, between four walls and a closed window, there were only a few—thin, pale, lazy, hanging near the ceiling like dust that had forgotten how to fall. He breathed in, and one drifted toward him. He breathed in again. Nothing. And again. Nothing.

After a hundred breaths, he had gathered four threads. They sank below his navel, settled there for a few seconds like tiny warm coins, and then—slowly—began to fade.

Ye Xuan opened his eyes.

"It's leaking," he said.

He tried again, paying closer attention. This time he followed the threads all the way down with his sight. There, below the navel, was the place the old women had filled without knowing it: a small hollow, round and dim, like a jar. But the jar had no lid. Or rather—it had something worse than no lid. The walls themselves were porous, like unglazed clay. Whatever went in seeped out through the sides.

*The dantian,* he thought. *In every novel, the first step is opening the dantian. I always skipped those chapters. The hero sits down, breathes for three pages, and boom, first layer.*

He was now deeply regretting all the chapters he had skipped.

He got up, found a pen and a notebook among the old Ye Xuan's things, and opened it to a clean page. The previous owner's handwriting filled the first half—neat engineering notes, formulas, a doodle of a cat. Ye Xuan turned past them carefully, as if past someone else's letters.

Then he wrote, in Chinese:

*RULES.*

*1. Do not look at the stone.*

*2. Do not stare at federal agents.*

*3. Understand this ability before it kills me.*

*4. Food.*

He looked at the list, tapped the pen against his teeth, and added:

*5. Never use it where cameras can see.*

*6. Gather qi every day. No excuses.*

*7. Nobody learns about the stone.*

Seven rules. It felt better already. In his old life, when the gaokao had seemed like a mountain he would never climb, he had broken it into rules and schedules and small, boring steps. Mountains were climbed one step at a time. So were heavens, probably.

"Now," he murmured. "Method."

* * *

He did not have a method. He had two memories.

The first was the old women in the park—their slow, rounded movements, the long breath in through the nose, the pause, the long breath out. Their bodies drew threads in by accident, the way a slow river picks up leaves.

The second was Juno on the rooftop: one, two, three, *breathe*, four, *release*. A rhythm built not to gather but to compress—to squeeze energy into a single moment and spend it all at once.

He sat on the bed, closed his eyes, and placed the two memories side by side.

Then he let his comprehension look at them.

It was different from looking at a phone or an engine. Those were in front of him, solid. This was inside his head. But the shift came anyway—gentler, like a page turning. He saw the grannies' breathing as a shape: wide, slow, open, a net cast loosely into water. He saw Juno's rhythm as another shape: tight, sharp, a fist closing.

And he saw what neither of them had noticed.

The net caught threads but couldn't hold them. The fist could hold, but only for a heartbeat before it let go.

*What if the net closes like a fist?*

Breathe in slowly for three counts, wide and open, drawing the threads close. On the fourth count, don't release—*fold*. Close the breath around the threads and press them down, the way you press the air out of a bag before you seal it.

He tried it.

One, two, three—a thread drifted toward him. Four—he folded the breath and pushed it down, into the jar below his navel.

It settled. And this time it stayed a little longer before it began to fade.

He did it again. And again. One thread every ten breaths or so, in this thin, closed room. It was slow. It was so slow that a fly crossing the ceiling seemed to be in a hurry by comparison.

But it worked.

Ye Xuan sat there for two hours, counting breaths, folding threads into a leaking jar, and feeling the faint warmth grow, fade, grow again. By midnight, his legs were numb and his head felt as if it had been washed with cold water. The warmth below his navel was the size of a fingertip.

He could feel, too, that it wasn't enough. The jar's walls had to change. A trickle would never do it. In the novels, the dantian opened with a flood—a breakthrough, a pill, a hidden treasure, a lucky fall off a cliff into a cave of spirit stones.

"I'm not falling off any cliffs," he told the ceiling.

The rabbit stain said nothing.

* * *

He got up to open the window for more air, and that was when he saw the car.

It was parked across the street, under the sign of a closed herbal shop: a black sedan, clean, the kind of car that didn't belong on Mott Street at midnight between the delivery vans and the fish trucks. Its engine was off. Its windows were dark.

But in the driver's seat, very briefly, a small orange point glowed and faded. A cigarette.

Ye Xuan stepped back from the window, out of the light.

*Bureau?* Kroll had said he wasn't under arrest. But the Bureau was exactly the kind of organization that parked black sedans outside people's homes.

*Or whoever killed Crane.*

He thought of the stone in the sock in the jacket on the hook behind the door. He thought of Kroll's voice: *much less polite.*

Slowly, he drew the curtain closed.

Then, because he had nothing else he could do about it, he sat back down on the bed and kept breathing. One, two, three, fold. One, two, three, fold. If someone was watching, he could at least be watching something boring.

Somewhere around two in the morning, he fell asleep sitting up.

* * *

He woke at five to the sound of trays.

Downstairs, the bakery was already alive. Bakers' hours. The smell of yeast and hot sugar crept up through the floorboards like a promise. He peeked through the curtain. The street was gray and wet with predawn mist. The black sedan was gone.

He washed his face, pulled on the black windbreaker, and went down.

Mrs. Tang was shaping buns at a long steel table, her arms floured to the elbows. A radio murmured Cantonese news beside her. She glanced at him, then at the clock, then back at him.

"Up early," she said. "Are you sick again?"

"I'm going to the park."

That made her stop. "The park."

"Columbus Park. The old ladies who do tai chi in the morning—what time do they start?"

Mrs. Tang looked at him for a long moment, as if he had announced that he planned to enter a cage of tigers wearing a suit made of pork.

Then she ladled a bowl of rice congee from a pot on the back stove, dropped some pickled vegetables and a salted egg on top, and pushed it across the table.

"Eat first," she said. "They start at six. Granny Liu's group is by the big tree." She went back to her buns and added, almost kindly, "They will eat you alive."

The congee was plain and hot and perfect. He finished every grain.

* * *

Columbus Park at ten to six was a different world from the evening before.

The chess players were gone. Mist hung between the trees. The concrete was dark with dew, and the city around the park was only just beginning to grind into motion—a garbage truck somewhere, a single siren far away.

And the air.

Ye Xuan stopped at the entrance and simply looked.

The threads were *everywhere*. Thicker than they had been last night, thicker than he had imagined—drifting down through the mist like slow, glowing snow. They gathered most around the great old tree at the far end of the park, an ancient locust with a trunk so wide that two people couldn't have joined hands around it. Around that tree, the threads moved in a slow spiral, like water circling a drain.

*Dawn,* he thought. *Of course. The old masters always meditated at dawn.*

Under the tree, a dozen elderly women in loose cotton clothes and sensible shoes were warming up, rolling their shoulders and arguing about something in three different dialects at once.

As Ye Xuan approached, the arguing stopped.

Twelve gray heads turned toward him in perfect unison. Twelve pairs of sharp eyes looked him up and down—his sneakers, his windbreaker, his hair, which was doing its usual thing of pointing in several directions at once.

There was a short, terrible silence.

Then a small round woman at the front, with a red tracksuit and the expression of a judge at a cooking competition, said in Cantonese:

"Fresh meat."
