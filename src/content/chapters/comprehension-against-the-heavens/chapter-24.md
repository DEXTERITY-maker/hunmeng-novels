The email came on a Tuesday, between a delivery to a dentist on Park Avenue and a delivery to a law firm on Lexington.

He read it standing in an elevator.

**From:** Office of the Bursar, Hudson Polytechnic Institute
**Subject:** URGENT: Outstanding Balance — Action Required

*Dear Xuan Ye,*

*Our records indicate that your account has an outstanding balance of $9,400.00 for the Fall semester. This balance results from the cancellation of your Dean's Merit Award, effective October 1, due to failure to meet the award's attendance and examination requirements.*

*Payment in full is due within 30 days of this notice. If payment is not received by the deadline, your enrollment for the current semester will be canceled.*

*Please note: as an international student on an F-1 visa, cancellation of enrollment will result in termination of your SEVIS record. You would be required to depart the United States within 15 days.*

*If you have questions, please contact our office. We are here to help!*

*Have a great day!*

The elevator doors opened on the thirty-second floor. Ye Xuan stood in the elevator and didn't move until they closed again.

* * *

He read it four more times during the day. On the green wave down Lexington. In a lobby on Fifty-Seventh Street. On a bench in Bryant Park, where he ate a sandwich without tasting it.

*Have a great day!*

He understood it perfectly. That was the worst part. In his first life, he had been the kind of student who read the fine print. He knew what a merit scholarship was. He knew what *failure to meet attendance and examination requirements* meant.

The other Ye Xuan had stopped going to class sometime around the beginning of September. Ye Xuan had found it in the laptop's calendar: classes, labs, a midterm exam in Materials Science on September 28, all marked and none of them attended. The boy had been spending his nights soldering copper lattices for Silas Crane. Then the pier. Then the fever.

And then Ye Xuan himself had arrived, and spent a month running packages around Manhattan and breathing in parks and fighting Rocco Vitale, and he had not gone to a single class either.

He had not even thought about it.

*Stupid,* he told himself. *Stupid, stupid, stupid.* In the novels, the hero never had to go to class. The hero's school was a sect on a mountain, and the hero skipped all the lessons because he was too busy getting stronger, and nobody ever sent him an email about it.

Nine thousand four hundred dollars. Thirty days.

Or fifteen days after that, a plane back to China.

* * *

He thought about China all afternoon.

It wouldn't be the end of the world. Not really. He'd go back to Hangzhou. To his mother and father—*this* world's mother and father, who were exactly his mother and father, who would meet him at the airport and cry and scold him and take him home to the apartment with the squeaking balcony door. He would eat braised pork. He would sleep in his old room.

He would never find out what happened at the pier.

He would leave Mrs. Tang with an empty room above her bakery. He would leave Juno with her fraying legs and Tank with his stiff back, and they would go back to burning out by twenty-five, because no one else in this world could see what he saw.

And the stone—

He touched his jacket. The tin was there, warm.

If he flew home, the stone would come with him. Across the Pacific. Through airports full of scanners and sniffer dogs and, for all he knew, Halcyon crystals in glass columns. And if someone was listening for it—

*They're not going to stop,* Shah had said.

He would be bringing it home. To his parents.

He put the sandwich down. He wasn't hungry anymore.

* * *

"Nine thousand *four hundred*?"

Big Lou took the cigar out of his mouth and stared at him across the desk in the glass office.

"Nine thousand four hundred," Ye Xuan repeated. "In thirty days."

"Kid, I like you. You're fast. You're the best ground runner I've had in years." Big Lou spread his scarred hands. "But I pay twelve bucks a delivery. You do thirty-five, forty deliveries a week. With tips, what, eight hundred? Nine on a good week?" He shook his head. "In a month, after rent, after you eat, maybe you've got two thousand. Maybe."

"I know."

"What about your folks? Back in China?"

Ye Xuan thought of his mother at the bus stop in her brown wool coat, the one she said was too expensive. His father's cardigan with the coffee stain. The apartment in the compound. They were a schoolteacher and an engineer at a water plant. They had already paid the first installment of tuition; he had seen the transfer in the bank records, and he knew exactly what that much money meant to a family like theirs. They had probably borrowed some of it.

*Cheap coats are expensive in the end.*

"No," he said. "Not my folks."

Big Lou chewed his unlit cigar for a while.

"I can give you more deliveries," he said finally. "Longer days. Weekends. Uptown runs, Brooklyn runs. It's not going to get you to nine grand. But it's something."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. You're gonna hate me by Friday." Lou stuck the cigar back in his mouth and turned to his invoices. "Now get out of my office. I got a goldfish going to Tribeca."

* * *

He came out of the office and found Tank and Juno sitting on the duct-taped couch, both of them trying very hard to look like they hadn't been listening through the glass.

Wren, at her desk, didn't bother pretending. She had her headphones around her neck.

"Nine thousand four hundred," she said. "That's a lot of goldfish."

"Thanks, Wren."

"Just saying." She tilted her head. "Your heartbeat's really fast. The normal one."

He sat down on the couch next to Juno. She shifted over to make room. He put his face in his hands.

"What happens if you don't pay?" Juno asked quietly.

"They cancel my enrollment. Then my visa. Then I have fifteen days to leave the country."

Nobody said anything. He felt Juno's shoulder against his, warm and solid. On his other side, the couch sagged as Tank leaned forward.

"Back to China?" Tank said.

"Back to China."

"But you just got here," Tank said, as if this were a reasonable argument against immigration law.

Ye Xuan almost laughed. He didn't quite manage it.

"I'll figure it out," he said. "More deliveries. Weekends. I'll figure something out."

"Two thousand," Wren said from her desk. "That's what you'll figure out. Lou's right. I did the math. Even if you work every day and don't sleep and somebody tips you a hundred bucks every afternoon, you get to maybe three thousand. Not nine."

"*Wren*," said Juno.

"What? He's an engineer. He likes numbers."

Ye Xuan did like numbers. These ones he did not like at all.

* * *

Juno walked out with him at six. They stood at the top of the ramp in the cooling evening, the flickering Lucky Suds sign above them, the cat asleep in the window.

"Dawn tomorrow?" she said.

"Dawn tomorrow."

She nodded. She didn't leave.

"For what it's worth," she said, looking at the street, not at him. "My legs. This morning, I ran from Harlem to the Battery. Fifteen jumps. Big ones. And tonight they don't hurt." She shrugged one shoulder. "First time in six months I ran a whole day without them hurting at night."

He looked at her.

"So whatever happens," Juno said. "Whatever you have to do. Don't go back to China."

She turned and ran up the side of the laundromat building—one, two, three, *four*—and was gone over the roof before he could say anything.

He stood at the top of the ramp for a long time.

Then he heard Tank's heavy footsteps coming up behind him, and turned.

Tank was standing at the bottom of the ramp, his huge hands in the pocket of his hoodie, his broad face troubled. He looked up at the roof where Juno had disappeared. Then back at Ye Xuan.

"I know a way," Tank said quietly. "To make nine thousand dollars in thirty days." He hesitated. "But Juno is going to be really mad at me for telling you."
