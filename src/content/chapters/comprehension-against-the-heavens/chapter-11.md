They came on Monday at half past seven in the morning, which was smart.

At half past seven, the Golden Lotus was full of the smell of the first batch of pineapple buns and empty of customers. The commuters came at eight. The grannies came after tai chi, around half past eight. At half past seven, there was only Mrs. Tang, the radio, and Tommy at the window table, eating congee and arguing with his tablet about whether Zenith Prime could beat a tidal wave.

And Ye Xuan, who had just come back from Columbus Park, damp with dew and smelling—Granny Wu had sniffed him suspiciously and confirmed this—like a normal human being.

The bell over the door jingled.

Rocco Vitale came in first. In daylight, he was even bigger than he had looked through the window on Friday: thick through the chest and neck, the gold chain resting on a black T-shirt, the shaved head shining. His hands were what you noticed, though. They were too big for the rest of him, even for someone his size—wide, heavy, with knuckles like walnuts.

Two more came in behind him. One was skinny, with a scar through one eyebrow and a toothpick in his mouth. The other was a short, round young man who couldn't stop looking at the egg tarts.

Mrs. Tang didn't stop shaping buns.

"We're not open," she said in English.

"Door was open." Rocco smiled. He had very white, very even teeth. "Morning, Mrs. Tang. You know why we're here. It's the first of the month. Five hundred."

"I told you in May. I told you in June. I told you every month." Mrs. Tang set a bun on the tray, perfectly round. "No."

"See, that's the thing." Rocco leaned on the glass display counter, which creaked under his weight. "In May, you said no, and nothing happened, 'cause we're nice guys. In June, you said no, and your delivery van got four flat tires. July, your back window. August, somebody put sugar in your generator." He spread his huge hands. "This neighborhood's dangerous, Mrs. Tang. Lot of crazy stuff happening since the Glow. You need insurance. Everybody else on the street pays."

"Then everybody else on the street is stupid."

The skinny one with the toothpick laughed. Rocco didn't.

He reached out and placed one hand on the stainless steel frame of the display counter—the thick steel edge that held the glass, the part that had probably been bolted to that floor since before Ye Xuan was born in either world.

He squeezed.

The steel groaned. Then it *folded*, crumpling under his fingers like a soda can, with a shriek of metal that made Tommy drop his spoon. The glass above it cracked from one side to the other in a long, jagged line.

"Oops," Rocco said.

The round one picked up a heavy metal napkin holder from the nearest table, weighed it, and threw it at the display case. The cracked glass exploded. Egg tarts tumbled to the floor in a shower of glittering shards.

Tommy screamed.

* * *

Ye Xuan was moving before he had decided to move.

It was the rain in Hangzhou again. The headlight. His body going first.

He stepped between Mrs. Tang and Rocco, his back to the old woman, glass crunching under his sneakers.

"She said no," he said.

Rocco looked at him for the first time. He looked down—he had to look down, Ye Xuan only came up to his chin—and his face went through surprise, then amusement, then something uglier.

"Who the hell are you?"

"I live upstairs."

"The ghost," said the skinny one, grinning around his toothpick. "That's the ghost, Rocco. The guy who doesn't pay rent. Hey, ghost, maybe you should pay *our* rent—"

Rocco's hand came down on Ye Xuan's shoulder.

It was like being caught in a hydraulic press. Pain shot down his arm, white and enormous, and his knees nearly buckled. He felt the bones of his shoulder grind together. Another two seconds of that pressure and something would break.

He looked.

He didn't decide to do it; his sight opened by itself, the way it had with the phone, with Kroll. And Rocco Vitale became lines.

The gift lived in his hands and forearms: thick threads of dull iron-gray light wrapped around the bones, layer on layer, like steel cable wound around a spool. It was crude. Ugly, even. Kroll's chains had been dense and ordered, every link in its place. Rocco's threads were tangled and lumpy, as if they had been wound in a hurry by someone who had never seen a spool before. But they were strong. They squeezed with the force of a machine.

And they all came from one place.

Inside the bend of his right elbow, where the forearm met the upper arm, every iron-gray thread passed through a single narrow knot—like a garden hose pinched where it went around a corner. The whole gift flowed through that knot. Upstream of it, in the upper arm, there was nothing; the gift ended there, as if the Glow had only reached that far.

*A choke point,* Ye Xuan thought, with the strange calm clarity that always came with the sight. *Everything goes through there. If something blocks the knot—*

He didn't have time for a plan. He had one thread of qi in his dantian, maybe two, left over from the morning in the park.

He brought his left hand up, inside Rocco's arm, and drove his thumb into the bend of the elbow, right at the knot. At the same moment, he pushed a thread of qi down his arm and through his thumb, the way he had pushed it into the copper wire—

—and *into* the knot.

It was like dropping a pebble into gears.

The iron-gray threads stuttered. For one second, the whole tangled cable of the gift seized up, as if it had forgotten what it was supposed to do. The crushing pressure on his shoulder vanished. Rocco's hand opened by itself, fingers splaying, suddenly just a large, ordinary human hand.

Rocco's eyes went wide.

Ye Xuan didn't waste the second. He grabbed Rocco's wrist with his right hand, twisted it outward, and stepped in—a joint lock he had learned from a cheap self-defense video in his first life, back when he thought he might have to fight off pickpockets on the Hangzhou metro. Without his gift, Rocco's arm was just an arm. A big, strong arm, but an arm, with an elbow that bent only one way.

Ye Xuan bent it the other way. Not far. Just enough.

Rocco Vitale went down on his knees in the broken glass with a grunt of pure astonishment.

* * *

The bakery went silent.

The skinny one's toothpick fell out of his mouth. The round one froze with his hand halfway to another napkin holder. Behind the counter, Mrs. Tang stood absolutely still, a ball of bun dough in her hands. At the window table, Tommy's mouth hung open so wide that Ye Xuan could see the congee.

Ye Xuan held the lock. His shoulder was screaming. His heart was beating so hard he could feel it in his teeth. A warm trickle had started in his left nostril.

He made his voice calm. He had no idea how.

"If anyone breaks anything else," he said, "I'll break his elbow."

It was a bluff. He had no idea if he could break an elbow. He had never broken anything in his life except a vase in his grandmother's house when he was six.

But Rocco didn't know that. Rocco was kneeling in glass, trying to make his fingers close, and they wouldn't. Ye Xuan could see it with his sight: the knot was still jammed, the threads still tangled and stuck. It would wear off—he could see that too, the qi he had pushed in already dissolving like sugar in water—but not for a few seconds more.

"Rocco?" said the skinny one uncertainly.

"Back off," Rocco said through his teeth. "Back off, Joey. Both of you. Get out."

They backed off. Glass crunched. The bell over the door jingled as they stumbled out onto the sidewalk.

Ye Xuan let go.

Rocco stood up slowly. He was flexing his right hand, staring at it, opening and closing it. Ye Xuan watched the threads in his forearm loosen, untangle, begin to squeeze again. The gift was coming back.

For a second, he thought Rocco would try again.

Instead, the big man looked at him. Really looked—not down this time, but straight into his face, as if memorizing it.

"What did you do to me?" he said quietly.

"Nothing," Ye Xuan said. "I'm an engineering student."

Rocco's lips pulled back from his white, even teeth. He leaned in close.

"You're dead, bakery boy," he said, almost gently. "You know that, right? You're already dead. You just don't know it yet."

He walked out. The door swung shut. Through the window, Ye Xuan watched the three of them climb into a dented gray car and drive off down Mott Street, faster than they should have.

* * *

Then Tommy was in front of him, grabbing his sleeve with both hands, glasses crooked, eyes enormous.

"You're Awakened!" he shouted. "You're *Awakened!* You did a—a thing—you touched his arm and he just *fell*—"

"I'm not Awakened."

"You *are!* What's your gift? Is it touch? Is it like a power-off touch? That's so cool, that's a class five at *least*—"

"It's called a joint lock," Ye Xuan said. "Anyone can learn it. It's on the internet." He wiped his nose with the back of his hand, and it came away red. "I'm an engineering student, Tommy."

"Engineering students can't do that!"

"They can if they watch enough videos."

Tommy looked at him with deep betrayal, as if he knew he was being lied to and couldn't prove it.

Behind the counter, Mrs. Tang set down the ball of dough she had been holding the whole time. She looked at the glass on the floor. At the crushed steel frame of her display case. At the egg tarts lying in the shards. At Ye Xuan's bloody nose.

She didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she wrapped two unbroken egg tarts in a napkin and held them out.

"Go to work," she said. "You'll be late."

* * *

By noon, the whole of Chinatown knew.

He didn't know how. He was on the other side of Manhattan, delivering architectural drawings to a firm on West Twenty-Third Street, when Wren texted him: *heard you beat up rocco vitale with one finger lol*. At one o'clock, a man at a fruit stand on Mott Street, whom he had never met, gave him a free bag of lychees. At three, a text arrived from a number he didn't know: *This is Granny Wu. My granddaughter is single, 22, pharmacist. Sunday dinner?*

He didn't answer that one.

But in the quiet moments between deliveries—in elevators, on the green wave down Sixth Avenue—his mind kept going back to the bakery. Not to the fight. To the knot.

Rocco's gift had been strong. Stronger than steel. And it had all passed through one narrow point, and one thread of qi in the right place had jammed the whole thing like a stick in a wheel.

*Every gift is a pattern,* he thought. *Kroll's chains. Juno's springs. Rocco's cables. And every pattern has a structure. Places where it's strong. Places where it's weak.*

*If I can see the weak point…*

He looked down at his right hand, holding a cardboard tube of drawings. It was trembling slightly.

Not from fear, he realized. From something closer to excitement.
