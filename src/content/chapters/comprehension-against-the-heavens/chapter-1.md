The last thing Ye Xuan saw in his first life was the headlight.

It came out of the rain like a second moon—low, white, and much too fast. The truck behind it was only a shape, a wall of wet steel that hadn't noticed the red light, or the little boy in the yellow raincoat who had stepped off the curb to chase a rolling ball.

Ye Xuan was eighteen. He had finished the gaokao eleven days ago. He had plans: sleep for a week, eat his mother's braised pork, and finally finish the web novel he'd been reading since middle school. Four thousand chapters, and the hero still hadn't fought the final boss.

He didn't think about any of that.

His body moved first. His umbrella went one way, and his hands went the other. He caught the boy by the hood and threw him backward, toward the curb, toward the screaming mother.

Then the headlight filled everything.

There was no pain. Only a sound like a bell struck once, very deep, somewhere beneath the world—and a calm, strange thought that didn't feel like his own.

*So this is where the road ends.*

*No,* something answered. *This is where it begins.*

* * *

He woke up to the smell of fried dough.

It was so ordinary, so specific—hot oil, sugar, a little burnt sesame—that for a moment Ye Xuan simply lay there and breathed it in. Somewhere below him, a radio was playing an old Cantonese love song. A truck reversed in the street, beeping. Someone shouted in English, and someone else shouted back in Mandarin with a thick Fujian accent.

Ceiling. Cracked plaster. A water stain shaped like a rabbit. A ceiling fan turning slowly, ticking on every third rotation.

*Hospital?* he thought.

No hospital in the world smelled like youtiao.

He sat up too fast, and the room tilted. It was small: a single bed, a desk under the window, a chair buried under clothes, a mini-fridge humming in the corner. Textbooks were stacked against the wall. *Electromagnetic Fields and Waves. Introduction to Materials Engineering. Calculus III.* All in English.

Ye Xuan looked at his hands.

They were his hands. Long fingers, the small white scar on the left thumb from a kitchen knife when he was nine. But they were thinner. The nails were bitten short, and he had never bitten his nails in his life.

There was a mirror on the closet door. He got up on unsteady legs and stood in front of it.

The face was his. Almost.

The same eyes. The same stubborn hair that never lay flat. But the cheeks were hollower, as if this version of him had skipped a lot of meals, and dark shadows lay under the eyes. Along the left side of his jaw ran a faint line—a healed cut he didn't remember getting.

He stared at his reflection for a long time.

Then he said, very quietly, "No way."

He had read this story before. Hundreds of times, in hundreds of versions. A boy dies—a truck is usually involved. The boy wakes up somewhere else, in a body that is his and not his, and the world is bigger and stranger than it has any right to be.

*Transmigration,* he thought, and a short, slightly hysterical laugh escaped him. *I got hit by a truck and transmigrated. Of course I did. Of all the clichés in the world.*

He sat down on the bed before his knees could make the decision for him.

"Okay," he told the empty room. "Okay. Calm down. First question: where am I?"

* * *

The window answered part of it.

Fire escapes zigzagged down the brick building across the street. Below, the sidewalk was crowded with fruit stands—lychees, dragon fruit, bitter melon—and red signboards with Chinese characters hung above narrow shops. But the street sign on the corner said MOTT ST in white letters on green, and the yellow cars crawling past were unmistakably New York taxis.

Chinatown. Manhattan.

Ye Xuan had been to New York exactly zero times. He had seen it in a thousand movies.

On the desk lay a phone with a spiderweb crack across the screen, a laptop with a dead battery, a stack of envelopes, and a folded newspaper. Next to the newspaper, a student ID:

**HUDSON POLYTECHNIC INSTITUTE · YE, XUAN · School of Engineering · International Student**

The photo showed the face from the mirror, maybe a year younger, before the shadows.

"Same name," he murmured. "Same face. Different life."

So he hadn't arrived in an empty shell. He had stepped into someone else's life—someone who had lived here, studied here, bitten his nails here. And that someone was gone.

Ye Xuan waited for the memories to flood in. In the novels, they always did: a splitting headache, and then a tidal wave of the previous owner's life.

Nothing came. Only a dull ache behind his eyes and a few scattered images, like photographs left out in the rain. A lecture hall. A woman laughing. A pier at night—black water, the smell of salt, and something burning.

Then nothing.

"Great," he said. "No memory package. Hard mode."

He picked up the phone.

* * *

That was when it happened for the first time.

The screen was locked: fingerprint or a six-digit code. He pressed his thumb to the sensor—the same thumb, the same scar—and the phone buzzed. *Try again.*

So the fingerprints weren't identical. Interesting. He frowned at the cracked glass, turning the phone in his hand, and something in his vision *shifted*.

It was like staring at one of those magic-eye pictures until the hidden image suddenly jumps out at you. The phone was still a phone. But beneath its surface, he saw lines.

Thin threads of pale light ran through it, branching and folding. The battery was a dense knot of slow, patient energy. Copper paths spread out like the veins of a leaf. The processor was a tiny city, with millions of gates opening and closing in perfect order. He saw how the current flowed and why. He saw how the screen knew where a finger touched it. And he saw—not as words or numbers, but as plain, obvious *understanding*—how the lock worked, and why the sensor didn't recognize him: a scratch across its surface, about two weeks old, distorting every print.

It felt like the most natural thing in the world. Like remembering how to breathe.

The lines faded. Ye Xuan blinked.

Something warm ran over his lip. He touched it. Blood.

The headache arrived a second later, like a hammer wrapped in cotton, and he had to grab the edge of the desk.

"What," he whispered, "was that?"

But he already knew how to open the phone. Not the code—he didn't know the code. The sensor. If he pressed his thumb at an angle and slid it away from the scratch…

He tried. The phone unlocked.

Ye Xuan sat very still, blood dripping onto a borrowed T-shirt, his heart hammering against his ribs.

In his old life, he had been good at school because he worked like a dog. Formulas never simply *came* to him; he had to grind them into his head, page after page, night after night.

This was different. He had looked at the phone, and the phone had explained itself to him.

*No,* he thought. *It didn't explain anything. I understood it. Completely.*

A phrase floated up from the back of his mind—from that strange moment in the dark, when the bell rang beneath the world.

*Comprehension against the heavens.*

* * *

He cleaned his face with a tissue and decided, for the sake of his sanity, to deal with one impossible thing at a time.

The newspaper first.

It was yesterday's *New York Ledger*. He unfolded it on the bed, and the front page stared back at him.

A photograph filled the top half: the Brooklyn Bridge at sunset, and above it—*above* it, maybe fifty meters over the cables—a man in a dark jacket, hanging in the air like a hawk riding the wind. No wires. No helicopter. The photographer had caught the moment the man turned his head, and his eyes were glowing a faint white.

The headline ran across the page in heavy black letters:

**TWELVE YEARS AFTER THE GLOW, SENATE SET TO VOTE ON AWAKENED REGISTRATION ACT**

Below it, in smaller type: *Bureau of Awakened Affairs reports 3,412 registered Awakened in New York State. "The real number is higher," admits Bureau director.*

Ye Xuan read the article twice. Then a third time, slower, his finger moving under the lines the way it had when he first learned English in elementary school.

Twelve years ago, on a summer night, the sky over the entire planet had burned white for seven minutes. Scientists called it the Global Luminous Event. Everyone else called it the Glow.

Afterward, people began to change. Not many—one in a hundred thousand, maybe fewer. Some could lift cars. Some could start fires with a thought, or walk through walls, or apparently fly over the Brooklyn Bridge at sunset. Nobody knew why it happened to them and not to others. Nobody knew what the Glow had been.

People called them the Awakened.

Ye Xuan lowered the paper.

"So it isn't just transmigration," he said. "It's transmigration into a world with superpowers."

The fan ticked overhead. Downstairs, the radio switched to an ad for a dental clinic on Canal Street.

He looked at his hands again—at the faint brown smear of blood on his thumb—and thought about the lines inside the phone. The threads of pale light. The feeling of the world opening like a book.

In the novels he used to read, there was a word for that kind of light. The old masters spent their whole lives meditating in mountain caves, hoping to glimpse it just once.

*The Dao.*

"No," he said aloud. "No, no. Calm down, Ye Xuan. You've known about any of this for five minutes. You don't know anything yet."

His heart wouldn't stop racing.

* * *

Someone knocked on the door.

Three sharp knocks, a pause, and then a voice through the wood—an older woman, speaking Mandarin with a heavy Fuzhou accent and the patience of someone who had already lost it.

"Xiao Ye! I know you're in there. I heard you talking to yourself."

Ye Xuan froze.

"It's Mrs. Tang," the voice went on. "Your rent is two weeks late. Two weeks! I'm not a charity, I'm a bakery."

He opened his mouth. Then closed it.

Mrs. Tang lowered her voice. When she spoke again, the anger was gone, and something else had replaced it—something that sounded uncomfortably like worry.

"And Xiao Ye… there are two men downstairs in gray suits. Government men. They're asking about you." A pause. "They want to know where you were three nights ago. At the pier."

Ye Xuan looked at the newspaper. At the flying man with the glowing eyes. At the student ID with the face that was his and not his.

*One missing night at the pier,* he thought. *Of course. The previous owner always leaves a mess.*

He wiped the last of the blood from his lip, stood up, and walked to the door.
