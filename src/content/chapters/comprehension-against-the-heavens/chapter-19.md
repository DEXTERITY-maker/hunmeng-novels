"Try what?" Juno said warily.

"I don't know exactly." It was the honest answer. "Something I haven't done before. It might not work. It might not do anything. But I don't think it'll hurt."

"You don't *think*."

"I'm fairly sure."

Juno looked at him for a long moment. Then she sat back down on the coffee table, stuck out her left leg, and pulled the leg of her jeans up to the knee.

"If it hurts," she said, "I kick you."

"That seems fair."

He knelt on the concrete floor in front of her and looked.

* * *

Up close, the damage was worse.

He let his sight sink into her left calf, slowly, carefully, ignoring the familiar pressure building behind his eyes. The copper coils filled his vision—hundreds of them, wound around and through the muscle and bone in a spiral pattern so intricate it made his head swim. Most of them were bright and tight. But here, just below the back of her knee, a cluster of them had gone dark and loose, and one strand in the middle had come completely unwound. It hung free, curled and kinked, sparking faintly every time it brushed against its neighbors.

*That's the pain,* he thought. *Every time it touches the others, it sparks. Every spark burns.*

He thought of the copper wire around the mint tin. Of how he had wound it, unwound it, wound it again, three hours of patient work, until the pattern was right.

*It's just wire,* he told himself. *Copper wire made of light. You know how to wind wire.*

He reached into his dantian. After the fight, there wasn't much left—a faint warmth, a single thread, maybe two. He took one. Led it up through his chest, down his arm, into the tip of his right index finger.

He placed the fingertip very gently against the back of Juno's calf, just below the knee.

She flinched. "Your hand is hot."

"Sorry. Hold still."

He pushed the thread of qi into her leg.

It wasn't like pushing it into copper. Copper was dead; it took whatever you gave it. This was alive, and full of its own energy, and for a moment the thread of qi just sat there in the middle of her gift like a drop of oil on water, not mixing, not doing anything.

Then he guided it.

Carefully. Delicately. The way you'd thread a needle with shaking hands. He moved the thread of qi to the loose copper strand, and wrapped it around the strand like a sleeve, and—slowly, so slowly—began to wind the strand back into its place in the coil.

It resisted. It had been loose for months; it had grown used to being loose. It kinked and sparked. Juno hissed through her teeth, and her leg jerked under his hand.

"Almost," he said. Sweat was running down his face. A warm drop fell from his nose onto the concrete. "Almost—"

The strand slid into place.

He felt it click—not like the dantian opening, but like a small bone settling into its socket. The loose strand lay against its neighbors, wound tight in the spiral where it belonged. And the thread of qi he had wrapped around it didn't dissolve. It stayed there, soaking into the copper, filling in the dark worn places on its surface, so that for a moment the strand glowed brighter than the coils around it.

The sparking stopped.

Ye Xuan pulled his hand back and sat down hard on the concrete.

* * *

Juno didn't say anything.

She was staring at her leg. She flexed her foot, slowly. Pointed it. Flexed it again. She put her hand on the back of her calf, just below the knee, and pressed.

Her face changed.

"It doesn't hurt," she said.

"Just that one spot," he said, wiping his nose. "There's a lot more. That was one strand. There are dozens like it. In both legs. I can't—I don't have enough to do more tonight."

"It doesn't *hurt*." She didn't seem to have heard him. She was pressing her calf in different places, as if testing a bruise that had been there so long she had forgotten what it felt like without it. "That spot—it's always there. Always. Even in the morning. It's been there for six months and it's—"

She stopped. She pressed her lips together hard.

Ye Xuan looked away, to give her a moment. He looked at Wren's empty chair, at the maps on the wall, at the cat-shaped water stain on the ceiling that was nothing like his rabbit.

When he looked back, Juno was wiping her eyes with the sleeve of her red jacket, angrily, as if her eyes had betrayed her.

"How?" she said. Her voice was rough. "How did you do that?"

* * *

He tried to explain.

"Your gift runs on energy," he said. "Every time you use it, the coils pull energy from somewhere and spend it. But they don't take it from outside. They take it from you. From your muscles, your bones, your body. That's what burnout is. The gift keeps spending, and your body keeps paying, and nobody ever pays back."

"Pays back with what?"

"With the same thing I just used." He held up his hand. The fingertip was still faintly warm. "There's energy in the air. Since the Glow. It's everywhere—in the city, in the parks, by the river. Very thin. Nobody can see it. But you can breathe it in, if you know how. And if you breathe it in, your body has something to pay with. The gift doesn't have to eat you."

Juno looked at him as if he had started speaking a foreign language.

"Breathe it in," she repeated.

"Yes."

"Energy in the air. That nobody can see."

"Yes."

"And you breathe it in. Like… air."

"Not exactly like air. There's a method. A rhythm." He hesitated, then decided he might as well say it all. "Actually, it's partly your rhythm. One, two, three, breathe, four. I mixed it with something else. Breathing exercises."

"What breathing exercises?"

"Tai chi."

Juno stared at him.

"There's a group of old women," Ye Xuan said, "in Columbus Park, at six in the morning, by the big tree. They do tai chi. They've been doing it every morning for years. And without knowing it, they've been breathing in energy from the air the whole time. That's why they're all so healthy. Granny Liu is ninety-one and she walks faster than me."

Juno kept staring.

"You want me," she said slowly, "to go to Chinatown at six in the morning and do tai chi with a bunch of grannies."

"Yes."

"To fix my legs."

"To *start* fixing your legs. I can fix the worst strands, one at a time, if I have enough energy. But that just patches the damage. If you learn to breathe, you stop the damage from happening in the first place."

Juno opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

Then she started to laugh.

She laughed so hard she had to hold onto the coffee table. She laughed until the tears came back, and this time they weren't the angry kind. Ye Xuan sat on the concrete and waited, holding the bag of melting peas, until she ran out.

"Okay," she gasped finally. "Okay. Grannies. Six in the morning. Fine. If this is a joke, I'm kicking you off a roof."

"It's not a joke."

"I know." She wiped her eyes again and looked at him, and the laughter faded into something quieter. "I know it's not."

* * *

He had his hand on the garage door, about to duck out under it, when she said, "Ye."

He turned.

She was still sitting on the coffee table, her left leg stretched out in front of her, her hand resting on her calf.

"Mateo," she said. "My brother. The asthma." She hesitated, and he saw how much it cost her to ask. "When you look at people. Not gifts—just people. Could you…"

She didn't finish.

Ye Xuan thought about the lines inside the phone. Inside the engine. Inside Kroll and Rocco and Juno. He had never looked at an ordinary person who was sick. He had no idea what he would see. He had no idea if he could do anything.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But I can look."

Juno nodded slowly.

"Six o'clock," she said. "Columbus Park. If the grannies eat me alive, it's your fault."

"They will definitely try," Ye Xuan said, and went out into the night.
