The first block nearly killed him.

He came up the ramp and out onto Canal Street at a sprint, and Canal Street simply did not care. It was a river of people—tourists with shopping bags, delivery men pushing carts of fish, office workers with coffee, a man selling fake designer sunglasses from a folding table. Ye Xuan dodged left, then right, then straight into a woman with a stroller, who shouted something in Italian that didn't need translation.

By the corner of Broadway, he was gasping.

His new body was better than it had been yesterday. The first layer of Qi Gathering had cleaned it, sharpened it, filled his lungs deeper. But it was still the body of a skinny engineering student who had skipped meals for a month. And it was still being asked to run two miles in about ten minutes.

*This won't work,* he thought. *Not like this.*

The light at Broadway turned red. A wall of taxis surged forward, honking.

Ye Xuan stopped at the curb, breathing hard, and did the only thing he could think of.

He looked.

* * *

The world shifted.

He didn't look deeply—he couldn't afford a nosebleed now. He let the sight skim across the street like a hand brushing water.

And the city opened.

The traffic light above him was a simple thing: a timer, a sensor loop under the asphalt, a small box that talked to other small boxes. But it didn't stand alone. Thin lines ran from it to the next light, and the next, all the way down Broadway—a chain. He saw it at once, with the obvious clarity of a solved equation: the lights were timed together. When this one turned green, the next would turn green eleven seconds later, and the next eleven seconds after that. A wave, rolling down the avenue at exactly the speed of a car obeying the limit.

*A green wave.*

And the crowds. He had thought they were chaos. They weren't. Every person was a thread, and the threads moved in patterns—thicker at the corners, thinner in the middle of each block, streaming around obstacles like water around rocks. There were currents. There were eddies. There were long, smooth channels along the curb where nobody walked because everybody was looking at their phones and drifting toward the shop windows.

It was all there. It had always been there. He just hadn't been able to *see* it.

The light turned green.

Ye Xuan ran.

He didn't run straight down Broadway. He ran with the wave—hitting each corner just as its light changed, never stopping, never waiting. When the crowd thickened, he slid into the channels along the curb. When a delivery truck blocked the sidewalk, he had already seen the gap behind it two seconds before it opened.

He breathed as he ran—one, two, three, *fold*—drawing in the thin threads of the city's qi and folding them into his newly opened dantian. Not enough to jump. Not enough for anything dramatic. Just enough to keep the warmth in his legs, to keep his lungs from burning, to let his body move a little lighter, a little longer, than it should have.

City Hall flashed past on his left. Then the narrow canyons of the Financial District, where the buildings rose so high that the street was in shadow at nine in the morning. He cut through a plaza, around a giant bronze bull surrounded by tourists, down a street so narrow it felt like a hallway.

And then there was the water, and the trees of Battery Park, and a glass office building with a sign that read HARBOR MUTUAL INSURANCE.

He checked the stopwatch app on his cracked phone. Eleven minutes, forty seconds.

* * *

The receptionist was a bored young man with a man-bun who looked at the envelope, looked at Ye Xuan—red-faced, soaked with sweat, steaming slightly in the cool morning air—and said, "Skyline?"

"Skyline."

"You guys usually come through the window." The receptionist scribbled a signature on the envelope's slip, tore it off and handed it over. "Is the girl in the red jacket sick?"

"She's busy," Ye Xuan said, and ran.

* * *

The way back was harder.

His legs were heavier now. The thread-by-thread trickle of qi wasn't keeping up with what he spent. The green wave on Broadway rolled north just as well as it rolled south, but halfway up, near City Hall, it broke: an entire block had been closed for construction. A plywood fence ran across the sidewalk and into the street, three meters high, plastered with posters. Police barriers channeled the crowd around it, across the avenue, down a side street, around the block. Three minutes, at least. Maybe four.

He looked at the stopwatch. Seventeen minutes, ten seconds.

He looked at the fence.

*Rule five,* he thought. *Never use it where cameras can see.*

He looked up. A traffic camera on the corner, pointed at the intersection. A security dome above a bank door, pointed at the bank door. On the fence itself, nothing. And behind the fence—he let his sight skim through the plywood—an empty lot of sand and gravel and a parked excavator, with no workers yet.

The crowd was moving away from the fence, toward the police barriers.

For three seconds, nobody was looking.

Ye Xuan stepped back. One step. Two. Three—*breathe*—and he pulled every thread of warmth he had out of his dantian at once and drove it down into his legs—

Four. *Release.*

The ground dropped away.

It wasn't the jump of yesterday, when he had nearly broken his neck on a fire escape. He had less qi now, and he spent it more carefully. He rose just high enough—his sneakers skimming the top of the plywood, a poster of a smiling politician flapping under his foot—and dropped into the empty lot on the other side, landing in soft sand with a crunch.

He didn't stop. He ran across the lot, between the excavator and a pile of steel pipes, and swung over the far fence the ordinary way, with his hands, scraping his palms, and dropped onto the sidewalk at the other end of the block.

A pigeon looked at him. A man walking a pug looked at him.

"Morning," Ye Xuan said, and kept running.

His dantian was empty. Completely, coldly empty, like a pocket after payday. His legs were shaking. But the green wave on Broadway was waiting for him, and he caught it, and rode it north, and when he finally turned onto Canal Street, he could see the red garage door and the flickering sign of Lucky Suds.

He stumbled down the ramp and under the door and slapped the signed slip onto Big Lou's desk.

Big Lou looked at the stopwatch in his hand.

He looked at it for a long time.

"Twenty-three minutes," he said. "And ten seconds."

Behind him, Tank let out a low whistle. Wren had taken off her headphones. She was looking at her screen, where a little blue dot—his phone—had traced a line down Broadway and back up.

"He went through the construction site on Broadway," she said slowly. "There's no path there. The dot just goes straight through."

"I climbed the fence," Ye Xuan said, still trying to breathe. "Twice."

"In five seconds?"

"I'm good at fences."

Juno was leaning against a shelf with her arms crossed. She didn't say anything. But the corner of her mouth twitched upward, very slightly.

Big Lou took the cigar out of his mouth and studied Ye Xuan the way an old boxer studies a young one who has just taken a punch that should have put him down, and didn't go down.

"No gift, huh," he said.

"No gift."

"Uh-huh." Lou put the cigar back. "Okay, kid. Ground runner. Twelve a delivery, tips are yours, cash at six. You start tomorrow at eight. Tank, get him a jacket."

Tank walked to a rack on the wall and came back with a red jacket, folded neatly. On the back, in white letters: **SKYLINE COURIERS — WE DON'T DO STAIRS**.

"Welcome," Tank said in his soft voice, and smiled. It was a very kind smile for someone who could lift a refrigerator with one hand.

Ye Xuan took the jacket. He felt absurdly happy. A job. A real job, with cash at six. Thirty-three dollars and seventeen cents was about to become something more.

He turned to thank Big Lou—

And stopped.

Wren had taken her headphones all the way off now. She was sitting very still at her desk, her head tilted slightly, like a bird listening for something in the grass. Her pale freckled face had gone serious.

She wasn't looking at him.

She was looking at the left side of his black windbreaker. At the inner pocket, where a gray stone lay wrapped in a sock, pressed against his ribs.

Listening.
