That night, the stone was on his desk, and Ye Xuan was trying very hard not to look at it.

He had taken it out of the sock and set it on a folded towel under the lamp, like a patient on an operating table. It sat there—gray, smooth, unremarkable—and if he hadn't known better, he would have thought it was just a pebble someone had brought back from a beach as a souvenir.

But he knew better.

*Something out there is listening for it,* Wren had said.

"Okay," he said aloud. "Problem: you're loud. Solution: make you quiet."

He had grown up in a family where problems were solved this way. His father was an engineer at a water treatment plant in Hangzhou; when something broke, he never cursed, never panicked. He sat down, drew the problem on a napkin, and asked: *what is it, actually? What does it do? Why?*

Ye Xuan pulled a napkin toward him and wrote:

*What is the "heartbeat"?*

Wren could hear it. Halcyon's crystals could feel it. So—sound? No. Wren's hearing was a gift; she heard heartbeats through walls, but she had said it wasn't really sound, just *something* her gift turned into sound. Radio waves? Possible. The crystals in the glass column had reacted from twenty meters away, through his jacket.

If it was radio, he knew how to block it. Every engineering student knew.

* * *

The bakery was dark and closed. He went down the stairs in his socks, past the steel table and the cooling racks, to the back kitchen where Mrs. Tang kept a microwave oven older than he was.

He opened its door and looked at the window.

The world shifted—gently, a skim. Inside the glass, he saw what he expected: a fine metal mesh, a grid of tiny holes. The holes were smaller than the microwaves inside the oven, so the waves couldn't pass through, while visible light, with its much smaller waves, could. A cage made of holes. Faraday's cage, the textbooks called it.

*So if I wrap the stone in metal mesh, or in foil…*

He took the roll of aluminum foil from the shelf, went upstairs, wrapped the stone in four layers, put it in the sock, and called Wren.

She answered on the second ring, sounding sleepy and irritated.

"It's eleven," she said.

"Sorry. Can you hear it now?"

A pause. He could hear her listening—or imagined he could.

"Ye, I'm in the Bronx. I can't hear you from the Bronx." Another pause. "Tomorrow. Bring it to work. And don't call me at eleven unless something's on fire."

She hung up.

* * *

The next morning, Wren took off her headphones as he came down the ramp, tilted her head, and listened.

"Nope," she said. "Same as yesterday. Two hearts."

"I wrapped it in foil."

"Foil," Wren repeated, and the corner of her mouth twitched. "Did you also try putting it in the fridge?"

He hadn't, but he had thought about it.

So it wasn't radio. Whatever the stone was sending out passed through metal as if metal didn't exist.

All day, between deliveries, he thought about it. On the green wave down Broadway. In the elevator of a Midtown tower. In a lobby on Park Avenue, waiting for a lawyer's assistant to sign for a box of contracts.

*What passes through metal? What passes through everything?*

And he remembered the threads.

The pale lines of qi drifting through Columbus Park didn't care about walls. They drifted through windows and brick and fences and people. They were thinner indoors, but they were *there*.

*It isn't sending out a signal,* he realized, standing in the lobby with the box of contracts in his arms. *It's breathing. It breathes qi out, the way I breathe it in. And anything that can feel qi—Wren's gift, those crystals, whatever else is out there—feels it breathing.*

You couldn't block that with foil. You'd need something that worked with qi itself.

The lawyer's assistant took the box and gave him a five-dollar tip. He barely noticed.

* * *

That night, he broke Rule one.

Not completely. Carefully. He sat at the desk with the stone on the towel, set the alarm on his phone for three seconds, and let his eyes focus—only on the surface. The very outermost layer. Like reading the cover of a book without opening it.

The world shifted.

The ocean was there, underneath; he could feel it pulling at him, endless and deep. He refused it. He kept his attention on the surface, the skin of the stone, and there he saw something simple enough to understand.

The stone breathed in rings.

Slow, concentric ripples of pale light spread out from its surface, one after another, like the circles that spread when you drop a pebble into still water. Each ring was a pattern—a fine, repeating lattice of lines, almost like the mesh in the microwave door, but woven from light instead of metal. The rings expanded outward, thinned, and passed through the towel, the desk, the walls, the city.

*Beep.* The alarm went off.

Ye Xuan tore his eyes away. A thin, warm trickle ran from his left nostril. He pressed a tissue to it and sat back, breathing hard.

Three seconds. And he had seen it.

*A wave,* he thought. *A repeating wave with a pattern. And if a wave has a pattern…*

In his first life, he had owned a pair of cheap noise-canceling headphones that he had bought to study on the bus. He had taken them apart once, out of curiosity. Inside was a tiny microphone that listened to the noise of the bus, and a tiny circuit that produced the exact opposite of that noise—the same wave, flipped upside down. When the two waves met, they canceled each other out. Silence.

*If I could make a pattern that's the opposite of the stone's rings—*

He sat up straight.

* * *

The hardware store on Grand Street was about to close when he ran in the next evening after work. He bought a spool of thin copper wire for six dollars and some change. At the bodega next door, he bought a small round tin of mints, the kind with a hinged lid.

He ate the mints on the way home. All of them. For engineering purposes.

At the desk, he laid out the wire, the empty tin, a pair of pliers borrowed from Tank, and the napkin on which he had sketched, from memory, the lattice of the stone's outer rings.

Then he drew it again, upside down.

Every line where the stone's pattern bent left, his bent right. Every place where the stone's lattice was dense, his was open. A mirror image. A negative. If he was right—and he had no idea if he was right—the stone's rings would hit this pattern and fold back on themselves.

He began to wind the wire around the tin.

It took three hours. The pattern was fine and precise, and copper wire did not want to bend the way he wanted it to bend. Twice he got the spacing wrong and had to unwind everything and start again. His fingers ached. Downstairs, the radio in the bakery went quiet, and the lights on Mott Street went out one by one.

At one in the morning, he held up the finished tin.

It looked like something a ten-year-old would make for a school science fair. Lumpy copper wire wrapped around a dented mint tin in a pattern that, to anyone else, would look completely random.

"It's not enough," he murmured.

He could see it. The pattern was right, but it was *dead*—just metal, shaped. The stone's rings were alive, made of qi. Copper alone couldn't fold them back. It needed—

He thought of the threads in his dantian. Of how, on the fire escape, a single thread of qi had turned his legs into springs.

He placed his fingertips on the copper wire, closed his eyes, and breathed. One, two, three, *fold*. He drew a thread from the warmth below his navel, led it up through his chest, down his arm, into his fingertips—

—and pushed it into the wire.

It was like pouring water into a maze. The thread slid into the copper and ran along the pattern, following every bend, every curve, and as it ran, the pattern *woke*. He felt it happen. The dead shape of metal became something else: a shape that held qi, that remembered it, that hummed with it faintly even after his fingers lifted away.

His nose bled. His hands shook. His dantian was a cold, empty pocket again.

But when he opened the tin, put the stone inside—sock and all—and closed the lid, the warmth he always felt from the stone through the sock dimmed.

Not gone. But dimmer. Like a heater behind a wall instead of a heater in the room.

* * *

"Quieter," Wren said the next morning, tilting her head.

Ye Xuan let out a breath he felt he had been holding for three days.

"Not gone," she added. "But quieter. It used to sound like a drum. Now it sounds like… a drum in another apartment." She looked at the lumpy tin sticking out of his jacket pocket and raised an eyebrow. "Is that a mint tin?"

"It's an engineering prototype."

"It's a mint tin with wire on it."

"It's an engineering prototype," Ye Xuan said with dignity, "that happens to have once contained mints."

Wren laughed—a short, surprised sound, as if she didn't do it often—and put her headphones back on.

He went out on his first delivery of the day thinking not about the stone, but about the wire.

He had taken a pattern—a pattern he had seen with his comprehension—and written it into copper. And the copper had *held* it. Held the qi, held the shape, done something that copper should never have been able to do.

*If I can write one pattern into metal,* he thought, *what else can I write?*

Somewhere far behind him, on a rooftop, the man in the long black coat stood very still, his head tilted, as if listening to something that had just become a little harder to hear.
