Opening his dantian did not pay the rent.

Ye Xuan sat on the bed after Granny Liu left, holding the red card Juno had flicked down to him on the fire escape, and did the math.

Two weeks of rent: four hundred and seventy-five dollars. A full month by Friday, if Mrs. Tang decided she had run out of patience, which, judging by the plumber, she had: nine hundred and fifty. Money in the bank: forty-three dollars and seventeen cents, minus six dollars of fried rice, minus four dollars for a toothbrush and soap.

Thirty-three dollars and seventeen cents.

*In the novels,* he thought, *the hero finds a bag of spirit stones in a cave. Or sells a pill recipe to a rich merchant. Or saves the daughter of a powerful family who insists on paying him a fortune.*

Nobody had ever written a novel about a cultivator who had to find a part-time job.

The card was bright red, slightly bent, with a winged shoe in the corner and three lines of white text:

**SKYLINE COURIERS**
**Canal St. — under Lucky Suds**
**WE DON'T DO STAIRS**

He put it in his pocket, boiled Granny Liu's herbs in the bakery's back kitchen while Mrs. Tang pointedly ignored him, drank a bowl of something that tasted like bark and regret, and went out.

* * *

Lucky Suds was a laundromat with a flickering sign and a cat asleep in the window. Next to it, a steep concrete ramp led down to a garage door painted the same bright red as the card. The door was half open. From inside came music, the clank of metal, and a man's voice shouting about Midtown.

Ye Xuan ducked under the door.

The garage was bigger than it looked from outside—a long, low space with bare concrete walls covered in maps of Manhattan, pinned with colored thumbtacks. Bicycles hung from hooks on the ceiling. Shelves of packages, envelopes, and boxes lined one wall. An old leather couch with a strip of duct tape across one cushion sat beside a coffee machine that looked older than he was.

At a desk in the middle, behind three monitors, sat a girl of about sixteen with bright red hair, a face full of freckles, and enormous headphones. She didn't look up.

"Truck's on Thirty-Fourth, says he can't get closer," she said into a microphone. "Tell him Tank is coming. Yes, *the* Tank. No, he doesn't need a dolly."

At the far end of the garage, a man was lifting a wooden crate the size of a refrigerator onto a hand truck.

With one hand.

He was the biggest human being Ye Xuan had ever seen—at least two meters tall, wide as a doorway, with dark skin, a shaved head and a soft, almost shy face. As he lifted, the skin on his forearm turned gray and rough for a moment, like weathered granite, then faded back to normal when he set the crate down.

*Awakened,* Ye Xuan thought, and felt his other sight stir.

He held it back. Rule two applied to more than federal agents. *Don't stare.*

"Can I help you?"

The voice came from a glass-walled office in the corner. A man stood in its doorway: sixty or so, short and broad, with a flattened nose, ears like cauliflowers, and a gray mustache. An unlit cigar was clamped in the corner of his mouth. His knuckles were scarred, and he held himself with the loose, heavy balance of someone who had spent a long time in a boxing ring.

"I'm looking for work," Ye Xuan said. "Juno gave me this."

He held up the card.

The man looked at the card, then at Ye Xuan, then at the card again. He took the cigar out of his mouth.

"Juno," he called, without raising his voice much. "You hand out my cards to strays again?"

Something thumped onto the roof.

A square skylight in the ceiling swung open, and Juno Reyes dropped through it—three meters straight down—landing on the concrete in a crouch so light it barely made a sound. Her red jacket flared. Her braid swung over her shoulder.

She straightened and looked at Ye Xuan with an expression he couldn't read.

"I said we were short of runners," she said. "I didn't say he'd actually show up."

"Well, he showed up." The man stuck the cigar back in his mouth. "Lou Castellano. Everybody calls me Big Lou, on account of I'm short. This is my company. The girl on the radio is Wren. The guy lifting my furniture is Tank. You've met Juno." He looked Ye Xuan up and down. "What's your gift?"

"I don't have one."

The garage went quiet.

Wren looked up from her monitors for the first time. Tank paused with his hand on the hand truck. Juno crossed her arms.

"Kid," Big Lou said, not unkindly. "Look around. This is an Awakened outfit. We don't do stairs. We don't do traffic. Juno goes over buildings. Tank carries stuff that needs a forklift. Wren hears a package fall off a truck six blocks away and tells us which truck. What do you do?"

*I copy gifts by looking at them,* Ye Xuan thought. *I breathe energy that nobody else in this world can see. I'm carrying a stone that a man was murdered for, and probably somebody is looking for it right now.*

"I'm fast," he said. "And I'm good at figuring out the quickest way to get somewhere."

"You know Manhattan?"

He had arrived in Manhattan yesterday.

"I'm learning fast."

Big Lou chewed his cigar. He glanced at Juno. Something passed between them—a question.

Juno shrugged. "He's quick," she said. "I saw him."

She didn't say what she'd seen. She didn't mention the jump, or the fire escape, or the four-count rhythm that was hers. She just looked at Ye Xuan with those narrowed dark eyes, and he understood that she was keeping his secret, and that she expected an explanation for it later.

"Quick," Big Lou repeated. "Okay. Quick, I can use. I pay twelve bucks a delivery for ground runners, cash, end of day, plus whatever tips you get, and you keep all the tips. Ground runners are for the stuff that doesn't need a gift—documents, small packages, lunch for lawyers who think they're too important to walk to the deli. You lose a package, you pay for it. You're late, you don't get paid. Questions?"

"No."

"Good. Then we test." Big Lou walked to a shelf, took down a thick yellow envelope, and wrote something on it with a marker. "There's an insurance office at the bottom of Manhattan, by Battery Park. Address is on the envelope. You take this there, get a signature from the front desk, and bring the signature back to me. No subway. No cabs. No bikes. Your own two feet."

He pulled an old silver stopwatch from his vest pocket.

"You've got twenty-five minutes."

Ye Xuan looked at the envelope. Battery Park. He pictured a map of Manhattan in his head—a long, narrow island shaped like a fish. Canal Street was somewhere in the lower third. Battery Park was the very tip of the tail.

"How far is it?" he asked.

"About two miles there," Wren said helpfully, without looking up. "Two miles back."

Six and a half kilometers round trip. Through the most crowded streets on Earth. In twenty-five minutes. Olympic distance runners could do it, on an empty track.

"Lou," Tank said gently, in a voice that was surprisingly soft. "That's the Juno test. Nobody does that on foot."

"Then he doesn't get the job." Big Lou held up the stopwatch. "Wren tracks your phone. If you cheat, she'll know. Ready?"

Ye Xuan took the envelope. He zipped up his windbreaker—feeling the stone in its sock press against his ribs—and looked at Juno.

She raised an eyebrow. *Well?*

"Ready," he said.

Big Lou clicked the stopwatch.

"Go."
