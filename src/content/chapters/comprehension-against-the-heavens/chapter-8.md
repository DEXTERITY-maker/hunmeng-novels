Wren didn't say anything that day. She put her headphones back on, turned to her monitors, and told a driver on Thirty-Fourth Street that Tank was on his way.

But Ye Xuan felt her attention on his back all the way up the ramp.

* * *

At eight the next morning, he was a courier.

It turned out to be a strange, wonderful, exhausting job. Wren sent the addresses to his phone, one after another, with little notes: *Back door, the doorman hates us. Ask for Gloria, not the receptionist. Elevator 3 is broken, use 4.* Big Lou handed him envelopes, tubes of architectural drawings, a box of lab samples marked FRAGILE in three languages, and once, inexplicably, a live goldfish in a plastic bag.

"Don't ask," Big Lou said. "Lawyers."

Ye Xuan didn't ask. He ran.

He ran the green waves on Broadway and Sixth Avenue. He learned that Wall Street was not one street but a maze, and that the lobbies of its buildings were guarded by men in gray uniforms who looked at a red Skyline jacket the way a cat looks at a mouse that has walked into its kitchen. He learned that everyone in Midtown was in a hurry, that nobody in Tribeca tipped, and that the tiny old ladies who ran the herbal shops on Elizabeth Street tipped in oranges.

He used his sight sparingly—skimming, never staring. The elevator in a forty-story tower on Water Street: a cable, a counterweight, a motor, a brain that decided which floor to stop at first. He saw that if he pressed the button for thirty-one and then thirty-two, the brain would send him the express car. He saved two minutes. A revolving door that always jammed: a worn bearing on the left side. He pushed on the right.

Small things. Tiny understandings. But together they made him fast—so fast that by noon Wren had stopped adding notes to his addresses and simply sent them, as she did for Juno and Tank.

By one o'clock, he had made six deliveries and forty-one dollars in tips. And two oranges.

He was eating one of the oranges on a bench in a little triangular park when his phone buzzed with the next address.

**Halcyon Dynamics — 200 Water St — Lobby — Envelope for Dr. Amari, Research Liaison**

Ye Xuan stopped chewing.

* * *

He had seen the name on the front page of the newspaper the day before, in an article he had read three times. He had seen it on billboards: a golden sunburst on a white background, and beneath it, three words.

**ASCENSION FOR EVERYONE.**

Halcyon Dynamics. The biggest corporation in the world that had been built on the Glow. Medical research, materials, energy, security—and the Zenith Initiative, the team of Awakened heroes that appeared on the news every time a building collapsed or a bridge caught fire.

He thought of Kroll's voice: *Crane wasn't alone on that pier.*

*It's an envelope,* he told himself. *It's an envelope for a research liaison. Couriers deliver envelopes to Halcyon a hundred times a day.*

He finished his orange and went.

The Halcyon building on Water Street was not their headquarters—that was the tower in Midtown that he had seen from the Brooklyn Bridge in the newspaper photo. This was a branch office, but even a Halcyon branch office was a temple: forty stories of white stone and golden glass, with a plaza out front where water fell over a sheet of polished granite into a pool.

The lobby was enormous, and quiet, and bright.

And in the middle of it, rising from the floor to the ceiling three stories above, stood a sculpture.

Ye Xuan stopped walking.

It was a column of glass—clear, flawless, as thick as the old locust tree in Columbus Park. Inside the glass, suspended at different heights as if floating in water, were crystals. Dozens of them. Some the size of a fingernail, some as big as a fist. Every one was gray. And every one held, deep inside, a faint vein of white light.

A brass plaque at the base read:

**THE GIFT OF THE SKY**
*Glow crystals recovered from the Pacific Luminous Zone.*
*Donated to the people of New York by Halcyon Dynamics.*

Against his ribs, inside the sock, inside the inner pocket of the black windbreaker, the stone *moved*.

Not a pulse. A lurch—as if it had been asleep and something had shouted its name. It went hot, so hot that he flinched, and then it began to beat. Fast. Hard. Faster than his own heart.

And inside the glass column, the crystals answered.

He saw it with his ordinary eyes, not his sight. The faint white veins inside the crystals brightened—flickered—all at once, like a crowd of faces turning toward him. For one second, the whole column shimmered with a light that had nothing to do with the sun pouring through the golden glass.

Then it faded.

A security guard near the column frowned and looked up, as if he had seen a lamp flicker. He tapped the radio on his shoulder, then shook his head and let it go.

Ye Xuan was already walking toward the reception desk. Steadily. Not too fast. Not too slow. His heart was hammering.

*Don't run. Don't run. Couriers don't run in lobbies.*

"Envelope for Dr. Amari," he said to the receptionist. His voice came out perfectly normal. He was almost proud of it.

The receptionist—a young woman with a Halcyon pin and a smile so professional it looked painted on—scanned a barcode, typed something, and held out a tablet for his signature.

"Have a wonderful day," she said. "Ascension for everyone!"

"Ascension for everyone," Ye Xuan said, and walked out.

He didn't let himself breathe until he was two blocks away, around a corner, in the shadow of a parking garage. Then he leaned against the wall and pressed his hand against his ribs.

The stone was still beating. Slower now, settling. Warm, like an animal that had been startled and was slowly calming down.

*They're the same,* he thought. *Those crystals in the glass. They're the same kind of thing as this. But smaller. Weaker. Broken.*

*And this one is not broken.*

He didn't know how he knew. He hadn't looked—Rule one. But he had felt the crystals in the column answer his stone the way a choir answers a conductor. They had brightened for it. They had *turned* toward it.

He closed his eyes.

*What did you carry, Silas Crane? What did you give a nineteen-year-old engineering student on a pier at four in the morning?*

* * *

He made four more deliveries that afternoon, and at six he came down the ramp into the garage with eighty-nine dollars in tips, a sore back and a head full of questions.

Big Lou paid him in cash from a metal box: one hundred and twenty dollars for ten deliveries. Ye Xuan held the bills and felt like a king.

Tank clapped him on the shoulder, very gently, as if he were afraid of breaking him. Juno wasn't there; she was out on a late run to Harlem. Big Lou retired to his glass office to shout at someone on the phone about invoices.

Ye Xuan was zipping up his windbreaker when Wren said, "Hey. Runner."

She had taken her headphones off. She sat at her desk with her knees pulled up to her chin, looking at him with that bird-like stillness.

"Ye," he said. "My name's Ye."

"Ye." She seemed to try the name out. "You know what my gift is, right?"

"You hear things."

"I hear *everything*." She said it without pride, the way someone might say *I have bad knees*. "Everything in about a block. Right now I can hear a woman crying in the laundromat upstairs because her husband forgot their anniversary. I can hear the cat in the window purring. I can hear Lou's heart—he drinks too much coffee. I can hear Tank humming in the back. I can hear yours."

She paused.

"Both of yours."

Ye Xuan said nothing.

"Yesterday, when you came back from the Battery Park run, I thought my ears were messing with me. Two heartbeats. One in your chest. One—" She pointed, not at his heart but lower and to the left, at the inner pocket of the windbreaker. "There. It's slower than yours. It's like… like something sleeping. But today, around two o'clock, you were on Water Street, and it got loud. *Really* loud. I could hear it from here." She tapped her ear. "Eight blocks."

"Around two," he repeated quietly.

"Mm-hm." Wren tilted her head. "Halcyon building's on Water Street."

The garage was very quiet. Upstairs, faintly, a washing machine began its spin cycle.

"I'm not going to tell Lou," Wren said. "I don't tell Lou anything. Lou doesn't want to know half the stuff I hear, believe me." She pulled her headphones back up around her neck. "But Ye? Whatever's in your jacket—it isn't quiet. I'm probably not the only thing in this city that can hear it."

She put the headphones on and turned back to her monitors.

"Good first day," she added, without looking up. "See you at eight."
