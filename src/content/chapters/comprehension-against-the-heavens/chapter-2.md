Mrs. Tang was smaller than her voice.

She waited outside Ye Xuan's room in a flour-streaked apron, holding a warm bun and an expression of disappointment.

“You owe rent, you look half-starved, and now people with badges are drinking my tea. This is not a good week for you.”

“I don't remember much of the last few days.”

“Then remember this. You still owe me money.”

She pushed the bun into his hand before leading him downstairs.

The bakery smelled of scallion oil and coffee. At a table by the window sat a woman with short dark hair and a broad-shouldered man in a gray suit. They stood when Ye Xuan approached.

“Priya Shah,” the woman said, showing a badge. “Bureau of Awakened Affairs. This is Daniel Kroll.”

“Am I under arrest?”

“No. We'd like your help establishing the events of October twenty-third.”

“Am I free to leave?”

“Yes. You're also free to refuse an interview.”

He sat. If he ran from everyone who might know something about the borrowed body's past, he would never learn what had happened.

Kroll placed a photograph on the table. It showed a warehouse beside dark water. A figure entered the frame beneath a streetlamp, face hidden by a raised collar.

“Do you recognize the location?”

“No.”

“Pier Seventeen. Your student pass was registered at an access gate nearby at ten forty-six that night.”

“Could someone else have used it?”

“That is possible,” Shah answered.

He watched her write down his words. The distinction between a registered pass and a verified person mattered to her.

“Why are you looking for me?”

“A technician at the site is missing,” Shah said. “We have an incomplete account of an unusual equipment test. Your name appears in the visitor records.”

Missing.

He thought of Ye Ming and the way every telephone call during the first month had made his mother stand up. Three years later, the word could still empty a room.

“Was the technician hurt?”

“We don't know. We haven't verified the last reported sighting.”

Kroll shifted. The table creaked, and a teaspoon rolled toward the man's elbow. Kroll set one finger on the wood. The teaspoon stopped.

Ye Xuan looked at it.

“You're Awakened,” he said before he could stop himself.

Kroll's expression changed slightly. “Registered, yes.”

“Something involving weight?”

“Not relevant to this interview.”

Ye Xuan had seen an ordinary object behave strangely. His talent did not reach into strangers and provide their secrets.

Shah slid a card across the table. “If you recover records of that evening, contact us. Please don't enter the restricted pier yourself. We can arrange another meeting if you want counsel present.”

They left without a threat. The quiet afterward felt provisional.

He saved the messages and wrote their dates on a receipt. He was learning how easy it was to tell a story from fragments, and how dangerous to believe the first story that fitted them.

Then he turned to the second physics volume.

The models were suddenly obvious, along with the experiments supporting them and the places where they remained uncertain.

An idea emerged immediately: a different way to account for one source of measurement noise in an induction experiment. He wrote it down in excitement.

Then he realized he had no instruments to test it.

That was the shape of his gift. Understanding could come in an instant. Reality still demanded equipment, time and proof.

The bakery's temperature controller had been displaying impossible readings. Mrs. Tang allowed him to inspect its diagnostic screen while a technician she trusted handled the machinery.

Ye Xuan recognized a pattern consistent with a failing sensor. The technician checked the readings and nodded.

“Good guess.”

“A hypothesis,” Ye Xuan corrected. “You verified it.”

The technician laughed. A replacement part had to be ordered. Comprehension could not make it appear.

Mrs. Tang offered him more food and extended the rent deadline by three days. It was not wealth, but it was a beginning.

This world's impossible powers had institutions, training programs and insurance forms. They were part of an economy.

His phone vibrated.

Unknown sender: The clock at the pier was fourteen minutes slow. Ask why the official report uses its time.

A second message appeared.

Do not trust the photograph they showed you.

He saved the messages, recorded their timestamps and sat on a bench to compare them with the records already in his possession.

He had wanted an answer. He had received a claim from a source whose reliability he knew nothing about.

He opened his notebook.

Under What I Know, he wrote three lines. The rest of the page remained blank.

For the first time in his life, he could understand every book he finished. But the person he most needed to understand had left no book behind.
