Mrs. Tang was smaller than her voice.

She stood in the narrow hallway with her arms crossed—a woman in her sixties who barely reached Ye Xuan's shoulder, with flour on her apron and reading glasses pushed up into her gray hair. She had the face of someone who had spent forty years telling delivery drivers they were late.

She took one look at him, and the scolding died in her throat.

"Aiya." She grabbed his chin and turned his face toward the light. "What happened to you? There's blood on your shirt. You look like a ghost who forgot to eat."

"Nosebleed," Ye Xuan said. "It's nothing."

It came out in Mandarin, easily and naturally—with a slight New York softness on the vowels that wasn't his. *The body remembers,* he thought, *even if I don't.*

"Nothing." Mrs. Tang snorted. "Three days you hide in this room. No rent, no food, and now blood. And government men in my shop, scaring my customers." She lowered her voice. "Listen to me, Xiao Ye. I don't know what you did, and I don't want to know. But those Bureau people never bring good news. You answer what they ask. Nothing more. Understand?"

"I understand." He hesitated. "Mrs. Tang… thank you."

She gave him a strange look, as if he had said something unusual. Maybe the old Ye Xuan hadn't thanked people much.

"Change your shirt," she said. "And pay me by Friday. Friday!"

* * *

He changed his shirt. On the way down the stairs, he opened the banking app on the cracked phone.

**Available balance: $43.17.**

Rent for a room in Manhattan—even a room the size of a closet above a bakery—was not going to be $43.17.

"Transmigrated into a broke student," he muttered. "The most classic opening of them all."

* * *

The bakery downstairs was called Golden Lotus. It was warm and bright and smelled even better than it had through the floor: egg tarts cooling on metal trays, pineapple buns, a basket of sesame balls, a steel pot of soy milk steaming behind the counter.

Two people in gray suits sat at the only table by the window, and the space around them had gone quiet in the particular way spaces go quiet around the police.

The older one was big—not fat, but built like a door. Maybe fifty, with a gray crew cut and a calm, heavy face. He held a paper cup of coffee in a hand that made it look like a toy. The younger one was a woman in her late twenties, with a sharp black bob and a tablet resting on her knees. Her suit was the same gray, but she wore it like armor.

"Mr. Ye." The big man stood. He did it slowly, and Ye Xuan heard the old floorboards creak under him—a deep, complaining sound, as if he weighed far more than he should. "Special Agent Daniel Kroll, Bureau of Awakened Affairs. This is Agent Priya Shah. Do you have a few minutes?"

He held out a badge in a leather wallet.

Ye Xuan looked at it.

He didn't mean to. He was nervous, his head still ached, and when he was nervous he paid attention to details—an old habit from exam halls. He looked at the badge, and the world *shifted* again.

The badge opened itself to him: the hologram layer, a fine lattice of reflective film that split light into colors; a thin chip under the photo, humming with a stored signal. That part was simple, almost boring.

But behind the badge stood Kroll.

And inside Kroll, there were lines.

They weren't like the lines inside the phone. Those had been thin, neat, orderly—the logic of something built. These were alive. Thick threads of dark gold coiled around the man's bones like chains wrapped around a pillar, dense and heavy and slowly turning. When Kroll shifted his weight, the threads tightened, and the floorboards groaned.

*He's Awakened,* Ye Xuan realized. *And his power is… weight? Density? He can make himself heavier.*

And he could understand more. He felt it pressing at the edge of his mind—the way the threads pulled at the world around them, the rhythm of them, the single place in Kroll's chest where they all began, like roots growing from one seed. If he looked just a little longer, he would understand *how*—

The headache flared, white and sharp.

Ye Xuan forced his eyes down to the photo on the badge and kept them there. *Don't stare. Not now. Not at a federal agent.*

The lines faded. He felt a warm trickle start in his nose and sniffed hard, as if he had a cold.

"Sure," he said. His voice came out almost normal. "A few minutes."

* * *

They sat. Mrs. Tang put a plate of egg tarts in front of him without a word and went back behind the counter, where she began wiping a perfectly clean surface very slowly, well within earshot.

Agent Shah turned her tablet around. On the screen was a grainy security-camera still: a pier at night, lit by orange lamps. A young man in a dark hoodie was walking along the railing with his hands in his pockets. The time stamp read **03:41 AM**.

The young man's face was turned toward the camera. It was the face from the mirror.

"Pier 17, South Street Seaport," Shah said. "Three nights ago. That's you, Mr. Ye?"

Ye Xuan looked at a stranger wearing his face.

He could lie. Say it wasn't him, say he'd lost that hoodie weeks ago. But the camera had a clear angle, and Kroll was watching him with the patient attention of a man who had heard every lie in the world at least twice.

So he told the truth. Part of it.

"I think so," he said. "But I don't remember."

Shah raised an eyebrow. "You don't remember."

"I've been sick for three days. Fever, nosebleeds." He gestured at his face—the hollow cheeks, the shadows. That part, at least, needed no acting. "I woke up this morning and I can't remember the last week clearly. I remember… water. Black water. And a smell, like something burning. That's all."

Kroll and Shah exchanged a look.

"What kind of burning?" Kroll asked quietly.

Ye Xuan searched the scattered fragments in his head. The pier. The black water. The smell—not wood, not plastic. Something sharper. Like the air after a lightning strike.

"Like a thunderstorm," he said. "Ozone."

Kroll set down his coffee.

"At three fifty-two that morning," he said, "eleven minutes after this picture was taken, a man died on that pier. His name was Silas Crane. He was an unregistered Awakened—an energy type, we think. He didn't drown. He wasn't shot. He burned. From the inside out." Kroll's voice didn't change at all. "There was a flash of white light, bright enough that three people across the river called 911, thinking a transformer had exploded. When our people arrived, Crane was dead, and the whole pier smelled like ozone."

The bakery was very quiet. Even the radio seemed to have turned itself down.

"Your phone," Shah added, "was connected to a cell tower two blocks from the scene until four ten. Then it went dark. No calls, no messages, no data. Until"—she glanced at her screen—"about forty minutes ago."

When he had unlocked it.

Ye Xuan thought of the bell beneath the world. Of the shreds of memory: black water, the smell of a storm. Of the previous owner of this body, who had come home from that pier, lain down in the narrow bed above the bakery, and never woken up.

*Did he see it happen?* he wondered. *Did whatever killed Crane kill him too—slowly, over three days?*

"I didn't hurt anyone," he said. "I don't think I could. Look at me."

"We know," Kroll said, surprising him. "Crane was killed by something much stronger than a hungry engineering student. We're not here to arrest you, Mr. Ye. We're here because you might be a witness. And because people who are present when an Awakened dies sometimes…" He paused, choosing the word. "Change."

"Change," Ye Xuan repeated.

"Exposure events. They're rare, but they happen. Fevers, nosebleeds, memory loss." Kroll's eyes never left his face. "Sometimes the person wakes up different. Stronger. Able to do things they couldn't do before."

Ye Xuan made himself pick up an egg tart and take a bite. It was still warm; the custard trembled. It was—somewhat unfairly, given the circumstances—the best thing he had ever tasted.

"If I could do something different," he said with his mouth full, "do you think I'd be eating breakfast on forty-three dollars?"

Shah's lips twitched. Kroll didn't smile, but something in his heavy face eased a little.

He slid a card across the table. Plain white, a phone number, the Bureau's eagle in the corner.

"If you remember anything," Kroll said, "or if you start feeling… different—call me. Day or night. The Registration Act will pass in a month, maybe two. After that, an unregistered Awakened is a criminal. Before that, he's just a kid who needs help." He stood, and the floor groaned again. "It's better to come to us than to wait for us to come to you."

Shah tucked the tablet under her arm. At the door, Kroll stopped.

"One more thing, Mr. Ye," he said, without turning around. "Crane always carried something with him. It wasn't on his body when we found him. We assume whoever killed him took it." He glanced back, and his eyes were very serious. "But if by any chance it's in your possession, then the people looking for it are much more dangerous than we are. And much less polite."

The bell over the door jingled. The gray suits disappeared into the crowd on Mott Street.

* * *

Ye Xuan sat at the table for a long time, looking at the white card.

"You didn't tell them everything," Mrs. Tang said from behind the counter. It wasn't a question.

"I told them everything I remember."

She studied him over her reading glasses. Then she put two more egg tarts into a paper bag, folded the top over twice, and pushed it across the counter.

"For the ghost," she said. "Friday."

* * *

Back in his room, he locked the door and stood in the middle of the floor.

*Crane always carried something with him.*

The previous owner had come home from the pier at four in the morning. Where would he have put things? Ye Xuan checked the desk drawers: pens, receipts, a broken calculator. The bed: nothing under the mattress. The mini-fridge: two eggs, a jar of chili oil, and a deep sense of shame.

Then he noticed the jacket.

It hung on a hook behind the door—a black windbreaker, still faintly damp at the cuffs even after three days. When he lifted it, he caught the smell: salt water, and under it, very faint, the sharp, clean scent of a thunderstorm.

Something heavy swung in the inner pocket.

He took it out.

It was a stone. Smooth and gray, the size of a quail's egg, so ordinary that he would have stepped over it on any beach in the world. But it was warm—warmer than his hand—and when he held it up to the window, he could see a thin vein of white light deep inside it. Not a reflection. A light of its own, like a tiny piece of that night sky twelve years ago, frozen in rock.

Ye Xuan didn't decide to look. His eyes simply focused, the way they had on the phone, on the badge, on Kroll.

The world shifted.

And he was falling.

Not lines this time—an ocean of them. Threads of white fire, too many to count, folded into one another in patterns that kept unfolding the longer he looked: spirals inside spirals, rivers inside a single drop. He understood one thread, and it led to a hundred more. He understood a hundred, and there were ten thousand behind them. It was like trying to read a library by opening every book at once.

*Too much—*

He tore his eyes away and hit the floor on his knees. Blood poured from his nose and spattered the boards. His head rang like the bell beneath the world.

For a full minute, he couldn't move. He knelt there, breathing, waiting for the room to stop spinning.

Then, in his clenched fist, the stone pulsed.

Once. Slowly. Warmly.

Like a heartbeat answering his own.
