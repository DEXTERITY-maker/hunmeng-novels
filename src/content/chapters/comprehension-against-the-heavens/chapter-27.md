Red Hook was the end of the world.

That was how it felt, at least, at ten o'clock on Saturday night. The subway didn't go there. They took a bus from Borough Hall and then walked, through streets of low brick warehouses and chain-link fences and cobblestones that shone under the few working streetlights. The wind came off the harbor smelling of salt and rust. Somewhere, a dog was barking at nothing.

Tank walked in front. Ye Xuan in the middle. Juno behind, her hands in the pockets of her red jacket, her eyes moving over every rooftop.

"There," said Tank.

It was a warehouse like all the others: long, low, brick, with a row of boarded-up windows and a loading dock facing the water. A faded painted sign on the side read SULLIVAN SHIPPING & STORAGE. No lights. No sound.

Except that, when Ye Xuan let his sight open, the warehouse was *full*.

Gifts. Dozens of them, maybe a hundred, all crowded together somewhere below ground level—a tangle of patterns so dense and bright it looked like the inside of a firework factory. Chains and coils and cables, sparks and embers and threads of cold blue light, all of it stirring, shifting, heating up.

He closed his sight again quickly. Even at the third layer, looking at that many gifts at once made his head throb.

"Lot of people tonight," he said.

Tank looked at him curiously. "How do you know?"

"Lucky guess."

* * *

The door was at the back, beside the loading dock. A steel door with no handle on the outside. Tank knocked: three times, a pause, two more.

A slot opened. A pair of eyes looked out.

"Bell," said Tank. "Marcus Bell. Dez's cousin. I brought a fighter."

The eyes looked at Ye Xuan. Then at Juno.

"She fighting too?"

"No," said Juno.

"Then she pays twenty to watch."

Juno paid twenty. The slot closed. Bolts slid back. The door opened.

Behind it stood a man who was almost as big as Tank, bald, with tattoos running up both sides of his neck. He didn't say anything. He jerked his head toward the inside.

The inside was a freight elevator.

It was old—a cage of steel bars with a wooden floor, big enough to hold a truck—and it went down. And down. And as it went, the sound came up to meet them.

At first it was a hum. Then a roar. By the time the elevator shuddered to a stop at the bottom of the shaft, it was a wall of noise: shouting, stamping, cheering, a bass line thumping from somewhere, and under it all, a sharp crack-crack-crack like lightning striking concrete.

The elevator gate rattled open.

* * *

The Pit was well named.

It was a basement—an enormous one, as big as a soccer field, with a low ceiling held up by thick concrete pillars. Strings of work lights hung between the pillars, bare bulbs throwing hard yellow light. In the center of the room, the floor dropped away: a square depression, maybe twenty meters on each side and three meters deep, with concrete walls and a concrete floor. Maybe it had once been a loading pool, or a tank for something. Now it was a ring.

People crowded around its edges, three and four deep. Hundreds of them. They leaned over the railings, shouting, waving fistfuls of cash. Some of them, Ye Xuan saw at a glance, were Awakened—gifts flickering in their bodies like pilot lights. Most weren't. They were just people. Dockworkers, office workers, students, men in expensive suits trying not to look like they were in expensive suits. Bookies with notebooks moved through the crowd, taking bets.

Down in the pit, two fighters circled each other.

One was a woman with crackling blue-white sparks dancing along her arms—an electric gift, like Crane's but cruder, rougher. The other was a man whose skin had gone the color and texture of tree bark. The woman threw out her hand, and a jagged arc leaped across the pit and struck the bark-man in the chest with a crack that Ye Xuan felt in his teeth. The bark-man staggered, smoking, and kept coming.

The crowd roared.

"Welcome," Juno said dryly, "to the worst idea you've ever had."

* * *

Mama Rocha's office was a shipping container.

It sat in the far corner of the basement, beyond the pit, painted bright orange, with a window cut into one side and a door guarded by two more bald men with neck tattoos. Inside, it was surprisingly comfortable: a carpet, a desk, a little refrigerator, a fan, a framed photograph of a beach somewhere warm, and a small altar with a statue of a saint and three candles burning in front of it.

Mama Rocha sat behind the desk.

She was in her fifties, heavy, with dark curly hair streaked with gray and gold hoops in her ears. She wore a bright yellow dress and reading glasses on a chain around her neck. She looked, Ye Xuan thought, like somebody's favorite aunt—the kind who fed you too much at family dinners and asked when you were getting married.

Her eyes did not look like somebody's favorite aunt's.

"Marcus Bell," she said warmly, in a slight Portuguese accent. "Dez's little cousin. Come in, come in. How is your mother?"

"She's good, Mama. She says hi."

"Tell her I said hi back." Mama Rocha's eyes moved past Tank to Ye Xuan. They rested on him with friendly, bottomless interest. "And this is the fighter."

"Yes, ma'am," said Ye Xuan.

"Sit."

He sat in the chair across from the desk. Tank and Juno stood behind him.

Mama Rocha took a form from a drawer—an actual paper form, with boxes—and uncapped a pen.

"Name," she said. "Not your real name. I don't want your real name. A fighting name. What do they call you?"

He hadn't thought about this. He opened his mouth to say *Ye*, and then thought better of it.

"Mott," he said. "Like the street."

"Mott." She wrote it down. "Age?"

"Nineteen."

"Gift?"

"I don't have one."

Mama Rocha's pen stopped.

She looked up at him over the top of the form. For a long moment, she said nothing at all. She just looked—and Ye Xuan, without meaning to, let his sight skim across her.

Her gift was in her mouth. On her tongue, specifically: a tight, delicate cluster of threads, pale gold, like taste buds made of light. And as he watched, the threads stirred, as if *tasting* something in the air. Tasting him.

*She can taste lies,* he understood. *That's her gift. When someone lies to her, she tastes it.*

The threads on her tongue stirred, tasted, and went still.

Mama Rocha's eyebrows rose very slowly.

"No gift," she repeated.

"No gift."

"Hm." She put down the pen. She folded her hands on the desk. "You know what I am, Mott?"

"I think you can tell when people lie."

The eyebrows went higher. "Who told you that?"

"Nobody."

The threads on her tongue stirred again. Tasted. Went still.

"Nobody," she repeated softly. "And that's true too." She leaned back in her chair and looked at him with an expression he couldn't read—part amusement, part caution, part something like hunger. "Twenty years I've had this gift. Since the Glow. I've sat at this desk and heard ten thousand fighters tell me ten thousand stories, and I know every one of them that was true and every one that wasn't. When people lie to me, it tastes like rust. Old pennies." She smiled slightly. "When you tell me you have no gift, it tastes like water. Clean water. You believe it completely. And you're right."

"I am."

"But you walk into my Pit, and you know what I am without anybody telling you. And you look at me like—" She paused. "Like you're reading the menu."

Ye Xuan said nothing. Behind him, he felt Juno go very still.

Mama Rocha watched him for another long moment. Then she laughed—a big, warm, startling laugh that filled the shipping container.

"Okay, Mott," she said. "Okay. I don't need to know what you are. In my Pit, I only need to know three things. One: you don't use weapons. Two: you don't kill anybody. Three: when I say it's over, it's over." She picked up the pen again and wrote something on the form. "First fight, five hundred if you win. Nothing if you lose. Bookies will give odds against you, because you're new and you're small and you've got no gift. Don't take it personally."

"I won't."

"Good." She tore off a slip of paper with a number on it—*14*—and handed it to him. "You're fourth tonight. Go stand by the east wall and wait for your number. And Mott?"

He looked up.

Mama Rocha had stopped smiling.

"There are people in my Pit tonight," she said quietly, "who come to watch. Not to bet. Not to fight. To *watch*. They wear nice jackets and they don't cheer and they take notes. Sometimes they take pictures. They're looking for something." She tapped the form. "If you're something special—and I think you might be—then you be careful what you show them. Understand?"

"Who are they?"

Mama Rocha looked at him for a moment. Then she glanced at the little altar with the saint and the three candles, and back.

"In this city?" she said. "Who do you think is shopping for gifts?"

He thought of a golden sunburst on a white wall.

"I understand," he said.

* * *

They stood by the east wall and waited.

The first fight ended when the bark-man finally caught the electric woman and threw her into the concrete wall of the pit; she didn't get up, and Mama Rocha's voice came over a crackling speaker—*It's over*—and two men with a stretcher climbed down to collect her. The second fight was two men with strength gifts who traded punches like trucks colliding until one of them went down. The third fight was short and ugly and Ye Xuan didn't watch most of it.

Juno stood beside him, her shoulder touching his. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to.

Tank went to find Dez and came back looking worried.

"Dez says the guy you're fighting is called Bricks," he said. "He's fought here a lot. He's won seven of his last ten."

"What's his gift?"

"Dez says it's like… his bones are heavy. Really heavy. Like they're made of concrete." Tank looked unhappy. "When he hits people, they don't get up."

Ye Xuan nodded slowly.

Across the room, by the far railing, he noticed a man in a dark green fleece jacket. Thirty, maybe. Clean-shaven, short hair, an expensive watch. He wasn't cheering. He wasn't holding cash. He was standing very still, watching the pit, and every so often he wrote something on his phone.

On the collar of his fleece, so small it was almost invisible, a tiny golden pin caught the light of the work lamps.

A sunburst.

The speaker crackled.

"*Number fourteen,*" said Mama Rocha's voice. "*Mott. To the pit.*"
