Mama Rocha sent for him before he had finished counting the money.

The shipping container was quieter now. The door was closed, and the noise of the pit came through the walls as a dull, distant roar. The candles in front of the saint had burned down to stubs. Mama Rocha sat behind her desk with her reading glasses on, looking at something on a tablet.

She turned it around so he could see.

It was the fight. Filmed from above, slightly shaky—one of her own cameras, he guessed, mounted on a pillar. Bricks throwing his hook. Ye Xuan stepping inside it. The kick to the knee. Bricks going down.

She played it again in slow motion. Stopped it at the moment his foot touched Bricks's knee.

"Fifteen years Bricks has fought in my Pit," she said. "Seventy-three fights. You know how many times somebody put him on the ground?"

"I don't know."

"Four. And every one of those four was somebody with a strength gift bigger than his. Somebody who could take the hit and hit back harder." She tapped the frozen frame. "You didn't hit him hard. Look. You barely touched him. And his leg just—" She made a gesture with her hand, a slipping motion. "*Gave up.*"

"His knees are bad."

"His knees have been bad for five years. Everybody knows his knees are bad. Fifty fighters have kicked him in the knees." She took off her reading glasses and looked at him. "It didn't work for them."

Ye Xuan didn't say anything.

The pale gold threads on Mama Rocha's tongue stirred, as if waiting to taste whatever he said next. He didn't give them anything.

After a while, she sighed.

"Okay," she said. "I told you I don't need to know what you are. I meant it. In my business, it's better not to know some things." She put the glasses back on. "But I'm going to ask you one question, and I want you to answer it, because I'm going to taste the answer either way. Okay?"

"Okay."

"Are you working for anybody?"

"No."

The threads tasted. Went still. Water.

"Not the Bureau? Not Halcyon? Not some gang from the Bronx who wants to take over my business?"

"No. Nobody. I need money for tuition."

Mama Rocha stared at him. Then she burst out laughing.

"Tuition!" She slapped the desk. "Tuition. *Deus me livre.* Fifteen years, I hear every reason. Debts. Drugs. Revenge. Pride. A sick mother. A sick kid. A girlfriend who wants a ring." She wiped her eyes. "First time somebody walks into my Pit and fights Bricks for *tuition*."

* * *

When she had finished laughing, she opened a drawer and took out a ledger—an actual paper ledger, bound in green leather.

"Every Saturday," she said. "You want a slot, you have a slot. Five hundred a win. After three wins, it goes up. After five, it goes up again. And if you keep winning…" She tapped the ledger. "Once a month, I do a big fight. Iron Night. Special card. Big crowd, big bets. The winner of Iron Night gets five thousand."

Five thousand. Ye Xuan did the arithmetic without meaning to. Five hundred tonight. Two more Saturdays at five hundred, maybe more. Iron Night. Deliveries. It was close. It was almost—

"But," said Mama Rocha.

He looked up.

"The man in the green jacket." She wasn't smiling now. "You saw him."

"I saw him."

"He came to me after your fight. Very polite. Nice watch. Asked me who you were. Asked how long you've been fighting, who trained you, what's your gift." She folded her hands on the ledger. "I told him the truth. I don't know anything. You're new. You said you have no gift."

"Did he believe you?"

"He believed I was telling the truth." She shrugged. "Then he gave me this."

She slid a small white card across the desk. It was thick, expensive, embossed. A golden sunburst in the corner. Below it:

**HALCYON DYNAMICS**
**Talent Partnerships**
*Daniel Avery — Senior Scout*

On the back, in neat handwriting: *Please pass along. We'd love to talk.*

Ye Xuan didn't touch it.

"They come every few weeks," Mama Rocha said quietly. "Halcyon. Scouts. They watch the fights, they find the good ones, they make offers. Training contracts. Medical care. Money—a lot of money. Some of my best fighters went with them over the years." She paused. "Some of them I saw on television later, wearing those gold suits, flying around, saving people. Zenith Initiative."

"And the others?"

"The others, I didn't see again."

She pushed the card a little closer to him.

"I'm giving it to you because I promised I would. What you do with it is your business." She sat back. "But if it was me, Mott, and I was a kid who didn't want people to know what he was? I'd be very careful going home tonight."

* * *

Juno and Tank were waiting by the freight elevator. Dez had gone; his own fight was later, and he had to warm up.

"Well?" said Juno.

"I have a slot," Ye Xuan said. "Every Saturday."

Juno's face did several things at once.

"And there's a man from Halcyon who wants to talk to me."

Her face stopped doing several things and settled on one.

"Where is he?"

Ye Xuan let his sight skim across the basement. The crowd, the pit, the hundred gifts flickering like pilot lights. He didn't see the green fleece jacket. He didn't see the little golden sunburst.

"I don't know," he said. "Gone."

"Gone where?"

"I don't know."

The elevator gate rattled open. They got in. The bald man with the neck tattoos pulled the lever, and they rose slowly up the shaft, the noise of the pit fading below them.

At the top, the steel door opened onto the night. Cold wind off the harbor. Cobblestones. The empty street, the boarded-up warehouses, the distant orange glow of the Manhattan skyline across the water.

And, about fifty meters away, parked under the only working streetlight on the block, a dark gray SUV with tinted windows.

Its engine was running.

* * *

"Don't look at it," Juno said quietly. "Keep walking."

They kept walking. Toward the bus stop, the way they had come. The cobblestones were uneven under their feet. Their footsteps echoed off the brick walls.

Behind them, the SUV's engine changed pitch. Tires crunched slowly on the cobblestones.

It was following them.

Ye Xuan let his sight open—just a skim, cheap, the third layer carrying it. Two people in the SUV. One was ordinary, a faint human glow. The other was not. Something thin and humming, coiled in the man's ears and throat, like a tuning fork made of light. A sound gift. Like Wren's, but different—not listening. Sending.

*Sonic,* he thought. *He can do something with sound.*

"Two of them," he murmured. "One has a gift. Something with sound."

Juno didn't ask how he knew.

"Tank," she said. "Bus stop's four blocks. Can you get there?"

"I can get anywhere," said Tank simply.

"Then go. Walk normally. Get on the bus. Text Wren when you're on." She glanced at Ye Xuan. "We're going up."

Tank looked at the two of them. Then he nodded once and turned down a side street, walking at an ordinary pace, his huge shoulders relaxed, his hands in his hoodie pocket. The SUV didn't follow him. It kept rolling slowly behind Ye Xuan and Juno.

"Can you climb?" Juno said.

"I can climb."

"Fire escape, left side, next building. When I say go."

They walked. Ten meters. Twenty. The SUV crept behind them, its headlights off now, just the engine growling.

"Go," said Juno.

They ran.

* * *

The fire escape was old and rusted and bolted to the side of a four-story warehouse. Juno didn't use it. She went up the wall beside it in two jumps—one, two, three, *four*, a push off the brick, a push off the air—and was on the roof before Ye Xuan had reached the first landing.

He climbed. Hand over hand, rung by rung, the way she had taught him on Rivington Street. Behind him, he heard the SUV's tires screech on the cobblestones. A door opened.

Then a sound hit him.

Not a loud sound. A *wrong* sound—a high, thin whine that went straight through his ears and into the middle of his head like a needle. His vision blurred. His hands went numb on the rungs. He felt his grip slipping.

*The sonic gift.*

He didn't think. He breathed. One, two, three—and on *fold*, he pulled the qi in his dantian up, not into his legs this time, but into his head, his ears, wrapping it around the inside of his skull like a scarf around a cold neck.

The whine dulled. Just enough.

He kept climbing.

Hands caught his wrists from above. Juno, lying flat on the edge of the roof, hauled him up the last two meters and over the parapet. They rolled onto the tar together.

"Can you run?" she said.

"I can run."

"Then *run*."

They ran across the roofs of Red Hook in the dark. Warehouse to warehouse. Juno jumping, him following—some gaps he could step across, some he jumped with a thread of qi and the four-count, some she pulled him across by the hand. Below them, the SUV's engine roared as it tried to follow along the streets. Then the streets ran out—they came to the end of a pier, to a rooftop over the water with nothing beyond it but the harbor—and Juno pulled him down behind a big rusted ventilation unit and put her hand over his mouth.

They waited.

The SUV's engine came closer. Stopped. Doors opened. Footsteps on the street below. Voices—too far away to hear words.

Then the doors closed. The engine started. And the sound of it faded away, back toward the city, until there was only the wind and the water and their own breathing.

Juno took her hand off his mouth.

They sat with their backs against the ventilation unit, looking out at the harbor. The Statue of Liberty stood in the distance, small and green and lit up, holding her torch over the black water.

"So," Juno said finally. "That was Halcyon."

"I think so."

"After one fight."

"After one fight."

She was silent for a while.

"You still want to do this?" she said.

Ye Xuan thought about it. The envelope with five hundred dollars was in his jacket, next to the tin with the stone. Twenty-five days until the tuition deadline. Three more Saturdays. Iron Night.

And a man in a green fleece jacket with a recording on his phone.

"I don't have a choice," he said.

Juno looked at him. Then she leaned her head back against the rusted metal and closed her eyes.

"Yeah," she said quietly. "That's what I was afraid of."
