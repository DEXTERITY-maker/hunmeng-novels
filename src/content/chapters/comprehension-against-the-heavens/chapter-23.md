He called the number on the napkin at two in the morning, sitting on his bed in dry clothes, with the copper box on the desk and Juno's granola bar half-eaten in his hand.

Shah answered on the first ring. She did not sound like she had been asleep.

"Mr. Ye."

"You said to call when I was ready to stop lying."

A pause. He heard keys clicking in the background—a keyboard, typed fast.

"Are you?" she said.

"Some of it."

"Some of it is a start." The keys stopped. "There's a diner on the Bowery, at Third Street. The Sunrise. Open all night. Can you be there in twenty minutes?"

He looked at the window. The curtain was drawn. He had checked the street before he called: no black sedan tonight. But a drone had watched them on the pier, and someone had seen what the drone saw.

"Thirty," he said. "I'll take the long way."

"Smart," said Shah, and hung up.

* * *

The Sunrise Diner was the kind of place that only existed in New York: a long narrow room with a counter, red vinyl booths, a pie case lit like a museum exhibit, and a waitress who looked like she had been working the night shift since the Glow. At half past two in the morning, there were four customers: two cab drivers eating eggs, a drunk man asleep in a booth, and Priya Shah in the last booth at the back, facing the door, with a laptop open in front of her and a cup of coffee going cold beside it.

She was wearing the green hoodie again. Her hair was pulled back in a short, messy ponytail. She looked tired and very awake at the same time.

Ye Xuan slid into the booth across from her.

"You look like hell," she said.

"I went swimming."

She didn't ask. She waved at the waitress, who came over and poured him coffee without being asked and left a menu he didn't open.

"Talk," Shah said.

He had decided on the way how much to tell her. He had walked the long way—up the Bowery, around through the East Village, down Second Avenue—turning it over and over. Rule seven: *nobody learns about the stone.* But Crane had died for that stone, and the other Ye Xuan had died because of it, and he couldn't do this alone. He needed someone who knew how the world worked. Someone with access.

So he told her about the pier.

Not about the sight. Not about the echoes in the wood. He told her that he had gone back to Pier 17 because he wanted to remember, and that standing at the end of the pier, he had remembered. Some of it. Crane. The box. Three people coming down the pier. Crane pushing him toward the ladder. The flash.

And then he took the copper box out of his jacket and put it on the table between them.

* * *

Shah looked at the box for a long time without touching it.

"You built this," she said.

"I don't remember building it. But my notes say I did. Crane paid me to. He wanted something that would hide what he was carrying."

"From what? Radiation detectors?"

"From people who could feel it."

Shah's eyes came up to his face. "What was he carrying?"

Ye Xuan wrapped his hands around the coffee cup.

"Before he died," he said carefully, "Crane said something. I remembered it last night. *Seed Zero. Don't let them plant it.*"

The effect on Shah was immediate.

She went completely still. Her hand, halfway to her coffee cup, stopped in midair. Then she pulled the laptop toward her and began to type, very fast, without looking at him.

"Say it again," she said.

"Seed Zero. Don't let them plant it."

"*Seed Zero.*" She typed something, hit enter, waited, typed again. "I've seen that. I've seen that somewhere. Two years ago—no, two and a half—there was a whistleblower forum, one of the dark ones, where people leak corporate stuff. Somebody posted a document from inside Halcyon. It was up for about six hours before it got taken down. The Bureau's monitoring team flagged it, but it got buried, nobody followed up—" She stopped typing. "Here."

She turned the laptop around.

* * *

It was a photograph of a printed page. Slightly blurry, taken at an angle, as if with a phone held under a desk. The paper had a golden sunburst at the top and the words **HALCYON DYNAMICS — INTERNAL — RESTRICTED**.

Below it:

**PROJECT ASCENSION — PHASE II SUMMARY**

*Cohort size: 64. Integration of PLZ fragments (Class B–D) via subdermal implantation, thoracic region.*

*Gift manifestation: 41 of 64 (64%).*

*Stable at 12 months: 9 of 64 (14%).*

*Adverse outcomes: see Appendix C (restricted).*

*Conclusion: Fragment-based integration produces unstable, short-lived results due to structural degradation of fragments. Phase III requires a stable integration seed. Recommend acceleration of PLZ-0 recovery and analysis. Without PLZ-0 ("Seed Zero"), sustainable Ascension remains theoretical.*

Ye Xuan read it twice.

His coffee had gone cold in his hands.

"PLZ," he said. "Pacific Luminous Zone."

"Where the Glow was strongest. Near the epicenter. Halcyon has had exclusive mining rights there for ten years." Shah's voice was very quiet. "That's where they get the crystals. Glow crystals. The things they put in their museums and their lobbies and their—" She tapped the screen. "*Subdermal implantation, thoracic region.* They put them in people's chests, Ye."

He thought of the hard gray point inside Ash's embers, right against his heart. Something that didn't belong. Something *put there*.

"Sixty-four people," he said.

"Forty-one got gifts. Nine were still stable after a year." Shah's jaw was tight. "What do you think happened to the other fifty-five?"

He didn't answer. *Adverse outcomes. See Appendix C.*

"Crane was one of them," Shah said. "Had to be. The Gift Enhancement Study was the public name. He was already Awakened when he went in—they must have wanted to see what a fragment would do to a natural gift. And then he walked out after eight months, and four years later he's dead on a pier with his gift burned through, and the Director closes his case in forty-six hours." She sat back. "And somewhere in there, he got his hands on something called Seed Zero."

* * *

The waitress came by and refilled their coffees. Neither of them drank.

When she was gone, Shah leaned forward and spoke very quietly.

"PLZ-0," she said. "Zero. The first one. Every other crystal in that memo has a class—B, C, D. Fragments. Pieces. But this one's just zero." She looked at him. "I think it's not a fragment. I think it's the thing the fragments are *from*. Or the thing they're trying to be. The original. And they lost it. Somehow Crane took it from them. And they've been looking for it for four years."

*They'll open the—* Crane had thought at the very end.

Open what?

"Mr. Ye," Shah said. "I'm going to ask you once, and I want you to think about the answer. Do you have Seed Zero?"

He thought about it.

He thought about the stone in its sock in the mint tin, in his jacket, pressed against his ribs right now. Warm. Breathing its slow forty-second breaths. He thought about Crane's last thought, burned into the wood. *Run, kid.* He thought about sixty-four people with stones in their chests.

"If I did," he said slowly, "what would you do?"

Shah held his gaze for a long time.

"If you did," she said, "I'd tell you that you can't give it to the Bureau, because someone inside the Bureau closed Crane's case in two days, and I don't know who. I'd tell you not to give it to anyone. I'd tell you to never, ever let it near a Halcyon building." She paused. "And I'd tell you that I'm sorry. Because if they know you have it, they're not going to stop."

"And if I didn't?"

"Then I'd tell you to be careful anyway. Because they *think* you do."

She closed the laptop.

* * *

They left separately. Shah first, out the front, hood up, walking north. Ye Xuan ten minutes later, out the back, through a kitchen where a cook was smoking by the open door and didn't even look up.

He walked home the long way again. The streets were empty, wet, shining under the streetlights. A garbage truck growled somewhere. The sky was beginning to turn gray in the east.

He thought about the memo.

*Without Seed Zero, sustainable Ascension remains theoretical.*

*Ascension for everyone,* said the billboards. *Ascension for everyone,* the receptionist in the Halcyon lobby had said, with her painted smile.

He thought of Ash, embers wrapped around a nail in his heart. Of the crystals in the glass column, turning toward his stone like faces toward a sound. Of Crane, frayed and burning, pressing the stone into a frightened boy's hand.

*It's not a fragment,* Shah had said. *It's the original.*

He touched his jacket over the tin. The stone was warm under his hand. In. Pause. Out.

"What are you?" he whispered.

The stone didn't answer. But just for a moment, so faintly he might have imagined it, he thought he heard a bell.
