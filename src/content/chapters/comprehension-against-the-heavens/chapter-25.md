"It's called the Pit," Tank said.

They were sitting on the curb outside a bodega on Canal Street, eating beef patties out of paper bags. Tank had bought four. He ate three of them in the time it took Ye Xuan to finish one.

"It's in Red Hook," Tank went on. "In Brooklyn, by the water. There's an old warehouse. Shipping stuff, years ago. Underneath, there's a big basement. Every Saturday night, people fight there." He wiped his fingers carefully on a napkin. "Awakened people."

"For money?"

"For money. Five hundred if you win. More if you win a lot of times in a row. People bet on the fights, too. A lot of money." Tank looked at his hands. "My cousin Dez fights there. He's been doing it two years. He's paying for his mom's house."

"Is it legal?"

Tank gave him a look that said *you're an engineering student, figure it out.*

"Okay," Ye Xuan said. "Not legal."

"The Bureau doesn't go there. Mostly. Mama Rocha—she runs it—she has a deal with somebody. I don't know who." Tank shrugged his enormous shoulders. "There are rules. No weapons. No killing. If you tap out, it's over. If you get knocked out, it's over. If Mama says it's over, it's over. People get hurt. But Dez says it's—" He searched for the word. "Fair. More fair than the street."

Ye Xuan thought about it.

Five hundred a win. Nine thousand four hundred in thirty days. Four Saturdays.

"How many fights in a night?"

"For a new guy? One. Maybe two if the crowd likes you." Tank looked at him seriously. "Ye. Dez is strong. Really strong. His gift is like mine—he gets hard, like rocks. And last year he got his jaw broken in two places. He couldn't eat for six weeks. He drank everything through a straw." He paused. "And Dez says there are people there who are much worse than him. People who don't fight to make money. People who fight because they like it."

* * *

He told Juno the next morning, after practice, sitting on the bench under the locust tree while the grannies packed up.

He had expected her to be angry. He had not expected her to go white.

"No," she said.

"Juno—"

"*No.*" She stood up. Two of the grannies looked over. Juno lowered her voice, but it was shaking. "Tank told you about the Pit? I'm going to kill him. I'm going to kick him off the Manhattan Bridge."

"He was trying to help."

"He doesn't *know*." She was pacing now, back and forth in front of the bench, her sneakers scuffing the concrete. "You know what happens there? People die, Ye. Not always. Not every week. But Mama Rocha's rules are *no killing on purpose*, and a lot of gifts don't care about on purpose. Last year a guy with a sonic gift ruptured another guy's eardrums so bad he bled into his brain. Two years ago, a fire-type burned somebody so badly they had to amputate his arm. There's a girl in the Bronx, used to live on my block, she went to the Pit three times. The third time she came back in a wheelchair."

"I know it's dangerous."

"You *don't* know." She stopped in front of him. Her eyes were bright. "You think it's like the alley. You think you'll look at people and see their weak spots and—poke them, and they'll fall down. But those were *Rocco's Dogs*. They're amateurs. They shake down bakeries. The people at the Pit fight every week. They've been hit by every kind of gift there is. They're *good*, Ye."

"So am I."

"You were bleeding from your eyes two nights ago!"

The grannies had stopped packing. Twelve gray heads were turned toward them. Granny Wu had an expression of deep, delighted interest, like someone watching the most exciting episode of her favorite drama.

Ye Xuan stood up.

"Let's walk," he said.

* * *

They walked down Mulberry Street in silence. The morning delivery trucks were rumbling past. A man was hosing down the sidewalk in front of a restaurant, and the water ran in the gutter, catching the sunlight.

"I don't want to do it either," Ye Xuan said finally.

Juno didn't look at him.

"I mean it. I'm not—I don't like fighting. I've never been in a fight in my life before Rocco." It was true; in his first life, the most violent thing he'd ever done was argue with a referee during a school basketball game. "I'm scared of it. I'm scared of getting hurt. I'm scared of someone finding out what I can do."

"Then don't."

"Then I go back to China."

Juno stopped walking.

"In thirty days," he said. "Twenty-nine now. I've done the math. Wren's done the math. Lou's done the math. There's no way to make nine thousand dollars by running packages. There's no way to make it doing anything legal. I don't have family here. I don't have savings. I can't ask my parents—" He stopped. "I can't ask my parents."

"There has to be another way."

"If there is, I can't see it." He laughed, a little bitterly. "That's funny, isn't it? I can see the inside of a traffic light. I can see the gift in your legs. And I can't see a way to pay my tuition."

Juno looked at him for a long time.

"If you go back," she said slowly, "what happens to the thing in your pocket?"

He didn't answer.

"It goes with you," she said. "Right? To China. To your family." She looked down at the wet sidewalk. "And whoever was flying that drone—whoever Crane was running from—they follow it."

"Maybe."

"Probably."

"Probably," he admitted.

* * *

They had reached the corner of Mulberry and Canal. Across the street, the red garage door of Skyline was rolling up. Big Lou was standing at the top of the ramp with a cup of coffee, glaring at the morning.

Juno looked at the garage. Then at him.

"My legs don't hurt," she said quietly. "For the first time in six months. Tank's back doesn't hurt. Do you know what that means? Do you know how many people in this city have gifts that are eating them alive? Every Awakened courier, every construction worker, every firefighter who got lucky in the Glow and has been paying for it ever since? And you're the only person who's ever been able to—" She stopped. "If you go back to China, that's gone. All of it."

"I know."

"So I hate this," Juno said. "I want to be really clear. I hate this, and I'm still going to kick Tank off the Manhattan Bridge."

"Okay."

"But if you go," she said, "I'm coming with you."

He stared at her. "To China?"

"To the *Pit*, idiot." Juno shoved his shoulder. "I'm not letting you go to Red Hook alone with Tank as your only backup. Tank's great, but Tank thinks everybody is nice. Somebody needs to watch your back. And somebody needs to drag you out when you start bleeding from your eyes."

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't." She was already walking toward the garage. Over her shoulder, she added: "And if you die, I'm telling the grannies it was your fault."

* * *

He stood on the corner for a moment, watching her go. The red jacket. The swinging braid. Her steps light and even, no trace of the tremor that had been in them a month ago.

Then he took out his phone and opened the notebook app where he had copied his rules.

*1. Do not look at the stone.*

*2. Do not stare at federal agents.*

*3. Understand this ability before it kills me.*

*4. Food.*

*5. Never use it where cameras can see.*

*6. Gather qi every day. No excuses.*

*7. Nobody learns about the stone.*

*8. Call home every Sunday.*

He thought about it, and added:

*9. Don't die in Red Hook.*

It wasn't a very good rule. It was more of a hope.

He put the phone away and crossed the street toward the garage, where Big Lou was already shouting about a box of lab samples that needed to be in Harlem twenty minutes ago.

Four Saturdays. Nine thousand four hundred dollars.

He had a lot of training to do.
