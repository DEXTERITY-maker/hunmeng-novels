The next ten days were the same day, repeated.

Five o'clock: wake up. Five-thirty: Granny Liu's back row, beside the old locust tree, breathing with the grannies until the sun came up over the Manhattan Bridge. Seven: congee with Mrs. Tang, who had started leaving a salted egg on top without being asked. Eight to six: Skyline. Broadway, Sixth, Water Street, Park Avenue, the green waves, the lobbies, the tips. Six-thirty: fractions with Tommy, who had graduated from pizzas to decimals and was furious about it. Eight: copper wire, the mint tin, the notebook. Eleven: bed.

He made five hundred and sixty dollars. He paid Mrs. Tang the rest of the month's rent and half the plumber. Rocco Vitale did not come back. The black sedan did—not every night, but often enough that he stopped thinking it was a coincidence.

And his cultivation stopped moving.

* * *

It wasn't that nothing happened. Every morning by the locust tree, he gathered qi. Every evening in his room, he gathered more, slowly, a thread at a time. His dantian was a smooth, warm jar now—it didn't leak—and every day he filled it to the brim.

But the jar didn't grow.

In the novels, the first layer of Qi Gathering was the easiest. The hero filled his dantian, and when it was full, the qi compressed, the dantian expanded, and he broke through to the second layer. Usually within a week. Usually in a single chapter.

Ye Xuan filled his dantian every morning. And every evening it was exactly the same size.

"It's full," he told Granny Liu on the tenth morning, after practice, walking her back toward Mott Street because she had decided he was going to carry her shopping bags from now on. "Every day it's full. And then nothing happens. It doesn't get… bigger."

Granny Liu walked slowly, leaning on the cane she didn't need.

"My husband said something like that," she said after a while. "When he was young. He said the old books talked about the jar filling, and then the jar breaking, and then a bigger jar." She shrugged. "He never got past the first jar. Nobody in his family did, for three hundred years. He said it was because there wasn't enough in the air to fill it all the way."

"There's enough now."

"Hm." She glanced up at him sideways. "Then maybe the air is not the problem. Maybe the water is not enough. Maybe you need a stronger water."

* * *

*A stronger water.*

He thought about it all day. On Broadway. In the lobby of an investment bank. On the roof of a parking garage on Twelfth Avenue, where he ate a sandwich and watched Juno, three buildings away, sail across a gap between rooftops with her red jacket snapping behind her.

The qi in the city was thin. Even at dawn by the old tree, even at its thickest, it was a fine mist, a pale snow of threads. He could gather it, but it was like trying to fill a bathtub with an eyedropper. The tub filled. But slowly, gently, never with enough force to *push*.

And there was only one source of stronger water that he knew of.

It was sitting in a mint tin in his jacket pocket.

* * *

At three in the morning, he sat cross-legged on his bed and took the tin out.

He had thought about this carefully. The black sedan was not outside tonight; he had checked twice. The window was closed and the curtain drawn. He had set the alarm on his phone, as before, for three seconds.

*Rule one: do not look at the stone,* he reminded himself. *I'm not going to look at it. I'm going to look at its skin. That's different.*

It was a very weak argument. He made it anyway.

He opened the tin, unwrapped the sock, and set the stone on the towel in front of him.

Without the tin around it, the warmth hit him at once—a soft, steady heat, like sitting near a radiator. He felt it in his face, his chest, his dantian. The qi in the jar below his navel stirred, as if it recognized something.

He started the alarm and looked.

* * *

The surface. Only the surface.

The rings were there, as he had seen them before: pale lattices of light spreading out from the stone's skin, slowly, one after another. But this time he wasn't trying to copy their pattern. He was watching their *timing*.

And he saw what he had missed before.

The rings didn't come out in a steady stream. They came in pulses. The stone was breathing. Slowly—much more slowly than a person—but unmistakably. It drew in: the threads of qi in the room drifted toward it, faintly, and vanished into its surface. It paused. Then it breathed out: a ring of pale light expanded from its skin, dense and bright—far denser than the threads it had drawn in—and spread out through the room, the walls, the city, growing thinner and fainter as it went.

In. Pause. Out.

About forty seconds per breath.

*Beep.*

He tore his eyes away. No nosebleed this time—just a faint pressure behind his eyes, like the beginning of a headache. He was getting better at it.

He sat back, breathing hard.

*It breathes in thin qi,* he thought. *And it breathes out thick qi. It's like… a compressor. Or a lung. It takes in the city's air and gives back something stronger.*

*And when it breathes out, for a moment, right next to it, there's a ring of qi ten times denser than anything in Columbus Park.*

A stronger water.

* * *

He didn't look at the stone again. He didn't need to.

He sat with it on the towel in front of him, closed his eyes, and listened—not with his ears, but with the warmth in his dantian. He felt the stone's breath. In: a faint pull, like a draft from an open window. Pause. Out: a soft wave of heat rolling over his skin.

He matched it.

When the stone breathed in, he breathed out, emptying his lungs, making space. When it paused, he paused. And when it breathed out—when the ring of dense, bright qi rolled over him—he breathed *in*, wide and open, the grannies' net cast as wide as he could cast it, and then on the fourth count, *fold*—

The qi poured into him.

Not a thread. A *stream*. Warm and thick and bright, filling his lungs, rushing down through his chest into his dantian like hot tea into a cup. The jar filled in a single breath. And then the next ring came, and he breathed it in too, and the jar was more than full, the qi pressing against its smooth walls from inside, and he remembered Granny Liu's tap on his elbow—*water fills the river*—and he didn't push. He just kept breathing. In with the stone's out. Out with the stone's in.

The pressure built.

It was the same as in the park, that first morning, but stronger and deeper—not a fist in his stomach this time, but something like a tide, rising slowly, steadily, against a seawall. He let it rise. He didn't fight it. Ten breaths. Twenty. Thirty.

On the thirty-third breath, the seawall gave.

It didn't click, the way the first layer had. It *bloomed*. He felt the jar's walls stretch, soften, open outward like a flower unfolding, and the qi that had been pressing against them rushed out into the new space, and the new space was bigger—twice as big—and it filled, and filled, and the warmth spread out from his dantian along paths he had never felt before: up his spine, down his arms, into his hands, his feet, his eyes.

His body *changed*.

He felt it in his muscles first—a deep, pleasant ache, like after a long swim, as if every fiber had been stretched and set back slightly stronger. His bones felt dense. His skin prickled. His heartbeat slowed and deepened, each beat moving more blood than before.

And very faintly, from somewhere beneath the floor, beneath the city, beneath the world, a bell rang.

Just once. So quietly he might have imagined it.

He opened his eyes.

*Qi Gathering. Second layer.*

The stone sat on the towel in front of him, gray and smooth, breathing its slow forty-second breaths. In the lamplight, deep inside it, the vein of white light seemed—for just a moment—slightly brighter than before.

* * *

He wrapped it back in the sock, put it in the tin, and closed the lid with hands that weren't quite steady.

Then he went to the window and moved the edge of the curtain a centimeter, to look at the street.

He had checked twice. The black sedan had not been there.

It was there now.

It was parked in its usual place under the sign of the closed herbal shop, engine off, windows dark. He didn't know when it had arrived. He hadn't heard it.

In the driver's seat, the small orange point of a cigarette glowed, faded, and glowed again.

And as Ye Xuan watched, the driver leaned forward, very slowly, and tilted his head back to look up—past the bakery's sign, past the second floor, straight at the third-floor window where a thin line of lamplight leaked around the edge of the curtain.

Ye Xuan let the curtain fall.
