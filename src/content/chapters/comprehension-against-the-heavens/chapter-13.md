He didn't sleep that night.

He sat at the desk until the window turned gray, the laptop open in front of him, the unfinished draft still on the screen. *But tonight I saw*. The cursor blinked after the last word, patient, as if the other Ye Xuan might come back at any moment to finish the sentence.

*Saw what?*

Crane being killed? Whoever killed him? The stone? A flash of white light over the black water, bright enough that people across the river called 911?

Whatever it was, the boy had come home, started to write to his mother, and stopped. Maybe the fever had already started. Maybe he had been too frightened. Maybe he had just decided to finish it in the morning.

There had been no morning for him.

At half past five, Ye Xuan closed the laptop, washed his face, and went to Columbus Park, because Rule six said *gather qi every day, no excuses*, and because if he sat in that room one more minute he thought he might start screaming.

The grannies took one look at him and didn't make a single joke. Granny Liu put him in the back row, beside the locust tree, and after the practice, without a word, Auntie Feng handed him a thermos of hot ginger tea and made him drink all of it.

* * *

The call came on Wednesday evening, while he was eating noodles at the desk.

The laptop chimed. A video call, from the messaging app that half of China used. The caller's name was 妈.

*Mom.*

Ye Xuan's chopsticks stopped halfway to his mouth.

It was eight in the evening in New York. Eight in the morning in Hangzhou. His mother always called before work—his first mother, in his first life, had always called before work too, from the bus stop, with the traffic roaring behind her—

The laptop chimed again.

He could let it ring. He could send a message—*sorry, in class, will call later*—and buy himself time. Days. Weeks. He could keep this world's Shen Lan at the end of a text message, where she couldn't see his face and he couldn't see hers.

The laptop chimed a third time.

He put down the chopsticks, wiped his mouth, and pressed *answer*.

* * *

She was standing at a bus stop.

Gray morning light, a row of plane trees, the traffic roaring behind her. She was wearing the brown wool coat she had bought three winters ago on Yan'an Road, the one she said was too expensive and wore every day anyway. Her hair was pinned up badly, because she always did it in the elevator. She had a little crease between her eyebrows that deepened when she was worried, and right now it was very deep.

It was his mother.

It was exactly, completely, impossibly his mother.

"*Finally*," Shen Lan said. "Finally! Do you know how many times I've called? Nine. Nine times, Xuan'er. Your father said, he's busy, he's a university student, leave him alone. I said, a university student has fingers. A university student can type two words. Are you sick? You look thin. You look terrible. Are you eating?"

Ye Xuan opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

His throat had closed completely. He was looking at her face on the cracked screen of a dead boy's laptop and he could not make a single sound.

"Xuan'er?" The crease deepened. "Can you hear me? Is the connection bad? Lao Ye, come here, the connection—"

"I can hear you," he managed. His voice came out rough. "Ma. I can hear you."

"Then why don't you say anything?"

"I was sick," he said. It was true, in a way. "I had a fever. For a week. I'm sorry. I couldn't—I didn't answer anyone. I'm better now."

The crease between her eyebrows changed shape. Worry became something else—the fierce, protective anger that mothers aim at illnesses that dare to touch their children.

"A fever! For a week! Alone! Did you go to a doctor?"

"I'm better now."

"That's not what I asked. Did you go to a doctor?"

"…No."

"Aiya." She pressed her hand to her forehead. Behind her, a bus pulled up and she waved it away without looking. "Lao Ye! Your son had a fever for a week and didn't go to the doctor!"

Another face appeared at the edge of the screen. Older. Glasses. A gray cardigan with a coffee stain on the pocket. His father had always had a coffee stain on the pocket of that cardigan. His mother had tried to wash it out a hundred times.

"Xuan," said Ye Mingyuan. "You're alive."

"I'm alive, Ba."

"Good." His father nodded, as if this settled an engineering problem. "Your mother was worried."

"*I* was worried? You were the one who checked the news every hour—"

"I was checking the weather."

"In New York? Why do you need the weather in New York?"

"To know if it was raining on him."

They bickered. They always bickered—gently, constantly, like two old trees rubbing branches in the wind. Ye Xuan sat in front of the laptop and listened, and somewhere in the middle of it he realized that his face was wet.

He wiped it quickly. Too late.

His mother had gone quiet. She was looking at him through the screen, through twelve thousand kilometers and one impossible gap between worlds, and the crease between her eyebrows had gone soft.

"Xuan'er," she said gently. "What's wrong?"

*I died,* he thought. *I died in the rain on Tianmushan Road and I'll never see you again, the real you, the one who lost me. And your son died too, in this world, in a bed above a bakery, and you don't know. And I'm wearing his face. And I'm so sorry. And I'm so happy to see you that it hurts.*

"Nothing," he said. "I just—I missed you. Both of you. That's all. I was sick and alone and I missed home."

His mother's eyes filled with tears too, instantly, the way they always did.

"Silly boy," she said. "Silly, silly boy. Come home at New Year. We'll buy the ticket. Your father will pay."

"I'll pay," said his father.

"I just said you'll pay."

"I'm agreeing with you."

Ye Xuan laughed, and it came out half a sob, and his mother laughed too, wiping her eyes with the sleeve of the brown coat.

* * *

They talked for forty minutes. His mother missed two buses. She made him show her his room through the camera—"So small! Is that a water stain? Tell the landlady!"—and his noodles—"Only noodles? I told you, not only noodles!"—and made him promise to buy the winter coat, a good one, because cheap coats were expensive in the end.

He told them he had a part-time job now, delivering packages. His father frowned. "Don't let it hurt your studies." He promised it wouldn't. He told them the landlady fed him. His mother wanted the landlady's name so she could send her tea from Hangzhou. He told them about Tommy and the fractions and the pizza drawings, and his father, who had taught Ye Xuan fractions with the same pizza drawings twenty years ago in another world, nodded slowly, as if he recognized something without knowing what.

He did not tell them about the pier, or the stone, or the men in gray suits, or Rocco Vitale. He did not tell them that the sky over New York was full of threads of light, or that he had opened his dantian in a park full of old women.

When his mother finally had to go—"I'm late, I'm so late, the principal will kill me"—she leaned close to the camera until her face filled the whole screen.

"Answer your phone," she said. "Every Sunday. Promise."

"I promise."

"And eat."

"I'll eat."

She looked at him for a moment longer. Then she touched the screen with two fingers, the way she used to touch his forehead when he was small and had a fever, and the call ended.

* * *

The room was very quiet.

Ye Xuan sat in front of the dark screen for a long time. Then he took out the notebook with his rules in it, opened it to the page, and picked up a pen.

He looked at the seven rules. *Do not look at the stone. Do not stare at federal agents. Understand this ability before it kills me. Food. Never use it where cameras can see. Gather qi every day. Nobody learns about the stone.*

He added:

*8. Call home every Sunday.*

He thought about it for a while. Then he wrote something below the list, not numbered, because it wasn't a rule. It was more like a promise.

*I will live this life properly. For both of us.*

*I will study. I will pay the rent. I will call my parents.*

*And I will find out what happened at Pier 17.*

He underlined the last line twice.

* * *

Before bed, he opened the laptop one more time—not the email, not the messages. The folder of coursework. He wanted to know what kind of student the other Ye Xuan had been. What he would be expected to know when he went back to class.

Lab notebooks. Neat. Careful. A good student—not brilliant, but thorough, the kind who double-checked his calculations. Ye Xuan recognized the type. He had been the same kind of student, before.

On the last page of the last lab notebook, under a table of measurements from an experiment on copper conductivity, the other Ye Xuan had written a note to himself. In Chinese. In very small letters, as if he hadn't wanted anyone else to see.

*Crane's stone. Do not look at it directly for too long.*

*When I look at it too long, I hear a bell.*
