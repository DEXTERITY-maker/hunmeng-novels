By Friday afternoon, Ye Xuan had made two hundred and twelve dollars in tips.

He counted it twice in the corner of the Skyline garage, smoothing the bills on his knee. Plus the cash from Big Lou for deliveries, minus what he had spent on food, copper wire and one emergency pair of socks after a delivery truck drove through a puddle on Houston Street.

Three hundred and eighty-four dollars.

It was more money than he had ever earned in his first life. In his first life, he had earned nothing; he had studied, and his parents had paid for everything, and he had never once thought about rent.

It was also not nine hundred and fifty.

* * *

He came back to Mott Street at half past six. The Golden Lotus was in its evening rush—people picking up bread for tomorrow, a line of office workers buying egg tarts on the way to the subway. Mrs. Tang moved behind the counter like a general commanding a small, flour-covered army: one hand taking money, one hand bagging buns, eyes everywhere.

And at the small table by the window, where Kroll and Shah had sat three days ago, a boy was doing homework.

He was about twelve, with a round face, glasses held together at one hinge with tape, and a T-shirt with a picture of a man flying over a city, arms spread, a gold sunburst on his chest. The same man from the newspaper. ZENITH PRIME, said the letters under the picture. Notebooks were spread around him, and a tablet propped against the napkin holder was playing a video of the same flying man lifting a bus out of a river.

The boy looked up as Ye Xuan came in. His eyes went wide behind the glasses.

"You're the ghost," he said.

Ye Xuan stopped. "Sorry?"

"Grandma said there's a ghost living upstairs who doesn't eat and doesn't pay rent." The boy pushed his glasses up his nose. "You don't look like a ghost."

"Thank you," Ye Xuan said. "I've been working on it."

From behind the counter, without looking up from the bag she was filling, Mrs. Tang said, "Tommy. Homework."

"I'm *doing* homework." Tommy turned back to Ye Xuan with the bright, unstoppable curiosity of a boy who has found a new person to talk at. "Are you Chinese-Chinese? Grandma says you're from Hangzhou. Is it true they have a lake with a pagoda where a snake lady is trapped? Do you like Zenith Prime? Zenith Prime is the best. He can fly at three hundred kilometers an hour. That's faster than a car. He saved forty-two people from the Hudson bus crash. Look." He spun the tablet around. "That's him. Are you Awakened?"

Ye Xuan blinked under the onslaught.

"I'm—" He thought about the green wave on Broadway, the fence by City Hall, the copper tin in his pocket. "No. I'm an engineering student."

Tommy deflated slightly. "Oh." Then he brightened again. "Engineering is okay. Halcyon has engineers. They built Zenith Prime's suit. Did you know Halcyon is going to make *everyone* Awakened one day? It's called Ascension. My teacher says it's propaganda but I think she's just jealous."

"Tommy," Mrs. Tang said. "*Homework.*"

Tommy sighed the enormous sigh of the deeply misunderstood and bent over his notebook. Ye Xuan glanced at it. Math. Fractions. A row of red crosses ran down the margin.

"Want help?" he asked, before he could stop himself.

* * *

They worked on fractions for forty minutes while the evening rush thinned.

Tommy was not stupid. He was bored. He had decided fractions were for losers who weren't going to be Awakened, and therefore he hadn't been listening. Ye Xuan didn't explain fractions the way his teachers had. He drew a pizza. Then he drew a Zenith Prime eating an eighth of the pizza, and a supervillain stealing three-eighths, and asked how much pizza was left for the citizens of New York.

Tommy got it on the third pizza. By the sixth, he was inventing his own supervillains.

"You're better than my teacher," he announced.

"Your teacher has thirty students. I have one."

"You're still better." Tommy packed his notebooks and looked at Ye Xuan with new respect. "Are you *sure* you're not Awakened? You're really fast at math."

"Studying," Ye Xuan said. "That's the only superpower I had for eighteen years."

It was true, and it was strange to say aloud. Behind him, the bakery had gone quiet. The last customer was gone. The metal shutter was halfway down.

Mrs. Tang stood behind the counter, wiping her hands on her apron, watching him.

"Tommy," she said in Fuzhou dialect. "Go upstairs. Tell your mother I'll be up in ten minutes."

Tommy went, thundering up the stairs to the apartment on the second floor where, Ye Xuan had gathered, his mother lived and worked long night shifts as a nurse.

Then it was just the two of them.

* * *

Ye Xuan took the envelope out of his jacket.

He had thought about this moment all day. He had thought about what to say. In the end, he just held it out.

"It's not all of it," he said. "It's three hundred and eighty. I'll have the rest in two weeks. I have a job now. Skyline Couriers. I'll pay everything, I promise. Plus the plumber."

Mrs. Tang took the envelope. She didn't open it. She weighed it in her floury hand, as if she could tell how much money was inside by the weight.

"Skyline," she said. "Lou Castellano's crazy people who jump on roofs."

"I don't jump on roofs."

"Hm." She opened the envelope, counted the bills with the speed of someone who had counted money for forty years, and nodded once.

Then she took out two twenties and a ten and pushed them back across the counter.

Ye Xuan looked at the money. "Mrs. Tang—"

"For food," she said. "A ghost who doesn't eat scares the customers."

"I can't—"

"You can." She pushed the bills closer. "You think I didn't see? A whole week you were up there, not coming down. No food, no water, nothing. I left congee by your door every day. Every day, the congee was still there in the evening." Her voice didn't change, but she looked away, at the empty street. "I called your phone. You didn't answer. I almost called the police. Then Tuesday you come down the stairs with blood on your shirt and you say *thank you*." She shook her head. "The old Xiao Ye never said thank you. He was a good boy, but he never said thank you."

Ye Xuan didn't know what to say.

A whole week. The previous owner of this body had come back from the pier and lain in that narrow bed, burning with a fever no one knew about, and every day this old woman had left a bowl of rice porridge outside his door, and every evening she had found it untouched.

He thought of his own mother in Hangzhou. Of her braised pork, of the way she always put the best piece on his plate and pretended she didn't like that piece anyway.

*She'll never know what happened to me,* he thought. *In that world, I'm just… gone.*

His eyes stung. He picked up the fifty dollars and folded it carefully.

"Thank you," he said. "Really."

"Hm," said Mrs. Tang, and turned away to the shutter, and that was the end of the conversation.

* * *

She had the shutter halfway down when she stopped.

Ye Xuan saw her shoulders go stiff. He followed her gaze to the window.

Three men were walking slowly past the bakery on the other side of the glass. Young, broad, in leather jackets despite the warm evening. The one in front was the biggest—a thick neck, a shaved head, gold chain. As he passed, he turned his head and looked into the bakery. Not at Ye Xuan. At Mrs. Tang. He smiled, and the smile had nothing friendly in it.

Then he lifted one hand and cracked his knuckles, slowly, one at a time, and the sound was so loud that Ye Xuan heard it through the glass.

Not like knuckles. Like walnuts being crushed.

The three men walked on and disappeared around the corner toward Canal Street.

Mrs. Tang pulled the shutter the rest of the way down with a crash. Her hands were steady, but her face had gone the color of the flour on her apron.

"Who were they?" Ye Xuan asked quietly.

For a moment, he thought she wouldn't answer.

"Rocco Vitale," she said. "And his dogs." She locked the shutter, two locks, and rattled it to check. "They come every month now. Since spring. Every shop on this street. *Insurance,* they call it." She spat the English word out like a bad seed. "Pay, or something happens to your windows. To your ovens. To you."

"Do you pay?"

Mrs. Tang turned and looked at him over her reading glasses, and her black eyes were as hard as Granny Liu's.

"Forty years I've been on this street," she said. "Through the Glow, through the riots after, through two recessions and a husband who—" She stopped. "I paid nobody. I pay nobody."

She switched off the lights.

"They come back on Monday," she said in the dark. "Go to bed, Xiao Ye."
