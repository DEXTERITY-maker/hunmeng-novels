On Sunday mornings, the Golden Lotus was a war zone.

Ye Xuan had not known this. He had been at the park or asleep on the previous Sundays. But this Sunday, Mrs. Tang's usual helper—a cousin's daughter who came in on weekends—had the flu, and at half past eight Mrs. Tang climbed the stairs, knocked on his door, and said, "You owe me for a plumber. Put on an apron."

So by nine he was behind the counter, in a white apron with a faded lotus printed on it, bagging buns.

The line went out the door. Families in their Sunday clothes on the way to church or dim sum. Old men who bought one egg tart and ate it standing up at the window, slowly, as if it were a ritual. Teenagers buying boxes of pineapple buns for parties. Tourists who had read about the Golden Lotus online and photographed every tray before ordering.

Mrs. Tang worked the register like a machine. Ye Xuan bagged, boxed, wrapped and handed, and tried not to drop anything. Twice he gave the wrong change. Once he put a custard bun in a box of red bean buns, and a woman in a purple hat—the same woman from the park, who had blamed Mrs. Tang's pork buns for his black sweat—looked at him as if he had insulted her ancestors.

By ten-thirty, the crush thinned. By eleven, there was only a trickle.

And at eleven-fifteen, the bell over the door jingled, and the temperature in the bakery dropped.

* * *

It wasn't dramatic. Nobody else noticed.

The Golden Lotus was always warm—three big ovens running behind the counter, the air thick with sugar and butter. Ye Xuan was used to it. He had been sweating in his apron all morning.

But when the man came in, the warmth… thinned. Like a door had opened to winter somewhere. The heat from the ovens behind him faded from a solid wall to something softer. On the counter, the steam rising from a fresh tray of egg tarts curled, wavered, and went flat.

Ye Xuan looked up.

The man was tall—taller than Kroll, though nowhere near as broad. Forty, maybe. A long black wool coat, unbuttoned, over a gray sweater. A gray scarf. Short dark hair going silver at the temples. A lean, quiet face, the kind of face you'd forget an hour after you saw it, except for the eyes. The eyes were a pale, washed-out brown, almost amber, and very calm.

His hands, Ye Xuan noticed, were scarred. Old burn scars—shiny, pinkish, running from the backs of the hands up under the cuffs of the coat.

The man stood in front of the display case, looking at the trays with polite interest, like any customer.

He breathed in.

And Ye Xuan's sight opened by itself.

* * *

He didn't want it to. Rule two: *don't stare*. But the sight came the way it had come with Rocco—a reflex, a flinch, an eye blinking at a sudden light.

Inside the man in the black coat, there were embers.

That was the only word for them. Around his heart, coiled tight, lay a pattern of deep red-orange threads that glowed like the coals at the bottom of a fire. They weren't chains or springs or cables. They were *hungry*. As the man breathed in, the ember-threads brightened, and Ye Xuan saw—actually saw—thin ribbons of heat drifting toward him from the ovens, from the trays, from the steaming egg tarts, from Ye Xuan's own skin, and vanishing into the coals.

He was *drinking* the heat.

Not much. Not enough to freeze anyone. Just a sip, the way someone might absently sip a drink while thinking about something else. But the coals around his heart glowed a little brighter with it, and the bakery grew a little colder.

And there was something else in the pattern.

Something that didn't belong.

Deep in the center of the embers, right against the heart, sat a small, hard point of gray light. It wasn't part of the man's gift. Ye Xuan could see the difference immediately—the way you can see a screw in a piece of wood. It was *put there*. The ember-threads were wrapped around it, tangled through it, as if the whole pattern had grown around it like a tree around a nail.

Ye Xuan made himself look down at the tray in front of him.

The sight closed. A faint pressure behind his eyes. No blood.

*Rule two,* he thought, his heart pounding. *Don't stare. Don't stare. Just a customer. Just a customer buying egg tarts.*

* * *

"Six egg tarts, please," said the man in the black coat. His voice was quiet and pleasant, with a slight New York flatness. "In a box, if you have one."

"Of course," said Mrs. Tang.

Ye Xuan took a box. Folded it. Picked up the tongs. His hands were perfectly steady. He was almost proud of them.

"These smell wonderful," the man said to Mrs. Tang while Ye Xuan worked. "My mother used to bake. Not egg tarts—apple pie, mostly, cinnamon rolls. Irish family. But the smell of a bakery on a Sunday morning…" He smiled. It was a nice smile, slightly sad. "It's the same everywhere, isn't it?"

"Bakeries are bakeries," Mrs. Tang said.

"They are." The man watched Ye Xuan put the fourth egg tart in the box. "I'm looking for someone, actually. A student. I was told he rents a room above a bakery on Mott Street. Chinese, about nineteen. Engineering."

The fifth egg tart went into the box.

"Lots of students on Mott Street," Mrs. Tang said without missing a beat. "Lots of bakeries."

"I'm sure." The man's pale amber eyes moved to Ye Xuan. They rested on him for a moment—his face, his hands, the lotus apron. "You're a student, aren't you?"

The sixth egg tart.

"Yes," Ye Xuan said. There was no point lying; he was clearly the right age, clearly Chinese, clearly behind the counter of a bakery on Mott Street. "Hudson Polytechnic."

"Engineering?"

"Yes."

"Then perhaps you can help me." The man leaned slightly on the counter. Not threateningly. Conversationally. "A few weeks ago, a friend of mine died. At Pier 17, down at the Seaport. You may have read about it."

"I don't read the news much."

"No, I suppose students don't." The man's smile didn't change. "My friend was carrying something that belonged to the people I work for. A keepsake. Very valuable to them. It wasn't with him when he was found. We think a young man who was with him that night may have it. A student. Chinese. About nineteen."

The box was full. Ye Xuan closed the lid and folded the tabs.

"If you happen to meet a young man like that," the man in the black coat said quietly, "would you give him a message for me?"

Ye Xuan held out the box. "What message?"

The man took it. His scarred fingers brushed Ye Xuan's hand, and they were dry and very warm, warmer than a person's hands should be.

"Tell him the people who own the stone want it back," he said. "Tell him we're asking politely."

He paused.

"For now."

* * *

In Ye Xuan's jacket pocket, on the hook by the back door, three meters away, the stone lay in its sock in its tin.

He didn't look at the jacket. He didn't let himself even think about looking at it. But he felt the stone—the faint warmth of it, even through the copper pattern—and he felt, too, the moment the man in the black coat felt it.

The man's head tilted. Very slightly. A few degrees to the left. The way Wren tilted her head when she was listening to something in the next building.

For three seconds, he stood absolutely still, holding his box of egg tarts, his head tilted, his pale eyes looking at nothing.

Then he straightened. Whatever he had heard—or almost heard—had been too faint to be sure of. A drum in another apartment.

"How much?" he asked.

"Twelve dollars," said Mrs. Tang.

He took out his wallet and laid a fifty-dollar bill on the counter.

"Keep the change," he said. "For the student fund."

He nodded to them both, politely, and walked out. The bell jingled. Through the window, Ye Xuan watched the long black coat move down Mott Street through the Sunday crowd, unhurried, until it turned the corner and was gone.

Behind the counter, the ovens slowly grew warm again.

* * *

For a long moment, neither of them said anything.

Then Mrs. Tang reached for the fifty-dollar bill. She picked it up—and dropped it again at once, with a small sharp sound, shaking her fingers.

Ye Xuan picked it up.

It was hot. Not warm from a pocket. *Hot*, like a plate fresh from the oven. And as he turned it over in his fingers, he saw that the corner where the man had held it was brown and curled, faintly scorched, as if someone had pressed a cigarette against it.

He looked up at Mrs. Tang. Her face was gray under the flour.

"Lots of students on Mott Street," she said quietly. "Lots of bakeries." She took off her reading glasses and rubbed her eyes. "But not so many, maybe, that he won't find you."

She put the glasses back on and turned to the next customer as if nothing had happened.

"Next," she said. "Who's next? Two red bean? Xiao Ye, two red bean."

Ye Xuan bagged two red bean buns.

His hands, he noticed, were no longer quite so steady.
