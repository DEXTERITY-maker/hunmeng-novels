Juno came at five fifty-eight. She brought Tank.

They walked into Columbus Park side by side through the morning mist—a slim girl in a red jacket with her braid tucked into the collar, and a man the size of a refrigerator in a gray hoodie, carrying two cups of coffee from a bodega. They looked like a mouse and an elephant who had decided to become friends.

"I told him," Juno said before Ye Xuan could ask. "He wanted to come."

"My back," Tank said apologetically, in his soft voice. "When I use my stoneskin a lot, it gets stiff. Juno said maybe…" He trailed off, embarrassed, and held out one of the coffees. "This is for you. I didn't know how you take it. I put sugar."

"Thank you," Ye Xuan said, touched.

Under the locust tree, the grannies had stopped warming up.

Twelve gray heads turned. Twenty-four sharp eyes took in the newcomers—Tank's enormous shoulders, Juno's red jacket, the coffee cups, the way Juno stood a little closer to Ye Xuan than to Tank.

There was a short, terrible silence.

"Aiya," said Granny Wu, with deep satisfaction. "Fresh meat. *Two* fresh meat."

* * *

The interrogation was even worse this time, because there were two of them, and because Juno didn't speak Chinese and the grannies switched to English with an enthusiasm that suggested they had been waiting years for the opportunity.

"Name?"

"Juno. Juno Reyes."

"Juno. What kind of name is Juno?"

"Roman goddess. My mom liked mythology."

"Hm." Granny Wu considered this and decided it was acceptable. "Age?"

"Nineteen."

"Girlfriend?" Granny Wu pointed at Ye Xuan.

"*No*," said Juno and Ye Xuan at the same time.

The grannies exchanged knowing looks.

"And you," said Auntie Feng, turning to Tank, her whistle swinging. "You're very big. Do you eat vegetables?"

"Yes, ma'am," said Tank.

"Good. Most big boys don't eat vegetables. You can stand in the front row. No—" She reconsidered, looking at his height. "You'll block everybody. Back row. Next to Little Bamboo."

"Little Bamboo?" Juno muttered.

"Me," said Ye Xuan. "Apparently."

At the front of the group, Granny Liu had said nothing. She stood leaning on her cane, looking not at Tank or Juno but at Juno's legs. Then at Tank's back. Then at Ye Xuan.

Something moved in her old black eyes.

"Back row," she said. "All three. Beside the tree. Don't talk."

The music started.

* * *

Tank was hopeless at tai chi. Juno was worse.

She had spent two years training her body to do one thing: compress and release, explosively, a thousand times a day. The slow, rounded movements of the form made her twitch. She kept anticipating the next move and doing it too early. She lifted her arms like she was about to punch somebody. On the third form, when everyone stepped left, she stepped left so hard that she nearly launched herself into the bushes.

Auntie Feng blew her whistle.

"*Slow!*" she barked. "This is not a race! The river does not run to the sea! It *flows* to the sea!"

"Sorry," Juno muttered.

"Don't think about the movements," Ye Xuan whispered beside her. "Just breathe. Watch my breathing."

"*No talking in the back row!*"

They stopped talking.

But Juno watched him. He could feel her watching—out of the corner of her eye, as she fumbled through the forms, her attention fixed on the rise and fall of his chest. Slowly, she started to match it. In, for three counts. A pause. And then, on the fourth count, instead of releasing—

He reached over and touched her elbow, very lightly, with one finger, and pushed the faintest thread of qi through it.

*Here. Like this. Fold.*

He saw it happen.

It was early. The sun hadn't come up yet over the Manhattan Bridge. The threads of qi around the old locust tree were thick and slow, spiraling in the mist. And as Juno breathed in—three counts, wide and open, the way the grannies breathed—one of them drifted toward her. Then another. They followed her breath down, past her lungs, into her belly.

And on the fourth count, guided by the thread of his qi, she folded them down and *held* them.

Two threads. Settled in a place below her navel that had never held anything before.

Juno stopped moving.

The music went on. The grannies went on, gliding into the next form. Juno stood frozen in the back row with her arms half-raised, her eyes wide.

"Oh," she said very softly.

* * *

He knew what she was feeling. He remembered it.

The warmth. A tiny, gentle warmth below the navel, like a cup of tea in cold hands. Not much. Hardly anything. But *there*. Something that hadn't been there before. Something that came from outside her, from the air, from the world, and filled a place inside her that she hadn't known was empty.

And then he saw something he hadn't expected.

The warmth didn't stay in her belly. It drifted—slowly, on its own, without any guidance from him—downward. Down through her hips. Down through her thighs. Down into her calves, into the bright copper coils of her gift, where the dark frayed patches were.

The coils drank it.

Like dry earth drinking rain. The two threads of qi soaked into the worn copper and vanished, and where they had touched, the dark patches were very slightly less dark. Not healed. Nowhere near healed. But *fed*, for the first time since she had Awakened. Paid back, just a little, for two years of taking.

Juno's legs stopped trembling.

He hadn't even noticed that they had been trembling until they stopped. A fine, constant tremor that she had carried so long it had become part of her. And now, for the first time, it was gone.

Juno stood in the back row of the grannies' tai chi practice, at six in the morning, in Columbus Park, with her arms half-raised and tears running down her face.

She didn't wipe them away. She didn't seem to notice them.

"Breathe," Ye Xuan whispered. "Again. Keep going."

She breathed.

* * *

Tank got one thread that morning. Just one, at the very end, with Ye Xuan's finger on his huge shoulder guiding it down. It went to his back, as Juno's had gone to her legs, and soaked into the gray granite pattern of his stoneskin, which ran along his spine in thick, heavy plates. When the practice was over, Tank stretched, slowly, and something in his back made a soft pop.

He looked at Ye Xuan with a kind of wonder.

"It doesn't hurt," he said. "When I bend. It always hurts when I bend."

"It'll come back," Ye Xuan said. "One thread isn't much. You have to come every day."

"Every day," Tank repeated, as if Ye Xuan had told him he'd won the lottery. "Okay. Every day."

Juno was sitting on the bench under the locust tree with her elbows on her knees and her face in her hands. She had stopped crying a while ago. Now she was just sitting there, breathing, slowly, in and out, as if she was afraid she might forget how.

The grannies were packing up their speaker and their water bottles and arguing about whose turn it was to buy the soy milk. Ye Xuan went to help Granny Liu with her bags, the way he did every morning.

She let him take them. But she didn't start walking.

She stood looking at Juno on the bench. At Tank, stretching his back, a slow smile spreading across his broad face.

"My husband," Granny Liu said quietly, "used to say that his family's breathing was for health. That's all. Live long, sleep well, walk without a cane." She tapped her cane on the concrete, the cane she didn't need. "Three hundred years his family practiced it, and that's all it ever did."

She turned and looked up at Ye Xuan.

"But you," she said. "You're not using it for health, are you?"

Ye Xuan didn't answer. He didn't know what to say.

Granny Liu nodded slowly, as if he had said something anyway.

Then she did something she had never done before. She put her hands together, the right fist inside the left palm, and bowed to him—a small, old-fashioned bow, the kind he had only ever seen in historical dramas on television. The kind a student gave a teacher.

"Little Master," she said. *Xiao Shifu.*

Behind her, Granny Wu's head snapped around.

"What did you call him?"

"Little Master," Granny Liu repeated calmly, and took her shopping bags back from Ye Xuan, and began walking toward Mott Street without another word.

Granny Wu stared at Ye Xuan. Then at Granny Liu's retreating back. Then at Ye Xuan again.

"Little Master," she said slowly, tasting it. A wicked gleam came into her eyes. "*Little Master*. Hm. Better than Little Bamboo."

By the time Ye Xuan got back to the Golden Lotus, every one of the twelve grannies was calling him that, and by noon, so was half of Mott Street.

* * *

He was eating congee at the bakery's back table, trying to ignore Mrs. Tang's raised eyebrow—"*Little Master?*"—when his phone buzzed.

Juno.

**Juno:** *same time tomorrow?*

**Ye:** *Every day. That's the rule.*

**Juno:** *ok*

**Juno:** *thank you*

**Juno:** *also*

**Juno:** *wren says the police took the tape down at pier 17 yesterday. nobody's watching it anymore*

**Juno:** *you said you don't remember that night*

**Juno:** *u want to go look?*

Ye Xuan looked at the message for a long time.

Then he typed one word.

**Ye:** *Tonight.*
