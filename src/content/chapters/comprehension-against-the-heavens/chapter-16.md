The video was eleven seconds long.

Agent Priya Shah held her phone out across the little metal table so he could see it. She didn't say anything. She just let it play.

A loading dock behind a warehouse in the Meatpacking District. Gray concrete, a row of dumpsters, a gap of maybe four meters between two raised platforms. A young man in a red Skyline jacket running along one platform with a cardboard tube under his arm. One step, two steps, three—a tiny pause, so small you'd miss it if you weren't looking for it—four. He jumped. He cleared the gap easily, too easily, landing on the far platform a good meter past the edge, and kept running.

The video ended. Shah pressed *play* again.

"Parkour," Ye Xuan said.

"Mm-hm." Shah let it play a third time. "I looked up parkour. I watched a lot of videos. Professionals can do a gap like that. Professionals with years of training. They don't usually do it carrying architectural drawings, in sneakers with no grip, without slowing down." She tapped the screen, freezing it on the moment of the jump. "And they don't usually pause like that before they jump. Like they're counting."

Ye Xuan said nothing.

They were sitting at the one table beside a halal cart on the corner of Fourteenth Street and Ninth Avenue. The cart sold chicken and rice for eight dollars, and Shah had bought two plates before he arrived and pushed one across to him without asking. She wasn't wearing her gray suit today. Jeans, sneakers, a dark green hoodie with a university logo on it. Without the suit, she looked younger—late twenties, sharp-featured, her black bob tucked behind her ears. But her eyes were the same. Dark, focused, not missing anything.

She had been waiting for him outside the warehouse when he came out. She had held up the phone and said, "Lunch?"

He had not felt he had a choice.

* * *

"Am I under arrest?" he asked.

"No."

"Then why are you here?"

Shah put her phone away and picked up her plastic fork. She ate a piece of chicken thoughtfully before she answered.

"I'm not here as a federal agent," she said. "Today's my day off. I'm here as a person who has a question she can't stop thinking about." She pointed the fork at him. "You know what the Bureau ruled about Silas Crane's death?"

"No."

"Gift overload. Accidental." She said it flatly, as if reading from a form. "Unregistered Awakened with an unstable energy-type gift pushes himself too hard, burns out, dies. Case closed. Thank you, next."

"Isn't that possible?"

"Sure. It happens. Burnout kills more Awakened than anything else." She stabbed a piece of rice. "But the case was closed forty-six hours after his death. No autopsy. No interviews except ours with you, which Kroll did on his own initiative, and which was never added to the file. The order to close it came directly from the Director's office. Not from our section chief. From *the Director*." She looked at him. "Do you know how unusual that is? The Director of the Bureau of Awakened Affairs doesn't personally close the file on a dead unregistered nobody on a pier in Lower Manhattan."

"Unless he isn't a nobody."

"Unless he isn't a nobody," Shah agreed.

She took a folded sheet of paper from her hoodie pocket and flattened it on the table between their plates. It was a printout—a photo of a man and a few lines of text. Ye Xuan recognized him from the photo on the laptop: thin, sharp-faced, a streak of gray in black hair, a tattoo of a lightning bolt on the side of his neck.

"Silas Crane. Thirty-four. Awakened during the Glow at twenty-two. Electrician from Newark." Shah's finger moved down the lines. "Four years ago, he volunteered for a clinical trial. Halcyon Dynamics. Something called the Gift Enhancement Study. Supposed to stabilize gifts, reduce burnout risk. Paid volunteers a lot of money." Her finger stopped. "He was in it for eight months. Then he walked out of the facility in New Jersey one night and never came back. No registration. No address. No bank account. He lived in cash for four years. Halcyon never reported him missing, never reported anything stolen, never contacted the Bureau."

"So Halcyon didn't care that he left."

"Or Halcyon cared so much that it didn't want anyone else looking for him." Shah folded the paper and put it away. "The day before he died, somebody accessed his file in our database from inside the Bureau. The access log was deleted the day after. I only know because I'm the one who built half the logging system, and I keep backups of my backups."

She leaned back.

"So here's my question, Mr. Ye. A man runs from Halcyon for four years. He dies on a pier, burned from the inside. The Director closes his case in two days. And the only witness—a quiet engineering student with no gift, who was standing on that pier with him eleven minutes before he died—is now jumping across loading docks like a professional free runner and beating up Canal Street racketeers with one finger." She smiled slightly. "Yes, I heard about that. Chinatown talks. So what did Silas Crane give you?"

* * *

Ye Xuan looked at his plate.

The chicken and rice was good. Spicy, with too much white sauce, the way he liked it. He had eaten half of it without noticing.

*Rule seven,* he thought. *Nobody learns about the stone.*

But Shah was not Ash. She was not Rocco. She had bought him lunch on her day off and shown him a file that she probably wasn't supposed to have. And she was right—about all of it. Something was wrong. Someone had closed a case that shouldn't have been closed.

He thought of Kroll's voice: *the people looking for it are much more dangerous than we are.*

He thought of the unsent draft. *I met a strange man who says the sky is lying to us.*

"I don't remember the pier," he said. It was true. "I've told you that. I really don't."

Shah watched him.

"But you're right," he went on slowly. "Something's wrong. And I want to know what happened there as much as you do. Maybe more."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have right now."

Shah studied him for a long moment. Then she sighed, took out a pen, and wrote a phone number on a paper napkin.

"My personal number," she said. "Not the Bureau's. Not Kroll's. Mine." She pushed it across the table. "When you're ready to stop lying to me—call."

He took the napkin. "I'm not—"

"You are. It's okay. Everybody lies to the Bureau. It's practically the national sport." She stood up, dropping her empty plate into the trash bin. "But Mr. Ye, I'm going to tell you one more thing, and I want you to listen to it carefully."

She leaned on the table, close enough to speak quietly.

"I've been watching Mott Street on my days off. Not officially. Just—sitting in a coffee shop across from your bakery with a book." She paused. "Last Sunday, a man in a long black coat came out of the Golden Lotus carrying a box of pastries. Tall, forties, scars on his hands. I took his picture. I ran it through every database the Bureau has access to—ours, the FBI's, the State Department's, Interpol."

"And?"

"And nothing." Shah straightened. "No driver's license. No passport. No arrest record. No tax return. No social media. No face match anywhere, on anything, ever. In this country, in this century, that doesn't happen by accident. That happens when somebody very powerful has gone into every system and erased a person."

She zipped up her hoodie.

"People like that don't buy egg tarts because they like egg tarts, Mr. Ye. Be careful."

* * *

She was halfway down the block when he called after her.

"Agent Shah."

She turned.

"The black sedan," he said. "Parked across from the bakery at night. Is that yours?"

For the first time, something flickered across Shah's face. Surprise. Then something colder.

"I don't have a car," she said. "I take the subway."

She looked at him for another second. Then she turned and walked away toward the subway entrance on the corner, quickly, and disappeared down the stairs.

* * *

He finished his chicken and rice standing at the cart, not tasting it.

*Not the Bureau.* The black sedan wasn't the Bureau.

He thought of the cigarette glowing in the dark. The driver tilting his head back to look up at the third-floor window. The man in the black coat tilting his head in the bakery, listening for a drum in another apartment.

*They know where I live,* he thought. *They've known for weeks. They're just not sure yet.*

*And when they're sure—*

His phone buzzed. Wren.

**Wren:** *hey runner. where r u. lou has a rush job uptown, 2 boxes, big tip*

**Wren:** *also*

**Wren:** *heard rocco's guys talking on canal earlier. 4 of them now not 3. they said ur name. or "the bakery boy" anyway. thought u should know*

He looked at the message for a long moment.

**Ye:** *Thanks. On my way.*

He put the napkin with Shah's number carefully in his wallet, behind his student ID, and ran.
