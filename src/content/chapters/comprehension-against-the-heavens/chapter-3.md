He slept for eleven hours.

When he woke, the stone was still in his hand, and the light outside the window had turned the orange of late afternoon. His head no longer hurt. In fact, he felt better than he had since arriving in this world—clear, rested, strangely light, as if he had put down a weight he hadn't known he was carrying.

He wrapped the stone in a sock, put the sock in the inner pocket of the black windbreaker, and decided that for today, he would not look at it again.

"Rules," he told himself, sitting on the edge of the bed. In his old life, he had made rules for everything: study schedules, exam strategies, a list taped to his wall titled *Things I Will Not Do Before the Gaokao*. Rules made chaos manageable. "Rule one: don't look at that stone. Rule two: don't stare at federal agents. Rule three: figure out what this ability actually is before it kills me."

His stomach growled loudly.

"Rule four," he added. "Food."

* * *

In the evening, Chinatown was a river. Ye Xuan let it carry him down Mott Street, past fish markets where crabs climbed over each other in plastic tubs, past herbal shops with jars of dried roots in the windows, past a barber stropping a razor on a leather strap. He bought a box of fried rice for six dollars and ate it standing on a corner. Then, because he was a responsible adult with $37.17 to his name, he did not buy a second one.

After that, he began to experiment.

Carefully. A little at a time. He looked at a traffic light until he understood it—the timer inside, the sensor loop under the asphalt, the simple program that decided when red became green. A slight pressure behind his eyes, nothing more. He looked at a parked motorcycle and understood its engine: the rhythm of fuel and spark and piston, as clear as a song. The pressure grew stronger. He stopped before it turned into pain.

*So the more complicated the thing,* he thought, *the more it costs. The phone was easy. The engine was harder. Kroll—a living person with a power—was much harder. And the stone…*

He didn't finish the thought. Rule one.

*It's like a muscle. Or like reading. A child can read a street sign. Reading a whole library takes a lifetime.*

He walked on and found himself in Columbus Park.

It was a small park, more concrete than grass, crowded with old men playing Chinese chess at stone tables and slapping the pieces down like insults. At the far end, under the trees, a group of elderly women was practicing tai chi, moving slowly and in unison to a scratchy recording of a guzheng.

Ye Xuan stopped to watch. In his old life, his grandfather had done tai chi every morning in their courtyard. Ye Xuan had always thought it looked like a very slow fight against an invisible enemy who wasn't trying very hard.

He looked at the women. And the world shifted.

There were no golden chains inside them—they weren't Awakened. But there was *something*. As they moved, the air around them stirred with faint threads, so thin and pale that he would never have noticed them without his strange new sight. With every breath the women drew in, the threads drifted toward them, curled for a moment around their bodies, and drifted away again as they breathed out. Most of it slipped away. But a tiny fraction stayed, sinking to a point just below the navel, where it pooled, warm and faint, like the last ember in a stove.

Ye Xuan stared.

*The air,* he thought. *There's something in the air.*

He turned his sight away from the women and toward the park itself—the trees, the pigeons, the evening sky. And it was everywhere. Pale, almost invisible threads of light drifted through the city like pollen, finer than spider silk, endless. They gathered thickest around the old trees and the stones, and the wind from the river carried them in slow currents down the streets.

He had read about this a thousand times. Every cultivation novel began with it. The ancient masters had a name for it.

*Qi.*

His old world had none. He was sure of it now; he would have felt it. But here, twelve years ago, the sky had burned white for seven minutes and poured… *this*… into the world. And nobody had noticed. Nobody could see it. The Awakened were simply people whose bodies had happened to soak up too much of it at once, by accident, the way a sponge soaks up spilled water. People called it powers. Mutation. Anomaly. Gift.

They didn't know it was *energy*. That it could be gathered. Refined. Cultivated.

The women finished their form and stood still, hands resting over their stomachs. One of them laughed and said something about her knees. None of them had any idea that every morning for years, they had been taking the first step on a road that, in another world's stories, led all the way to immortality.

Ye Xuan realized his hands were shaking.

He took a breath—slowly and deliberately, the way the women did—and *watched* the threads as he did it. Watched them drift toward him. Most of them slid away. But one, just one, thin as a hair, followed his breath down, down, past his lungs, and settled into that point below his navel.

Warmth bloomed there. Tiny. Unmistakable.

"Holy—" he whispered in English, then switched to Mandarin, because some things had to be said in your own language. "Heaven and earth."

* * *

The scream of brakes pulled him out of it.

A delivery van had stopped short at the corner of Mulberry Street, and the driver was leaning out of the window, yelling at the sky.

No. Not at the sky. At the rooftops.

Ye Xuan looked up.

Someone was running across the roofs: a girl in a red jacket, a square delivery bag strapped to her back, sprinting along the edge of a five-story building as if it were a sidewalk. At the end of the roof was a gap—an entire street, ten meters of empty air above the traffic.

She didn't slow down.

She jumped.

For a moment she simply hung there against the orange sky, arms out, the red jacket snapping behind her. Then she landed on the opposite roof, rolled once, came up running, and vanished behind a water tower. The van driver honked twice and shouted something about lunatics and insurance rates.

Nobody else on the street even looked up. Apparently, in this New York, a girl jumping across Mulberry Street wasn't news.

But Ye Xuan had been looking at her when she jumped. With his *other* sight.

And he had seen it.

The threads inside her weren't chains, like Kroll's. They were springs—bright, copper-colored coils wound tight in her calves and the soles of her feet. As she ran, they wound tighter with every step: one, two, three. On the third step, she breathed in, short and sharp. On the fourth, she released the breath and the coils together, the coils snapped open, and the air under her feet became something she could push against.

One, two, three—*breathe*. Four—*release*.

It wasn't only power. It was technique. A rhythm she had worked out on her own, through hundreds of falls, through trial and error. The power was hers. But the rhythm—

The rhythm, Ye Xuan understood.

He understood it completely, the way he had understood the phone. Not just *what* she did, but *why* it worked: how the breath compressed the energy, how the timing sent it into the push instead of letting it leak away.

And there was a tiny, warm ember below his navel.

He didn't decide to try. His body moved first, the way it had on a rainy street in Hangzhou a lifetime ago.

One step. Two. Three—*breathe*—and the ember flared, a single thread of warmth racing down through his legs to the soles of his feet—

Four. *Release.*

He jumped.

He meant to jump onto the bench in front of him. Instead, the ground fell away. The bench flashed past beneath him, then the sidewalk, then the roof of the delivery van—and he slammed sideways into the iron fire escape of the building on the corner, a full story above the street, and grabbed the railing with both hands just before gravity remembered him.

He hung there, gasping.

His legs were trembling. His nose was bleeding again. Below him, the van driver was staring up with his mouth open.

"Lunatics," the driver said weakly, to no one in particular, and drove away.

* * *

Ye Xuan hauled himself over the railing and collapsed onto the metal grating of the fire escape. The ember below his navel was gone—burned out completely by that one jump. He felt hollow, shaky, and more alive than he had ever felt in either of his lives.

*I copied it,* he thought. *I saw her technique, understood it, and copied it. With a single thread of qi.*

*What could I do with ten? With a hundred?*

Something landed on the fire escape above him with a soft, light thump.

He looked up.

The girl in the red jacket was crouching on the landing one level higher, forearms resting on her knees, looking down at him. Up close, she was about his age, with a long dark braid tucked into her collar and a fresh scrape across one cheekbone. The delivery bag on her back read **SKYLINE COURIERS — WE DON'T DO STAIRS**.

She did not look happy.

"You," she said, "copied my step."

Ye Xuan wiped the blood from his lip with the back of his hand. "Sorry?"

"Don't play dumb. A four-count, breath on three. That's *my* rhythm. It took me two years and one broken collarbone to figure it out." She tilted her head, and her eyes narrowed. "Nobody can copy a gift. That's not how it works. Gifts don't transfer, and they can't be taught. Everybody knows that."

"I'm—" He thought fast. "I'm just a student who's late on his rent."

She looked at him for a long moment. At the blood on his face. At his trembling legs. At the thin, hungry wrists sticking out of his sleeves.

Then, unexpectedly, she laughed.

"Okay, student." She stood up, perfectly balanced on the narrow railing. "Free advice. Whatever you are, don't do that where there are cameras." She pointed down at a small black dome above the door of the pharmacy on the corner. "The Bureau watches the street cams. They love new faces."

"Thanks," Ye Xuan said. And then, because $37.17 was a very small number: "Are you… hiring? At Skyline?"

She blinked. Then she grinned, reached into her jacket, and flicked something down to him. A card. He caught it.

"We're always short of runners," she said. "Cash, no questions. Ask for Juno."

She ran up the fire escape in a few bounding steps—one, two, three, *four*—and was gone over the edge of the roof.

* * *

Ye Xuan sat on the fire escape until the sky turned dark blue and the first lights came on across Chinatown.

He turned the two cards over in his hands. A plain white one with an eagle: *Special Agent Daniel Kroll*. A bright red one with a winged shoe: *Skyline Couriers*. In his jacket pocket, wrapped in a sock, the stone lay warm against his ribs.

The world called them powers, he thought. Gifts. Anomalies. A lottery that a few people won and everyone else lost.

But he had seen what lay underneath. Lines. Patterns. Principles. This world was full of qi, and no one in it knew how to cultivate. Every Awakened in this city had stumbled into their strength by accident, and every one of them was standing on the first step of a road they couldn't see.

He could see it.

*If this world has no Dao,* Ye Xuan thought, *then I'll be the one to find it.*

He breathed in slowly, the way the old women in the park had, and watched a single pale thread drift down to settle below his navel.

It was a very small beginning.

He smiled anyway.

* * *

On the roof of the building across the street, a man in a long black coat lowered his binoculars.

He took out his phone and dialed a number from memory.

"It's me," he said. "I found the boy from the pier." He listened for a moment. "No. The Bureau has already talked to him. They don't know." A pause. "Yes, I'm sure. I could feel it from across the street. Crane's heartstone is with him."

He hung up and stood watching the small figure on the fire escape a while longer. Then he stepped back into the dark.
