Pier 17 at midnight was beautiful, which somehow made it worse.

It stuck out into the East River like a long wooden finger, lined with old-fashioned lamps that threw pools of orange light onto the planks. At its root stood a big glass building with a rooftop where, Juno said, people paid two hundred dollars to listen to concerts in the summer. Now it was dark and closed. Beyond the railing, the river moved black and slow, and on the far side, the lights of Brooklyn glittered under the great stone towers of the Brooklyn Bridge.

There was no one else on the pier. The wind off the water was cold.

"It was out at the end," Juno said quietly. "Wren listened to the police radio that night. They said the far end, by the north railing."

They walked out together. Their footsteps sounded hollow on the planks.

Ye Xuan didn't need her to tell him where. Halfway down the pier, he could already feel it—a pressure in the air, faint, like the moment before a thunderstorm when the hair on your arms stands up. The closer they got to the end, the stronger it became.

And then he saw it with his ordinary eyes.

The planks near the north railing were new. Pale yellow wood, unweathered, a patch of maybe three meters by three, set into the old gray planks around it like a bandage. The railing above it had been replaced too—shiny new steel bolted to old posts. On one of the old posts, just beside the new section, the wood was black. Not painted. *Burned*, in a pattern like a branching tree, the kind of mark lightning leaves when it strikes the ground.

"They replaced what they could," Juno said. "Wren said the planks were burned right through."

Ye Xuan crouched beside the blackened post.

The smell was gone—a month of wind and rain had taken it. But the pressure was strongest here. He put his hand flat on the burned wood. It was cold and rough under his palm.

"I'm going to look," he said. "Properly. It might take a minute. If I start bleeding a lot—"

"I'll pull you away."

"Thanks."

He closed his eyes, took a breath, opened them, and let his sight sink into the wood.

* * *

At first, there was only wood.

Old wood. Long fibers of cellulose, dead and dry, saturated with salt from decades of river spray. The burned pattern on its surface, a tree of black carbon.

But underneath the carbon, *inside* it, there was something else.

Light.

Not threads of qi, drifting and soft. Something sharper, hotter, frozen in place—as if, in the moment of the burning, something had pressed itself into the wood so hard that it had left an impression, the way a hand leaves an impression in wet concrete. White-blue lines, jagged, branching, twisted together. Crane's gift. The pattern of it, burned into the pier like a photograph burned into film.

And around that, fainter, other impressions. Overlapping. Layered. Like several photographs printed on top of each other.

He looked deeper.

The world around him dimmed. The lamps, the river, the cold wind, Juno's quiet breathing—all of it went far away. There was only the pattern in the wood, and the pattern was *opening*.

It was not like seeing the past. Not exactly. It was more like reading a very detailed description of it, written in a language he had never learned and understood perfectly. The impressions arranged themselves into shapes. The shapes into figures. The figures into a moment.

* * *

Night. The same lamps, the same black river.

A man stood at the railing. Thin, sharp-faced, a streak of gray in his black hair. His pattern blazed inside him—white-blue arcs crackling along every nerve, an energy gift wound so tight and so bright it was almost painful to look at, even in the echo. And it was *damaged*. Ye Xuan saw it at once. The arcs were frayed, cracked, burning at the edges, much worse than Juno's coils. Crane had been burning out for years.

In front of him stood a young man in a dark hoodie, holding a small box.

Ye Xuan's breath caught.

He knew that young man. He had looked at that face in a mirror every morning for a month.

The other Ye Xuan was frightened. His pattern—an ordinary person's pattern, a faint human glow, no gift—was flickering with fear. He was holding out the box. Copper, small, latticed. Prototype 3.

Crane took the box. He opened it. And from inside his own jacket he took out something small and gray—

—and the echo *flared*, and Ye Xuan flinched, because even here, even as an impression burned into wood a month ago, the stone was bright. Brighter than Crane's gift. Brighter than anything. A white-gold star in the man's hand, its rings pulsing out across the pier.

Crane put the stone in the box. Closed the lid. The rings dimmed, but not enough. Seventy percent.

And then Crane's head turned sharply toward the shore.

Ye Xuan followed the turn.

Three figures were coming down the pier. Not running. Walking. The one in front was tall, in a long coat, and inside him, coiled around his heart, embers glowed red-orange—and in the middle of the embers, a hard gray point, like a nail.

*Ash.*

Behind him, two more. Patterns he didn't recognize. Something sharp and humming in one. Something heavy in the other.

In the echo, Crane did something very fast.

He opened the box again. He took out the stone. He grabbed the other Ye Xuan's hand—the boy flinched, tried to pull away—and pressed the stone into his palm and closed the boy's fingers around it with both his own hands.

Crane's lips moved. Ye Xuan couldn't hear words; there was no sound in the echo. But he saw Crane's face, very close to the boy's. Urgent. Desperate. Almost gentle.

Then Crane shoved him.

Not toward the shore. Toward the railing. Toward a gap in the railing a few meters along, where a metal ladder ran down the side of the pier to the dark water below, to the forest of wooden pilings that held the pier up.

The boy stumbled. Looked back. Crane shouted something.

The boy went down the ladder.

Crane turned to face the three figures walking down the pier. He threw the empty box, hard, toward the river. It flashed in the lamplight and vanished over the edge.

And then he raised his hands, and every frayed, burning, white-blue arc in his body lit up at once.

* * *

The echo went white.

Ye Xuan was thrown out of it so hard that he fell backward onto the planks. The world came back all at once—the lamps, the wind, the river, Juno's hands on his shoulders, Juno's voice saying his name over and over.

"—Ye. *Ye.* Hey. Look at me."

He looked at her. Her face swam in and out of focus.

Something warm was running down his face. Not just from his nose this time. From his eyes too, he realized, touching his cheek. His fingers came away red.

"You're bleeding from your *eyes*," Juno said. Her voice was very calm, the voice of someone who was very frightened. "You said if you started bleeding a lot. This is a lot. This is way more than a lot."

"I'm okay."

"You are definitely not okay."

"I saw it." He sat up. His head was pounding, a deep, hot throb behind his eyes, as if someone was hitting a drum inside his skull. "Juno. I saw what happened."

She held his shoulders and waited.

"He was here," Ye Xuan said. "Crane. And—me. I was here. I brought him a box. He put something in it, and then they came. Three of them. The man in the black coat. And Crane—" He swallowed. "Crane gave the thing to me. Pushed it into my hand. And pushed me down the ladder. Under the pier. And then he… used everything he had. All of his gift. At once. To stop them. To cover me."

Juno's hands tightened on his shoulders.

"He died so you could get away," she said.

"Yes."

They sat on the cold planks without saying anything. The river moved below them. Far away, a ship's horn sounded on the water, long and low.

"The thing he gave you," Juno said finally. "The second heartbeat. That's it, isn't it."

It wasn't a question. He didn't answer it. He didn't have to.

* * *

He looked at the gap in the railing a few meters away. The ladder was still there—old rusted iron rungs bolted to the side of the pier, leading down into darkness.

"I need to go down there," he said.

"No. Absolutely not. You're bleeding from your eyes."

"There's more." He touched the burned post again, lightly, without looking. He could still feel it—the echo, the impressions, layered and dense. The flash had been the brightest part. But under the flash, deeper, something else was pressed into the wood. Fainter. Different. Not Crane's gift. Not Ash's.

It felt like a voice.

"There's more here," he said. "Something Crane left. Something he—said, maybe. Or thought. Right at the end. I couldn't reach it. The flash was too bright."

"Then come back tomorrow. When you're not bleeding from your eyes."

He looked at her. Then at the dark gap in the railing. Then at the lamps along the pier, where, he realized for the first time, a small round camera was mounted on every third post.

The cameras were all pointed at the walkway. Not at the railing. Not at the ladder.

"Tomorrow," he agreed.

Juno let out a long breath.

They walked back down the pier together, slowly. He leaned on her more than he wanted to. At the root of the pier, where the glass building stood dark, he looked back once, at the patch of new yellow planks at the far end, glowing pale under the lamps.

Something—someone—had stood there a month ago and given up his life so that a frightened nineteen-year-old engineering student could run.

*And that boy died anyway,* Ye Xuan thought. *Three days later. Alone. In a bed above a bakery.*

*So it's up to me now.*

High above the pier, so high that neither of them noticed it, a small black shape hung motionless against the clouds, a single red light blinking on its underside.
