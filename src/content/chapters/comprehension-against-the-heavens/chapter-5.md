The interrogation lasted eleven minutes.

Ye Xuan had once survived a three-hour oral examination for a provincial math competition. This was worse.

"Name?" demanded the woman in the red tracksuit, who turned out to be Granny Wu.

"Ye Xuan."

"From where?"

"Hangzhou."

A ripple of approval went through the group. Hangzhou was respectable. Hangzhou had West Lake and good tea.

"Age?"

"Nineteen." He almost said eighteen, then remembered the student ID.

"Married?"

"No."

"Girlfriend?"

"No."

"Why not?" said Granny Wu, as if this were a personal failure that reflected badly on his entire family.

"I've been… busy."

"Busy," Granny Wu repeated to the others, and they shook their heads sadly.

"Are you eating?" asked a tall, straight-backed woman with short gray hair and a whistle around her neck—Auntie Feng, he would learn, a retired physical education teacher who had never really retired. "You look like a bamboo pole somebody forgot to water."

"I'm eating."

"He lives above Tang Meiling's bakery," said someone at the back. "I've seen him. Tang says he doesn't pay rent."

"I'm going to pay rent," Ye Xuan said. "On Friday."

There was a pause.

Then the smallest woman in the group stepped forward. She was tiny—she couldn't have been taller than a ten-year-old—and very old, with skin like crumpled rice paper and white hair pulled into a neat bun. She leaned on a carved wooden cane she clearly didn't need. Her eyes were very black and very bright.

Everyone else went quiet.

"Why do you want to practice with us?" she asked in Mandarin with a soft Fujian lilt.

Ye Xuan opened his mouth to say something polite—*for my health*, *my grandfather used to practice*, *I want to learn*. All true, in their way.

But the old woman was looking at him the way Kroll had looked at him: patiently, as if she had all morning and could wait through every lie.

"Because when you breathe," he said, "something happens. I want to understand what."

The old woman studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded once.

"Back row," she said. "Next to the tree. Don't talk."

And that, apparently, was that.

"Granny Liu has spoken," Granny Wu announced, slightly disappointed that the interrogation was over. "Back row, fresh meat."

* * *

The back row was the best place in the park.

He hadn't asked for it, and he didn't think Granny Liu had known—but maybe she had. The old locust tree stood two meters behind him, and the threads of qi spiraled around it so thickly that the air itself felt different: cooler, softer, alive. When he breathed in, he could almost taste it, like the first breath after a thunderstorm.

The music started—a crackling guzheng from a small speaker on the bench. The grannies began to move.

Ye Xuan followed.

He was terrible at it. He had watched his grandfather for years without ever trying, and his body didn't know the forms. His arms went up when everyone else's went down. He stepped left into Auntie Feng's whistle range and received a sharp look. Granny Wu, in the row ahead, kept turning around to see if he was still failing, and seemed pleased each time.

But the movements weren't the point.

He breathed. One, two, three, *fold*. One, two, three, *fold*.

And here, in the dawn, beside the old tree, the threads came—not one every ten breaths, as in his closed room, but one every three. Then one every two. They drifted toward him in long, pale ribbons, followed his breath down, and settled into the leaking jar below his navel.

The warmth grew.

A fingertip. A thumb. A coin. A small, glowing pebble.

It still leaked through the walls. But now it was filling faster than it could leak, and the pressure began to build.

At first it was pleasant—a warm, full feeling, like after a good meal. Then it was uncomfortable. Then it was something else entirely: a deep, pushing ache, as if he had swallowed a fist and the fist wanted out.

He kept breathing. Harder. Faster. He folded the threads down with more force, cramming them into the jar, and the jar's walls groaned—he could almost hear it—and didn't break. They just leaked faster. The pressure climbed into his stomach, his chest. Sweat broke out on his forehead despite the cold mist.

*Come on,* he thought, gritting his teeth. *Come on, open—*

A dry little hand tapped his elbow.

He opened his eyes. Granny Liu stood beside him. He had no idea when she had left the front row. Her eyes were on his face—on the sweat, the clenched jaw, the white knuckles.

"You're pushing," she said quietly.

"I'm trying to—"

"I know what you're trying to do." Her voice was calm. "My husband used to make the same face. Listen. Water does not push the riverbank, child. Water fills the river. When the river is full, the bank gives way by itself."

She tapped his elbow once more and walked back to the front row as if nothing had happened.

Ye Xuan stood very still.

*Water fills the river.*

He had been treating the dantian like a lock to be broken. He had been forcing it—the way he had forced formulas into his head, the way he had forced himself through every exam. Push harder. Work harder. Grind.

But the jar wasn't a lock.

He closed his eyes again, and this time he stopped folding the threads down. He stopped pushing. He simply breathed with the grannies—slow, wide, open—and let the threads come to him, and let them settle wherever they wanted to settle.

The ache eased.

The warmth spread, filling not just the jar but the space around it, soaking into his belly, his lower back, the tops of his thighs. It filled slowly, patiently, like rain filling a hollow in a stone.

And then, so quietly that he almost missed it—

*Click.*

It felt exactly like a sound, though there was no sound. Like a key turning in a lock that had been rusted shut for nineteen years.

The walls of the jar changed.

He felt it happen. The porous clay became something smooth and whole. The warmth that had been leaking out of it all morning stopped leaking. It *stayed*. And then, as if a dam had broken, all the warmth that had been soaking into his body rushed back toward the center and poured into the jar at once, filling it to the brim with a single, deep, golden pulse.

Ye Xuan's eyes flew open.

The world was sharper.

He could see every droplet of mist hanging in the air. He could hear a pigeon walking on the roof of the park pavilion, its tiny claws scratching the tiles. He could smell the bakery three streets away—sesame and sugar—and under it, the river. His body felt light, as if someone had removed a backpack full of stones he hadn't known he was carrying.

*Qi Gathering,* he thought, dazed. *First layer.*

He had done it.

He had opened the dantian. In a park in Chinatown, at dawn, in another world, among twelve grannies who thought he was a bamboo pole somebody forgot to water.

He wanted to laugh. He wanted to shout. He wanted to tell someone, anyone—

Then the smell hit.

* * *

It started as a faint itch across his skin. Then a dampness, as if he were sweating heavily. He looked down at his hands.

They were covered in a thin layer of something black.

Not dirt. Something that was coming *out* of him—a dark, greasy film oozing from his pores, beading on his wrists, his neck, his face. And with it came a smell so foul that his eyes watered: rotten eggs, old garbage, a gas station toilet in August.

*Impurities,* he remembered. *Of course. The body expels impurities when the first layer opens. The hero always takes a bath afterward. I skipped those chapters too.*

The music stopped.

Twelve gray heads turned toward the back row.

Granny Wu sniffed. Her face changed. "Aiya!" she shrieked. "What is that? Something died!"

"It's the boy," said Auntie Feng, and blew her whistle as if that would help.

"He's sick!" said someone else. "Food poisoning! Look, he's turning black!"

"Tang Meiling's pork buns," said a woman in a purple hat darkly. "I always said."

"It's not the pork buns," Ye Xuan said desperately. "I'm fine, I'm just—"

"Go home!" Granny Wu flapped her hands at him. "Go home, wash! Wash three times! Don't come back until you smell like a person!"

He ran.

* * *

Mott Street at a quarter to seven was full of delivery men and early shopkeepers, and every one of them turned to stare as a black-streaked young man sprinted past trailing an odor that made a stray cat hiss and climb a fence.

He burst into the Golden Lotus. Mrs. Tang looked up from the oven.

She opened her mouth to say something. Then the smell reached her.

"*AIYA!*"

He fled up the stairs, into the tiny bathroom, and turned the shower on as hot as it would go.

The water ran black for five minutes. Then gray. Then, finally, clear. He scrubbed until his skin was pink, then scrubbed again. The drain gurgled, choked, and filled with a dark sludge that refused to go down.

Below, he heard Mrs. Tang shouting on the phone in Fuzhou dialect about plumbers, and pipes, and ghosts who destroyed drains.

When he finally stepped out and wiped the fog from the mirror, he froze.

The face looking back at him was still his, still thin. But the gray shadows under his eyes had faded to almost nothing. His skin was clear. His eyes were bright, the whites very white. Even the faint scar on his jaw seemed paler.

He made a fist. It felt solid in a way it hadn't before. He took a breath, and his lungs filled all the way down, deep and easy.

"First layer," he whispered to the mirror, and grinned like an idiot.

* * *

The knock came an hour later, after the plumber had left and Mrs. Tang had stopped speaking to him.

It was Granny Liu.

She stood in the doorway of the bakery, tiny and straight, holding a paper bag of dried herbs. She looked at him—clean now, damp-haired, glowing in a way he couldn't hide—and something moved in her old black eyes.

"Boil these," she said, handing him the bag. "Drink it for three days. It helps the body settle after it… cleans itself."

He took the bag slowly. "You knew."

Granny Liu didn't answer directly. She looked past him, at the street, at the morning light.

"Sixty years ago, in a village outside Fuzhou," she said, "my husband sweated black like that. Once. Only once, in his whole life. He practiced every morning until he died, and he said it never happened again, because"—her mouth twisted, as if at an old, bitter joke—"because the heavens had closed, and there was nothing left in the air to breathe."

She turned to go. Then she paused.

"Six o'clock tomorrow," she said. "Back row. And bring a towel."
