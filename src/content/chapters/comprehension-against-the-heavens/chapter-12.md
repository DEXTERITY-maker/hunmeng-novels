The charger for the laptop had been under the bed the whole time, tangled with a phone cable and a single lost sock.

Ye Xuan found it that evening, when he got down on his knees to look for Tank's pliers, which had rolled off the desk. He sat back on his heels and looked at the coiled white cable for a long moment.

He had been putting this off. He knew he had.

The phone had been easy—a tool, a key to unlock, a bank balance to check. But the laptop was different. The laptop was where a person kept their life. Letters. Photos. Notes to themselves. The things they cared about.

It felt like reading someone's diary after their funeral.

*But I am him now,* he thought. *Whether I like it or not. And whatever happened at that pier happened to me, too.*

He plugged the laptop in.

* * *

It asked for a password.

He tried *password*, which was embarrassing. He tried *yexuan* and *YeXuan19* and *hudson*, which was more embarrassing. He considered looking at it with his sight, the way he had looked at the phone, and decided against it—a laptop's lock wasn't a scratched sensor, and he didn't want a nosebleed for nothing.

Then, almost as a joke, he typed his mother's birthday. His mother in Hangzhou. His first mother, in his first life. Eight digits. Year, month, day.

The screen unlocked.

Ye Xuan sat very still with his hands on the keyboard.

*Coincidence,* he told himself. *Lots of people use their mother's birthday. Lots of people's mothers were born on—*

He opened the email.

* * *

There were hundreds of messages, mostly from the university: course registration, library fines, a newsletter from the School of Engineering. Spam about cheap flights. A pizza coupon.

And a folder labeled, in Chinese, 家—*home*.

He opened it.

The most recent email was from eight days ago. The sender was **沈岚**. Shen Lan.

His mother's name was Shen Lan.

He read it with his heart pounding.

*Xuan'er,*

*Why don't you answer your phone? Your father says you're busy with exams, and I know you are, but one message is not too much to ask. Two words: "I'm alive." That's all.*

*It is getting cold here. It must be colder in New York. Did you buy the winter coat? Don't buy a cheap one. Cheap coats are expensive in the end, because you have to buy another one.*

*Your father fixed the balcony door. It still squeaks, but now it squeaks more quietly. He says to tell you that.*

*Eat well. Don't eat only noodles. Don't stay up too late. Answer your phone.*

*Mama*

Ye Xuan read it three times.

*Cheap coats are expensive in the end.* His mother said that. His real mother, his first mother, said exactly that, every single winter, every time she dragged him to the department store on Yan'an Road and made him try on coats he didn't want.

He scrolled down. Older emails. More from Shen Lan, long and worried and full of small instructions. And a few, much shorter, from **叶明远**. Ye Mingyuan.

His father.

*Xuan. Good results on the midterm. Proud of you. Don't let your mother worry too much. —Dad.*

*Xuan. Saw on the news there was an Awakened incident near your university. Are you safe? —Dad.*

*Xuan. Balcony door is fixed. Mostly. —Dad.*

There was a photo attached to that last one. The balcony door of an apartment. Ye Xuan knew that door. He knew the potted orange tree beside it, the one that never gave oranges. He knew the view through the glass: the gray apartment blocks of their compound in Hangzhou, the shape of the hills behind them in the mist.

He had stood on that balcony a thousand times.

He put his hand over his mouth and sat like that for a long time.

* * *

When he could think again, he thought this:

The world was different. There had been a Glow here. There were Awakened, and Halcyon Dynamics, and a man who flew over the Brooklyn Bridge. History had split somewhere, maybe twelve years ago, maybe long before that.

But some things were the same. Mott Street. The Brooklyn Bridge. His face. His name.

His parents.

In this world, too, there was a Shen Lan who worried about winter coats and a Ye Mingyuan who fixed balcony doors badly. They had a son. They had sent him across the ocean to study engineering in New York.

And their son had died in a narrow bed above a bakery, alone, with a fever nobody knew about. And they didn't know. They were still writing emails to him, asking him to answer his phone.

*I'm sorry,* he thought, and didn't know if he was saying it to them, or to the other Ye Xuan, or to his own parents, in the other world, who had lost a son on a rainy street and would never get an email from anyone.

* * *

It was past midnight when he made himself close the folder.

He wiped his eyes on his sleeve and opened the rest of the laptop the way his father would have: methodically, one folder at a time. *What is it, actually? What does it do? Why?*

Coursework. Lab reports. A half-finished essay on the thermal properties of ceramics. A folder of photos—Hudson Polytechnic's campus, a lecture hall (*the lecture hall*, the one from his scattered memory images), a group of students at a bar he didn't recognize. A girl laughing at the camera—*the woman laughing*—with a red scarf. He didn't know her name. There was no label.

And then, on the desktop, a folder named simply **CR**.

Inside were invoices.

*Crane Repairs. Services rendered: repair of portable radio receiver, $80.*

*Crane Repairs. Services rendered: rewiring of handheld detector, $150.*

*Crane Repairs. Services rendered: custom enclosure, prototype 1, $400.*

*Crane Repairs. Services rendered: custom enclosure, prototype 2, $600.*

There was a messaging app on the laptop, too—an encrypted one, the kind journalists and paranoid people used. He opened it.

Only one contact. **S.C.**

The conversation went back four months.

The first messages were ordinary. A man looking for someone to fix old electronics cheaply, no questions asked. The original Ye Xuan replying politely, quoting prices. Photos of broken radios. Photos of a strange handheld device with a needle gauge, like a Geiger counter but not quite.

Then, two months ago, the messages changed.

**S.C.:** *Can you build a box?*

**Ye:** *What kind of box?*

**S.C.:** *Small. Fits in a pocket. Has to stop something from getting out.*

**Ye:** *Radiation? You need lead.*

**S.C.:** *Lead doesn't work. Tried it. Tried everything. Look at this.*

There was an attached image. A sketch, done in ballpoint pen on lined paper: a lattice of lines, repeating, intricate, like the frost pattern on a window.

Ye Xuan stared at it.

It was not the same as the pattern he had wound around the mint tin. But it was *related*. The same idea. The same kind of shape. Someone else—someone without his sight—had looked at the stone's rings and tried to draw them.

**Ye:** *What is this?*

**S.C.:** *Something I see when I'm very tired. Make the walls of the box look like this. Copper. Very fine.*

**Ye:** *That's weird.*

**S.C.:** *Two thousand dollars weird?*

**Ye:** *OK.*

After that, the messages were about the box. Sizes, materials, failed tests. **Ye:** *Prototype 2 reduces the needle reading by 30%.* **S.C.:** *Not enough. They'll still find it.* **Ye:** *Who's "they"?* **S.C.:** *Don't ask questions you don't want the answers to.*

Ye Xuan scrolled down to the last messages. His hand was not quite steady on the trackpad.

They were from the night of the pier.

**S.C.:** *Is the box ready?*

**Ye:** *Prototype 3. Best one yet. 70%.*

**S.C.:** *It'll have to do. Pier 17. 3:30. Come alone. Tell no one.*

**Ye:** *3:30 AM??*

**S.C.:** *They're close. I need to move it tonight. Listen to me, kid. If something happens to me, you take it and you run, and you don't let Halcyon have it. Whatever they offer you. Whatever they say. Don't let them have it.*

**S.C.:** *Promise me.*

There was a pause of four minutes between messages. Ye Xuan could see it in the timestamps. Four minutes in which a nineteen-year-old engineering student had sat at this desk, in this room, and thought about it.

**Ye:** *OK. I promise. Be careful.*

That was the last message.

* * *

There was no box in the room. He had searched the room twice. Prototype 3, seventy percent, the best one yet, had gone to the pier and had not come back.

Only the stone had come back. In a jacket pocket. In the hand of a boy who had made a promise and then lain down and died.

Ye Xuan sat back in the chair. Outside, a truck rumbled down Mott Street. The ceiling fan ticked on every third turn.

He was about to close the laptop when he noticed one more thing.

In the email program, under Drafts, there was a single message. Unsent. Addressed to Shen Lan. Dated four-fifteen in the morning, the night of the pier.

It was very short. The original Ye Xuan had typed it in Chinese, quickly, with two typos he had never corrected.

*Mama, I'm fine. Don't worry. I met a strange man who says the sky is lying to us. I think he's crazy. But tonight I saw*

That was where it stopped.
