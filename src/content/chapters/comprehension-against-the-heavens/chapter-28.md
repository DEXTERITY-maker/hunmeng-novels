The ladder down into the pit was steel, bolted to the concrete wall. Ye Xuan climbed down it with three hundred people screaming above him, and when his feet touched the bottom, the screaming changed.

It became laughter.

He understood why. He looked up and saw himself the way they saw him: a skinny Asian kid in a gray T-shirt and jeans, no gift flickering anywhere, standing alone in a concrete box. A bookie leaning over the railing shouted, "Twenty to one on the kid! Twenty to one!" and the laughter got louder.

Then Bricks came down the other ladder.

He didn't climb. He let go halfway and dropped the last two meters, and when he hit the floor of the pit, the concrete *cracked*—a spiderweb of fractures spreading out under his boots with a sound like a gunshot. The crowd roared.

He was not as big as Tank. He wasn't even as big as Rocco. He was maybe a head taller than Ye Xuan, broad, thick-necked, with a shaved head and a flat nose that had been broken many times. He wore a black tank top and gray sweatpants. His arms were covered in old scars.

He looked at Ye Xuan and grinned. Several of his teeth were gold.

"No gift?" he said. "They gave me a kid with no gift?"

"Apparently," said Ye Xuan.

"You know what my gift is, kid?"

"Your bones are heavy."

Bricks's grin widened. "Smart kid. Know what happens when a heavy bone hits a regular bone?"

He didn't wait for an answer. The speaker crackled—"*Fight*"—and Bricks came across the pit like a falling wall.

* * *

Ye Xuan was already looking.

He had opened his sight on the ladder, slowly, carefully, letting the third layer of Qi Gathering carry the weight of it. And Bricks was easy to read. His gift lived in his skeleton: every bone wrapped in dense threads of gray, packed so tight they looked solid, like rebar inside concrete. They made his bones heavy, three or four times heavier than a normal person's. When he punched, the weight of the bone itself drove the fist forward like a sledgehammer. When he stood still, the weight held him to the ground like an anchor.

And that was the problem with it.

*Heavy things are slow to start,* Ye Xuan thought. *And slow to stop.*

Bricks threw a right hook. It came in a long, heavy arc, and Ye Xuan saw it coming from the moment Bricks's shoulder began to turn. He stepped back. The fist went past his face and kept going—Bricks's whole body turning after it, dragged by the weight of his own arm—and for half a second Bricks's back was turned.

Ye Xuan didn't hit him. He stepped sideways.

Bricks came around, roaring, and threw a left. Ye Xuan stepped back again. A right. He ducked. A kick, slow and enormous, like a tree falling sideways. He jumped back, and Bricks's foot hit the concrete wall of the pit and *punched through it*, leaving a hole the size of a dinner plate.

The crowd went wild.

"*Fight him!*" someone screamed from above. "*Stop running and fight him!*"

Ye Xuan kept running.

* * *

It wasn't running, exactly. Juno had taught him how to fall; Granny Liu had taught him how to breathe; the green waves on Broadway had taught him how to move through a crowd without touching anyone. He used all of it now. He kept just out of Bricks's reach, stepping, sliding, ducking, letting every punch pass by a hand's width away—never fighting the weight, never meeting it, just letting it go past.

And as he moved, he watched.

He watched the gray threads in Bricks's bones. Where they were thickest—the long bones of the arms and legs, the skull, the spine. Where they were thinnest.

They were thinnest at the joints.

At the knees, the elbows, the ankles, the wrists—every place where one bone met another—the dense gray rebar thinned out. It had to. Joints had to move. If the threads had been as thick there as in the long bones, Bricks would have been a statue. So at every joint, the gift stretched thin, like a rope pulled tight between two posts.

And the knees carried all the weight.

Bricks's knees were wrapped in thick braces of old scar tissue. He had been carrying that weight for years, and it was grinding his knees down, Ye Xuan saw—the cartilage was worn, inflamed. Every time Bricks shifted his weight, the threads at his knees strained and flickered.

*There.*

Bricks threw another right hook. Ye Xuan didn't step back this time. He stepped *in*—inside the arc of the punch, so close that Bricks's elbow brushed his ear—and as the big man's weight swung past him, he dropped low and drove the edge of his foot into the side of Bricks's left knee.

With a thread of qi behind it.

Not a strong thread. He couldn't afford strong. But a sharp one, focused to a point, the way he had pushed qi into Rocco's elbow. It struck the thin, strained place where the gray threads stretched across the knee joint—

—and the threads *slipped*.

Just for a moment. Like a rope sliding off a post. The weight that Bricks's left leg had been holding up suddenly had nothing to hold it, and the leg buckled sideways.

Bricks went down on one knee.

The concrete cracked under him.

* * *

The crowd's roar changed pitch. Surprise. Disbelief.

Bricks was up again in a second, snarling, but he was limping now, and his eyes had changed. Not amused anymore. Careful.

"What'd you do?" he growled.

Ye Xuan didn't answer. He was breathing hard. His dantian was a quarter empty. A thin trickle had started from his left nostril, but it was slow. The third layer, he thought. Even a month ago, that would have cost him a full nosebleed.

Bricks came at him again. Slower now. Smarter. No more big hooks. Short jabs, heavy, each one meant to end the fight if it landed. Ye Xuan slipped the first. The second. The third grazed his shoulder, and even that glancing touch was like being hit with a hammer. He felt it all the way down to his toes. He staggered back and hit the wall of the pit.

Bricks grinned and closed in.

*Don't fight the weight,* Ye Xuan thought. *Use it.*

Bricks threw a straight right at his face—short, hard, the whole weight of his skeleton behind it.

Ye Xuan dropped.

Not a duck. A *fall*. Juno's fall, chin tucked, knees bent, collapsing straight down the wall like a puppet with its strings cut. Bricks's fist went over his head and hit the concrete wall behind him with a crack like a cannon.

And stuck.

The fist went into the wall up to the wrist. Bricks pulled, and the wall held him, and for one second, the heaviest man in the pit was nailed to the concrete by his own arm.

Ye Xuan came up from the floor under Bricks's arm and put both palms flat against Bricks's right knee and his own back against the wall—and pushed. Every thread of qi he had left. Not a strike. A *push*. Into the strained gray threads at the joint, all at once.

The threads slid off the knee like a rope off a post.

Bricks's right leg folded. With his fist still stuck in the wall and his left knee already weak, he had nothing left to stand on. He fell—slowly, heavily, the way a building falls—and his own weight dragged his fist out of the wall in a shower of concrete chips, and he hit the floor of the pit on his back with a sound like a truck being dropped from a crane.

The concrete under him cracked in a perfect circle.

He didn't get up.

He wasn't unconscious. Ye Xuan could see that. He was lying on his back, staring at the work lights on the ceiling, breathing hard. He just—wasn't getting up. His knees, Ye Xuan saw, were both flickering, the threads at the joints sliding slowly back into place. It would take a minute.

The pit was absolutely silent.

Three hundred people leaning over the railing. Not one of them making a sound.

Then the speaker crackled.

"*It's over,*" said Mama Rocha's voice. And then, after a pause, almost thoughtfully: "*Winner: Mott.*"

* * *

The noise that came down into the pit was not a roar. It was something stranger. Shouting, yes, but confused, angry, delighted, all at once. People who had bet on Bricks were screaming at the bookies. People who had taken the twenty-to-one on a whim were screaming with joy. Someone threw a beer can. Someone else threw a fistful of dollar bills, which fluttered down into the pit like confetti.

Ye Xuan barely heard it.

He walked over to Bricks and crouched down beside him.

Bricks looked at him. His face was very red. "What," he said slowly, "*was* that?"

"Your knees," Ye Xuan said quietly. "They hurt you. Every day. Don't they?"

Bricks stared at him.

"Your gift is too heavy for them," Ye Xuan said. "It's wearing them out. In a year, maybe two, you won't be able to walk." He hesitated. "I'm sorry. I didn't do it. I just used it."

He stood up and held out his hand.

Bricks looked at the hand for a long time. Then, slowly, he took it. Ye Xuan braced himself and pulled, and Bricks came up off the floor, heavy as a refrigerator, and stood swaying on his knees.

Above them, the crowd's noise changed again. Something like a cheer.

"Nobody ever told me that," Bricks muttered. "About the knees. Doctors just said, use the gift less." He looked at Ye Xuan with narrowed eyes. "How do you know?"

"I'm an engineering student," Ye Xuan said. "I know about load-bearing structures."

He climbed the ladder out of the pit.

* * *

Juno was at the top. She grabbed him by the front of his T-shirt and pulled him close and looked at his face—his nose, his eyes—with fierce, quick attention.

"Your nose is bleeding," she said.

"Only a little."

"Your eyes aren't."

"No."

She held onto his shirt a second longer than she needed to. Then she let go and punched his shoulder. The one Bricks had hit. He winced.

"That's for scaring me," Juno said.

Behind her, Tank was grinning so widely that his whole face seemed to have turned into a smile. Next to him stood a man who could only be Dez—Tank's size, Tank's face, a scar along his jaw from the broken bones—shaking his head slowly.

"Five hundred," said a voice.

One of Mama Rocha's bald men was standing beside them, holding out an envelope. Ye Xuan took it. It was thick.

He looked across the room.

The man in the green fleece jacket was still standing by the far railing. He wasn't writing on his phone anymore. He was holding it up, pointed straight at Ye Xuan.

Recording.
