He had four days until Saturday. He used every hour of them.

* * *

At dawn, the back row of Granny Liu's practice now had three people in it, and the grannies had stopped pretending not to be fascinated.

Juno came every morning. Tank came every morning, carrying coffee for everyone, including—after the second day—the grannies, who accepted it graciously and complained that it was too bitter. Granny Wu had decided that Tank was her favorite. Auntie Feng had decided that Juno had potential, if only she would stop moving like she was trying to punch the air.

Juno was getting better. Her forms were still terrible—she moved like a coiled spring trying to pretend it was a willow branch—but her breathing was changing. He could see it. Every morning, she gathered a few more threads than the day before. Two, then five, then eleven. On Thursday, she gathered twenty, and the dark, frayed patches on her copper coils had faded to a dull bronze.

"I can feel it," she whispered to him on Thursday, during the slow part of the form. "In the morning. Before I get up. It's like—warm. Down here." She touched her stomach. "Like I ate something hot."

"That's it," he whispered back. "That's exactly it."

"*No talking in the back row!*" said Auntie Feng, and blew her whistle.

* * *

At night, he sat with the stone.

He had a routine now. Window locked. Curtain drawn. Street checked—sometimes the sedan was there, sometimes not; he no longer let it stop him. Tin open, sock unwrapped, stone on the towel.

He never looked at it. He never needed to. He only listened—with the warmth in his dantian—for its slow, steady breath. In. Pause. Out. And when it breathed out, he breathed in, and the dense, bright qi rolled into him like warm water.

The second layer of Qi Gathering had doubled the size of his dantian. Every night, he filled it. Every night, he felt the walls of the jar stretching, slowly, under the pressure. But it wasn't enough to break through. He could feel that too. The jar was bigger, and it wanted more than he could give it in a single night.

*Water fills the river,* Granny Liu had said. *When the river is full, the bank gives way by itself.*

He was filling the river. The bank was still holding.

* * *

In the afternoons, Juno taught him the rooftops.

It started on Wednesday, when she found him on a bench in a playground in the Lower East Side, eating a sandwich between deliveries, and dropped down next to him from the top of a handball wall.

"If you're going to fight at the Pit," she said, "you need to know how to fall."

"I know how to fall."

"You know how to *land*. Badly. On fire escapes." She stood up. "Falling is different. Falling is what you do when somebody hits you so hard you go flying, and you have half a second to decide whether you break your wrist or roll. Come on."

She took him up the fire escape of a six-story building on Rivington Street, to a flat tar roof covered with old satellite dishes and pigeon feathers. And for an hour, she threw him.

Not hard. Not at first. She'd push him, and he'd stumble, and she'd say "Roll." Push. "Roll." Push. "No, not like that, you'll crack your skull. Tuck your chin." Push. "*Roll.*"

By the end of the hour, he could fall in any direction without hurting himself, and he hurt everywhere.

On Thursday, she taught him to move on the rooftops. Not with the gift—he didn't have her gift, and he couldn't afford to spend his qi on every jump. With his body. How to run along a parapet without looking down. How to climb a water tower. How to judge a gap: this one you can step across, this one you have to jump, this one you go around, this one you don't even think about.

"How do you know which ones?" he asked, standing on the edge of a roof on Delancey Street, looking at a gap that seemed to him exactly the same as the last three.

"Practice," said Juno. "Lots of falling. One broken collarbone."

"I'd rather not."

"Then watch."

And he watched her. Not with his sight. With his eyes. The way she moved, the way she judged distance, the small adjustments she made without thinking. She had spent two years on these rooftops. She knew them the way he knew the green waves on Broadway.

It was, he thought, the first thing in this world he had learned the ordinary way—by watching someone who was better than him, and trying, and failing, and trying again.

He liked it more than he had expected.

* * *

On Friday, the day before the Pit, she took him to Brooklyn.

They crossed the Manhattan Bridge on foot, on the pedestrian walkway, with the subway trains roaring past on the tracks beside them and the river glittering far below. On the other side, Juno led him through the streets of DUMBO to a building by the water—an old warehouse turned into apartments, eight stories, with a roof that looked straight across the river at the whole of Lower Manhattan.

They sat on the edge of the roof with their legs dangling over the side and watched the sun go down behind the towers.

"I come here sometimes," Juno said. "When I need to think. Nobody knows about it except Wren, and Wren knows about everything."

The sky was turning orange and pink. The glass towers of the Financial District caught the light and burned gold. The Brooklyn Bridge stretched across the water to their right, its great stone arches dark against the sky.

And the air—

Ye Xuan let his sight open, just a little.

The river was full of qi.

He had never seen it like this. In the parks, by the old trees, the threads drifted like slow snow. But here, above the moving water, they streamed—long, bright ribbons of light flowing with the current, gathering in the eddies under the bridge, rising off the surface like mist. Moving water, he realized. Moving water gathered qi the way a river gathers leaves, carrying it along, concentrating it.

And at sunset—at the moment when the day turned into night—the streams were brightest of all.

"Juno," he said quietly. "Can you give me some time? Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty."

She looked at him. Then at the river. She didn't ask what for.

"I'll keep watch," she said.

* * *

He sat cross-legged on the edge of the roof, facing the water, and closed his eyes.

He didn't take the stone out. He didn't need it here.

One, two, three—*fold*.

The qi came to him off the river in long, cool ribbons. Different from the stone's dense, warm rings. Different from the park's soft, drifting threads. River qi was fluid and fast. It wanted to keep moving. It slid into his dantian and swirled there, restless, like water poured into a bowl too quickly.

He let it swirl.

One, two, three—*fold*.

More came. And more. The sunset was deepening, the streams over the water were brightening, and he breathed them in, and the jar in his belly filled, and kept filling—not with the steady pressure of the stone's qi, but with a *current*, round and round, faster and faster, a whirlpool spinning inside him.

The walls began to stretch.

He felt them—the familiar ache, the tide against the seawall. But this time it was different. The whirlpool wasn't just pressing on the walls. It was *turning* them. Smoothing them. Shaping them the way a river shapes the stones in its bed over centuries.

*Water fills the river.*

*But water also carves it.*

He stopped trying to hold the qi still. He let it spin. He let it carve.

And the jar *opened*.

Not a click. Not a bloom. A *rush*—as if a dam had broken somewhere deep inside him, and all the spinning qi poured outward at once into a space three times as large as before. It flooded down his legs, up his spine, along his arms, into his fingers and toes, the soles of his feet, the crown of his head. His whole body felt for an instant like a riverbed in a spring flood.

When it settled, the world was different again.

He opened his eyes.

* * *

The sunset was almost gone. The towers of Manhattan were turning from gold to blue, their windows lighting up one by one. And he could see—*without trying*—the qi.

Not with the deep sight, the one that cost him blood. With his ordinary eyes, half-open, relaxed. The ribbons over the river. The threads drifting over the rooftops. Faint, like a watercolor wash over the evening. But there.

He looked at the bridge. Without effort, without the shift, he saw the faint lines of tension running through its cables—not deeply, not completely, but the way you might glance at a familiar map. He looked at a ferry crossing the river and saw, for a moment, the rhythm of its engine. He looked at Juno, sitting a few meters away, and saw the copper coils in her legs, bright and nearly whole, glowing gently in the dusk.

No pressure behind his eyes. No warmth in his nose.

*Skimming,* he thought, dazed. *I'm skimming without paying for it.*

"Ye?"

Juno was looking at him. Her face was strange in the blue twilight—surprised, a little awed.

"Your eyes," she said. "For a second. They were glowing. Like—a little bit gold."

He touched his face. His skin was warm.

"Third layer," he said. His voice came out hoarse, as if he hadn't used it in a long time. "Qi Gathering, third layer."

He didn't explain what it meant. He wasn't sure he could. But Juno looked at him for a long moment, and then she smiled—a slow, real smile, the first one he had seen on her face that didn't have an edge of something sharp in it.

"Okay," she said. "So tomorrow, at the Pit."

"Tomorrow at the Pit."

She looked out at the lights of Manhattan across the river.

"Try not to bleed from your eyes," she said.
