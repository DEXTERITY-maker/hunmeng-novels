The garage was dark and empty. Big Lou had gone home. Tank was on a late run to New Jersey. Wren's monitors glowed blue on her empty desk, her headphones hanging on the back of her chair.

Juno flicked on the light over the duct-taped couch and pointed at it.

"Sit."

He sat. She disappeared into Big Lou's glass office and came back with a first aid kit the size of a suitcase. It was old and dented and had clearly been used a great deal. She sat on the coffee table in front of him, opened it, and took out cotton, antiseptic and a bag of frozen peas from a mini-fridge.

"Head," she said.

He bent his head. She cleaned the scrape on the back of his skull with quick, efficient hands, ignoring his hiss when the antiseptic touched it.

"You've done this a lot," he said.

"Couriers fall off things." She pressed a gauze pad against the scrape. "Hold that. Now look up."

He looked up. She tilted his chin to the light and studied the marks on his throat, red and swelling. Her face was very close. She smelled like the city—exhaust, wind, a little sweat—and something sweet underneath, like vanilla.

"He could have crushed your windpipe," she said quietly.

"He didn't."

"Because I came." She handed him the bag of frozen peas. "For your throat. Ten minutes on, ten off."

He held the peas against his neck. They were very cold. Juno sat back on the coffee table and crossed her arms, and the efficient medic vanished. The look she gave him now was the look from the fire escape. The look of someone who had been patient for two weeks and had just run out.

"Okay," she said. "Now you talk."

* * *

"About what?"

"Don't." Her voice was flat. "Don't do the 'I'm just an engineering student' thing. I've watched you for two weeks. I saw you take the Battery Park test, and I *know* nobody does that on foot. I watched you on the loading docks in the Meatpacking. I watched you tonight. You saw Dee's flash coming before she took off her glasses. You knew Joey's arms would snap back. You knew where to grab Rocco." She leaned forward. "And three weeks ago, you copied my step. My step, Ye. The thing that took me two years and a broken collarbone. You looked at me once, from the street, and you did it."

The peas were melting against his neck.

He thought about Rule seven. *Nobody learns about the stone.*

But Rule seven was about the stone. It wasn't about *him*.

"I can see how gifts work," he said.

Juno didn't say anything. She waited.

"It's—hard to explain." He put the peas down. "When I look at something, really look, I can see how it works. Not just the outside. The inside. Like—like seeing the wiring behind a wall. A phone, an engine, a traffic light. I see the parts and how they fit together and why." He hesitated. "And people with gifts. I can see the gifts. They look like… patterns. Lines of light inside the body. Each one is different. Kroll's are like chains. Rocco's are like steel cables in his arms. Yours…"

"Mine," Juno said, very quietly.

"Yours are springs. Copper-colored. In your calves and the soles of your feet. When you run, they wind tighter with every step. One, two, three. On three, you breathe in—short, sharp—and it squeezes the coils even tighter. On four, you let the breath out and the coils snap open at the same time, and for a second the air under your feet is solid." He paused. "Your left side is a little weaker than your right. You favor the right foot when you push. I think—maybe it's from when you broke your collarbone. You landed on your left side, and you've been protecting it ever since, without knowing."

Juno stared at him.

Her face had gone very still. Her lips were slightly parted.

"Nobody knows that," she said. "About the left side. I've never told anyone. Not Lou. Not Tank. Not even my—" She stopped.

"I'm not reading your mind," Ye Xuan said quickly. "I can't do that. I just see the pattern. It's lopsided. Like a spring that's been wound unevenly."

"And you looked at the pattern," she said slowly, "and you understood it, and you copied it."

"Not the gift. I can't copy the gift. It's yours. I copied the *rhythm*. The technique. The way you use it." He rubbed his face. "I don't know how to explain this part. I have… something. Not a gift. Something else. Energy. A little. I can push it into my legs and use your rhythm, and it works, sort of. A worse version. I nearly broke my neck the first time."

"On the fire escape."

"On the fire escape."

For a long time, Juno didn't say anything. She sat on the coffee table with her arms crossed, looking at him, and he could almost see her turning it over in her mind—the impossible thing he had just said, and all the evidence for it, and how much she wanted to believe it was a lie.

"Is that why you can't stand to look at people sometimes?" she said finally. "You do this thing. You look at someone and then you look away really fast, like you touched a hot stove."

He hadn't known it was that obvious.

"It costs something," he admitted. "Looking. If I look too hard, or too long, my nose bleeds. My head hurts. Like tonight." He touched the dried blood on his upper lip. "Living things are harder than machines. Gifts are harder than people."

Juno nodded slowly. Then she laughed—a short, disbelieving breath.

"I knew it," she said. "I knew there was something. Wren said you have two heartbeats. Tank said you smell like the park after rain, which is a really weird thing for Tank to say. Lou said you run like you've got a map in your head that the rest of us don't have." She shook her head. "But *this*—"

"You can't tell anyone."

"Who would I tell? *Hey, Lou, the new kid sees glowing springs in my legs.* He'd send me to a doctor." She looked at him. "But I'm going to ask you something, and you're going to answer honestly. Okay?"

"Okay."

"When you look at my legs," Juno said, "what else do you see?"

* * *

He had hoped she wouldn't ask.

He had seen it in the alley—the trembling coils, the dark frayed edges. He had been seeing it, he realized, for two weeks, every time he let his sight skim over her on the rooftops. He just hadn't let himself think about it.

"They're damaged," he said.

Juno's face didn't change. She had expected it.

"How bad?"

He looked. Properly, this time, the way he had looked at Kroll, at Rocco. He let his sight sink down into the bright copper springs coiled through her calves and the soles of her feet, and he made himself see them clearly.

They were beautiful. Even now. Tightly wound, intricate, alive—a gift that had been shaped by two years of hard practice into something precise and strong.

But they were wearing out.

All along the coils, here and there, the bright copper had gone dark. Not just at the edges anymore. In places, deeper. The coils were fraying the way old electrical wire frays when it's been bent too many times, and in the worst places—in her left calf, especially—strands had come loose entirely and hung there, tangled, sparking faintly when they touched each other. Every time she used her gift, he saw, the coils pulled energy from somewhere. From her muscles. From her bones. From her own body. They burned it like fuel and gave nothing back.

"They hurt," he said. "Don't they. At night. After a long day."

Juno looked away.

"Since about six months ago," she said. "Before that, just after really long runs. Now… every night. Like cramps. But deeper. Like the bones are hot." She rubbed her left calf without seeming to notice she was doing it. "Sometimes my legs shake in the morning before I get up. I have to wait for them to stop."

"Have you seen a doctor?"

"An Awakened doctor costs three hundred dollars just to look at you. And you know what they say?" She laughed without humor. "Burnout. *Rest. Use your gift less.* That's the treatment. That's the whole treatment. Nobody knows how to fix it. Nobody even really knows what it is."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Lou had a runner before me. Ricky. Gift kind of like mine, only he could stick to walls. Best runner in the city. Last year, his legs started shaking. He kept running because he had a kid. Then one morning he woke up and he couldn't feel his feet." Her voice was very even. "The doctors said the gift had—crystallized. That's the word they used. His legs are like stone now. Below the knee. He's in a wheelchair in Staten Island. He was twenty-four."

Ye Xuan said nothing. There was nothing to say.

"Awakened couriers burn out by twenty-five," Juno said. "Everybody knows that. It's like a joke in the business. You make good money for a few years and then you're done." She shrugged. "I'm nineteen. So I've got a few years."

"Then why keep running?"

She looked at him then. Straight at him, her dark eyes hard.

"Because my little brother has asthma," she said. "Bad asthma. The kind where you go to the emergency room twice a month and sometimes they keep you overnight. He's nine. His name is Mateo. Our mom died three years ago. Our aunt Carmen helps, but she cleans offices at night and she's got her own kids. His medicine costs more than our rent. The specialist he needs costs more than that." Her jaw set. "So I run. And I make good money. And when my legs give out, I'll have enough saved that Mateo can breathe for a few more years, and that's the deal. That's the deal I made."

She stood up and started packing the first aid kit, briskly, the conversation clearly over.

"Keep the peas," she said. "And go home. Rocco's going to need a few days, but he'll be back, and next time he'll bring more than four."

* * *

Ye Xuan looked at her back. At her legs, in their dusty jeans, standing very straight. At the dark, frayed copper inside them that only he could see.

He thought about the knot in Rocco's elbow. About pushing a thread of qi into a pattern and watching it stutter.

*If I can jam a pattern,* he thought slowly, *if I can make it tangle—*

*Then maybe I can do the opposite.*

"Juno," he said.

"What?"

"Can I try something?"
