The next night, Juno brought a flashlight, a rope, and a granola bar.

"For after," she said, shoving the granola bar into his jacket pocket. "When you're bleeding from your eyes again."

"I'm going to be more careful."

"You said that yesterday."

They were standing at the gap in the railing at the end of Pier 17, looking down. The ladder was old iron, rusted orange, its rungs slick with river damp. It went down about four meters to a narrow wooden walkway that ran along the side of the pier just above the waterline—a maintenance catwalk, Juno said, for workers who checked the pilings. Below the catwalk, the black water slapped quietly against the wood.

Ye Xuan went down first.

The iron was cold and wet under his hands. Halfway down, the light from the pier's lamps disappeared and the world became the beam of Juno's flashlight from above, swinging across rust and wood and water. He felt for the catwalk with his foot, found it, and let go of the ladder.

A second later, Juno dropped down beside him without touching a single rung. Her sneakers hit the catwalk without a sound.

"Show-off," he said.

"Efficient."

* * *

Under the pier, it was another world.

The pilings stood in rows, hundreds of them, thick wooden posts black with age and slime, disappearing into the water. Between them, the river moved in slow, dark currents. The air smelled of salt and rot and diesel. Above them, the underside of the pier was a ceiling of beams and planks, dripping. Every sound echoed—the water, their breathing, a car passing on the FDR Drive far away.

Juno swept the flashlight across the pilings. The beam caught a plastic bag snagged on a post, a beer can, the white flash of a fish turning under the surface.

"He hid here," Ye Xuan said quietly. "The other—I hid here. After the ladder."

He could feel it, even without his sight. The pressure was weaker down here than above, but it was still present, like an echo of an echo. He let his sight open, carefully, just a skim, and saw it at once: a faint trail of impressions on the catwalk and the pilings. A boy's pattern, flickering with fear, moving along the catwalk, then off it—into the water, to the waist—deeper under the pier, between the pilings. Hiding. Waiting.

He followed the trail with his eyes until it stopped at a piling about ten meters in, where a crossbeam ran between two posts just above the waterline.

"There," he said. "He stood there. In the water. For a long time."

"How long?"

"I don't know. Long." He looked at the impressions: dense, layered, overlapping, a boy standing very still in cold black water while something terrible happened above him. "Long enough to be very cold."

Juno pointed the flashlight at the crossbeam. The beam slid over slime and barnacles and a coil of old rope—

—and caught something that flashed.

Copper.

* * *

It was wedged in the angle where the crossbeam met the piling, half-buried in a mat of river weed. Ye Xuan saw it and knew what it was before Juno's light had finished sweeping over it.

"The box," he said.

He was in the water before Juno could stop him.

It was shockingly cold—a cold that went straight through his jeans and into his bones, the October river already thinking about winter. It came up to his waist. The bottom under his feet was soft, slimy, uneven. He waded between the pilings with his arms raised, toward the crossbeam, while Juno swore at him from the catwalk in two languages and held the flashlight steady.

He reached up and pulled the box free.

It was small. Smaller than his hand. A rectangular case made of copper—not solid copper, but a fine latticework of copper wire soldered together in a pattern so intricate it looked like lace. One corner was crushed. The lid hung by a single hinge. River weed was tangled through the lattice. But the pattern was still there, and as he held it, he saw it clearly with his ordinary eyes.

It was almost the pattern from Crane's sketch. Almost the pattern he had seen in the stone's rings. Not quite right—the spacing was uneven in places, and there were gaps where the lattice should have been dense—but close. So close. Someone had looked at a ballpoint sketch drawn by a dying man and turned it into this, by hand, with a soldering iron, over weeks.

*The best one yet,* the other Ye Xuan had written. *Seventy percent.*

"You were good," Ye Xuan said softly to no one. "You were really good."

"Ye!" Juno's voice from the catwalk, sharp. "You're standing in the East River in October. Talk to the box *later*."

He waded back. She grabbed his collar and hauled him up onto the catwalk like a wet cat, then stood there with her hands on her hips while he dripped and shivered.

"Hypothermia," she said. "That's what you're going to get. And then I'm going to have to explain to Lou why his new runner died of being stupid."

"Five minutes," he said through chattering teeth. "There's something else. I felt it last night. I need to try once more."

"Ye—"

"Five minutes. Please."

Juno looked at him for a long moment. Then she sighed, took off her red jacket, and threw it over his shoulders.

"Five," she said. "And then I carry you up the ladder, and you don't get a vote."

* * *

He sat on the wet catwalk with his back against a piling, the copper box in his lap, and looked up at the underside of the pier.

Directly above him—directly above the place where the boy had stood in the water—was the spot where Crane had died. He could see it even through the planks. The flash had burned down through the wood, through the beams, and the underside of the pier there was scorched black in the same branching, lightning-tree pattern as the post above.

And the impressions here were different.

Above, on the pier itself, they had been a crowd: Crane, the boy, Ash, the two others, all layered over each other and blasted through with the white flash. Down here, the flash was still there, but the others were faint. Just Crane. Just the last of him, burning down through the wood like water soaking through paper.

And under the flash, deep and quiet, the thing Ye Xuan had felt last night.

He let his sight sink into it. Slowly. Gently. Ignoring the drum that had started again behind his eyes.

The flash was too bright to look at directly, so he looked past it. Around it. The way you can see a faint star better by looking slightly to one side of it. And there, at the very edge of the burning, he found it.

Not a pattern. Not quite. A shape left in the wood by something that had passed through it at the very last moment. A thought. A last, desperate thought, pressed into the world so hard that the world had kept it.

It didn't come as words. It came as a feeling, a meaning, an understanding—the way the stone's rings had come, the way the phone's lock had come. But if it had been words, they would have been these:

*Seed Zero.*

*Run, kid. Don't let them plant it.*

*They'll open the—*

And then the flash, and the white, and nothing.

* * *

He came out of it slowly this time. Not thrown. Just—surfacing, like a swimmer coming up from deep water.

His nose was bleeding. His eyes felt hot, but when he touched them, his fingers came away clean. Better than last night. His head was pounding like a festival drum.

Juno was crouched in front of him with the flashlight pointed at his face.

"Well?" she said.

"Seed Zero," Ye Xuan said. His voice sounded far away to his own ears. "He was thinking about Seed Zero. *Don't let them plant it.* And then something about opening. *They'll open the*—and then it stops."

"Seed Zero? What does that mean?"

"I don't know." He looked down at the copper box in his lap. "But I think it's the name of the thing in my pocket."

Juno didn't say anything for a while.

"Plant it," she said finally. "Like a seed? Plant it where?"

"I don't know."

"Who's 'them'?"

He thought of the glass column in the Halcyon lobby. The crystals inside it brightening, all at once, turning toward his stone like faces turning toward a sound. The plaque. *The Gift of the Sky.*

*Don't let Halcyon have it,* Crane had written. *Whatever they offer you.*

"I think I know who," he said.

* * *

He tried to stand up and discovered that his legs didn't work.

They were numb from the cold, and the drum in his head had reached the part of the festival where everyone hits everything at once. He got halfway up, swayed, and the catwalk tilted sideways under him.

Juno caught him.

"Okay," she said. "Five minutes are over. I'm carrying you. Hold on to the box."

"You said you weren't going to carry me."

"I lied." She got her shoulder under his arm, wrapped her other arm around his waist, and lifted him as if he weighed nothing. He forgot sometimes how strong she was. "Hold on."

She took the ladder in three jumps—one, two, three, *four*—each one pushing off the rusted iron rungs and the air beside them, and they came up out of the dark into the lamplight at the end of the pier.

And something buzzed above them.

Juno froze. Ye Xuan looked up.

It hung about ten meters above the end of the pier, perfectly still despite the wind off the river: a small black drone, four rotors humming, a single red light blinking on its underside. A camera lens on a little gimbal turned slowly, following them.

On its side, in the lamplight, a small symbol caught the light. A golden sunburst.

"Oh, *hell* no," Juno said.

She let go of him—he sat down abruptly on the planks—took two running steps along the pier, and jumped.

One, two, three, *four*.

She went straight up. Ten meters, in one leap, her body uncoiling like a spring released, her arm reaching—and she caught the drone by one of its rotor arms and pulled it down with her as she fell.

She landed in a crouch on the new yellow planks. The drone buzzed and whined in her hands, its rotors spinning against nothing.

Juno looked at it. Then she turned and threw it, overhand, as hard as she could, out over the railing and into the black East River.

It hit the water with a small splash and sank.

She turned back to him, breathing hard, her braid swinging, eyes blazing.

"Okay," she said. "Now somebody knows we were here."
