Agent Priya Shah chose a diner where the coffee tasted like scorched cardboard and the booths were too narrow for anyone to hide a weapon comfortably.

"That is not why I chose it," she said when Ye Xuan pointed out the booth dimensions.

"Then why?"

"Because nobody from the Bureau will willingly eat here."

She pushed a folder across the table. Its corners were bent, and somebody had written PERSONAL NOTES in black ink on the cover.

Ye Xuan did not open it.

"You're suspended," he said.

"Two weeks without pay. Kroll told you?"

"He came to the bakery."

"Of course he did." Priya looked briefly amused, then not amused at all. "He bought pastries, didn't he?"

"Two boxes."

"He's worried."

"About you."

"He's right to be."

The waitress refilled their mugs. They waited until she had gone.

"Respira," Ye Xuan said.

Priya's expression changed.

* * *

He showed her the photograph Carmen had allowed him to take, with the identifying details of the family hidden.

Priya read the form once. Then again, more slowly. She underlined a phrase with her finger.

"Proprietary particulate carrier."

"What does that mean?"

"It means they're not telling parents what the particles are, at least not in this summary. They may disclose more in the full protocol, but that needs checking."

"Why would particles be useful for asthma?"

"There are legitimate experimental approaches to drug delivery." She tapped the page. "But Halcyon's primary research division doesn't manufacture conventional inhalers."

"What does it manufacture?"

Priya opened her folder.

Inside were printed shipping manifests, public filings, and a copy of a requisition record. No classified stamp. She had made a point of keeping only material she could explain possessing after her suspension.

"Heartstone fragments," she said.

Ye Xuan felt the floor of the diner become distant.

He remembered Seed Zero pressing cold against his palm, the echo of Silas Crane at Pier 17, the warning: Don't let them plant it.

"Fragments," he repeated.

"Crystallized material recovered from Glow sites. Halcyon calls them luminous substrates. Most pieces are tiny. They're unstable, and they produce responses in living tissue that their published papers don't explain."

He studied a shipping date.

It was two days before the first round of Respira invitations had gone out in the Bronx.

"You're saying the trial uses those pieces?"

"I'm saying the shipment and the study have overlapping personnel, facilities, and accounting codes. That's a trail, not proof." Priya looked him in the eye. "If you're going to help that family, don't confuse those."

Ye Xuan nodded.

Rule seven: a pattern was not the same as an explanation.

* * *

Priya had one more document: a heavily redacted safety summary filed with a university review board.

The first six pages described respiratory outcomes, participant protections, and adverse-event reporting. The seventh referred to a secondary endpoint: post-exposure resonance stability.

"Resonance with what?" Ye Xuan asked.

"Good question."

"Do the parents know about that?"

"They should have access to an explanation of the actual procedures and risks. Whether they got one is another question." Priya leaned back. "I tried to request the complete protocol before they suspended me. Somebody noticed."

"Kroll?"

"Not Kroll. If it had been Kroll, I would have received a lecture instead of a reprimand."

Ye Xuan stared at the words resonance stability.

His copper plate protected him because the stone had a pulse. If the fragments were pieces of that same phenomenon, perhaps they also pulsed. A particle lodged in the body might not simply sit there. It might try to become part of a larger circuit.

He thought of Mateo's cold imbalance.

"What if those particles are being used as something other than medicine?"

Priya watched him very carefully.

"Do you know something I don't?"

"I saw a child who might be connected to the trial."

"Might?"

"The child's family hasn't enrolled. I saw something unusual, but I can't identify it yet."

Priya put her cup down. "Then don't turn a suspicion into a diagnosis. And don't try experimental fixes on the child."

"I won't."

"Good." She took back the safety summary. "There may be a legitimate study wrapped around something very ugly. We need to separate them before we act."

* * *

They spent the next hour building a map with names and dates. Halcyon Dynamics funded Respira through a foundation. The foundation contracted a clinic network. One of its investigators appeared on a materials-research agreement with Hudson Polytechnic.

Professor Elias Ward.

Ye Xuan kept his face still.

"You know him," Priya said.

"He teaches my class. I started working in his lab."

"Of course you did." She rubbed her forehead. "You have a gift for walking into the worst possible rooms."

"I don't have a gift."

"That's the part that worries me most."

She drew two boxes on a napkin: RESPIRA and ASCENSION. A third box connected them, marked UNVERIFIED.

"Project Ascension isn't something I can prove in court," she said. "The name appears in procurement references and budget transfers. If they're trying to give people abilities by implanting crystal material, a respiratory trial might be one way to test whether the body tolerates it."

"And if it doesn't?"

Priya didn't answer immediately.

"That's why trials have ethical review, transparent consent, and independent monitoring. That's why secrecy matters."

Ye Xuan remembered the voices at the Pit talking about fighters who left with Halcyon and never returned.

"What do we do?"

"First, the family gets the full study information and asks questions through their doctor. Second, I keep tracing documents that are legal for me to access. Third, you do not break into a laboratory or touch restricted materials."

He looked at her.

"That last one was oddly specific."

"Because I met you."

* * *

The diner was filling with office workers by the time they left. Priya paid cash and refused his offer to contribute.

"Save your money for tuition. And don't send me sensitive information over ordinary messages."

"You sound like Kroll."

"That's a terrible thing to say to somebody who's just bought you breakfast."

Outside, a pale morning sun showed through the buildings. Traffic crawled past, horns sounding in impatient succession.

Priya paused at the curb.

"Ye. That boy. Mateo, was it?"

He looked at her.

"I haven't given you the family name."

"You said his sister works at Skyline. I know more about their drivers than Lou would like." She caught herself and shook her head. "Sorry. Habit. I didn't look at his medical records."

"Good."

She met his gaze. "If anything changes, tell me. And if a doctor tells them there's a safe, evidence-based option, don't let our suspicions delay it."

He watched her disappear into the crowd.

* * *

At Hudson Polytechnic, Ward had left him a new stack of samples. He worked in silence, recording measurements and rejecting the temptation to look through every container with comprehension.

That was the difficulty with a power that could show him what ordinary tools could not. It encouraged certainty before he had earned it.

At four, an unfamiliar woman wearing a Halcyon visitor badge passed the laboratory door. She stopped beside Ward's office and tapped twice.

Ward came out to meet her.

Through the glass, Ye Xuan saw her present a sealed envelope. Ward took it without smiling.

"Tuesday," she said. "The medical review wants preliminary characterization by Tuesday."

"We haven't received the samples."

"You have now."

Her gaze passed over Ye Xuan and paused on the small copper-colored mark near his thumbnail.

He lowered his hand beneath the bench.

As she left, her phone lit for a moment. A photograph filled its screen before she locked it.

The picture showed the concrete floor of the Pit and a skinny fighter standing over Bricks.

It showed Ye Xuan.
