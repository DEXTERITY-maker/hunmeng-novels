Mrs. Tang had decided that Sunday would be a day of dumplings.

Nobody was asked whether they wanted dumplings. Nobody was allowed to bring food except Auntie Feng, who had ignored that rule before Mrs. Tang finished announcing it. At eleven, the Golden Lotus smelled of ginger, garlic, fresh dough, and a dozen overlapping opinions about the correct thickness of a wrapper.

Ye Xuan arrived carrying spring onions and a bag of oranges.

"You're late," Mrs. Tang said.

"It's ten fifty-eight."

"Late for chopping."

She pointed him toward the kitchen.

He obeyed.

* * *

By noon, the back room was full.

Granny Liu sat near the window correcting Tank's dumpling folds. Big Lou had taken the largest chair and was pretending not to be fascinated by Tommy's explanations of superhero rankings. Wren wore her headphones around her neck, occasionally looking up as if she could hear a delivery problem three blocks away.

Juno arrived with Mateo and Carmen.

Mateo looked better than he had in the apartment. He still tired easily, and Carmen carried the medication and medical plan his doctor had provided, but he was smiling and arguing with Tommy about whether Zenith Prime could outrun a train.

"Depends on the train," Ye Xuan said.

Both boys turned on him as if he had committed a personal betrayal.

"Not you too," Tommy groaned.

"Physics doesn't take weekends off."

"It should," Mateo said.

Juno set her jacket over a chair and caught Ye Xuan looking at her brother.

"He's okay," she said quietly. "Doctor's follow-up is next week. We're keeping to the plan."

"Good."

"And we got more information about the Respira study."

"Good?"

"Not good." Her expression darkened. "They say the particles are proprietary. Carmen asked for the full protocol and an independent explanation of the risks. We're waiting."

Ye Xuan nodded.

He wanted to tell her about the broken fragments in Ward's lab. Not at the table, not with two boys fighting over imaginary flight speeds.

Later, he decided.

* * *

The dumplings were folded in four different styles. Granny Liu declared three of them unacceptable. Mrs. Tang declared that anyone who complained would have to wash dishes.

The room became peaceful at once.

Ye Xuan sat between Tank and Juno, eating more than he had intended. Food still tasted unexpectedly vivid to him. Ginger, vinegar, pork, the warmth of a bowl in his hands. He had once eaten meals like this in Hangzhou without noticing the separate ingredients.

In his first life, home had been something he assumed would still be there after the next examination.

In his second, he knew better.

He thought of his mother in a kitchen thousands of kilometers away, or perhaps in another world altogether, depending on what the bell had done. The thought settled behind his ribs with a weight no cultivation layer could lift.

Mrs. Tang placed another dumpling in his bowl.

"Eat," she said.

He did.

* * *

After lunch, Lou took Tank and Tommy to carry boxes from the storeroom. Wren helped Carmen find the nearest bus route to Mateo's clinic. The grannies argued about whether a person who could fly still needed to exercise his legs.

Juno stayed to wash dishes.

Ye Xuan dried them.

For a while the only sound was running water and the soft collision of ceramic bowls.

"I heard you won again," she said.

"I was there when you heard it."

"Yes, but I'm bringing it up now."

"Strategic."

"Very."

She passed him a bowl. "Iron Night. You're thinking about it."

"Five thousand dollars."

"That's not an answer."

"That's most of my tuition problem."

She shut off the tap.

"Ye. You can be clever enough to beat a man who weighs as much as a truck and still be stupid enough to let somebody kill you over a bill."

He looked at the drying rack. Every bowl had a place. Every plate leaned against another.

"I'm not fighting for the bill alone."

"Then what?"

He could have answered with Mateo, or Ward's samples, or Priya's papers. All would have been true in pieces.

"I don't want to spend this life running from people who can buy it," he said.

Juno dried her hands slowly.

"Then don't make it easy for them to take."

* * *

Mrs. Tang entered carrying a small wooden box. It was old and polished smooth at the corners. She set it on the table between them.

"I heard enough," she said.

Juno looked embarrassed. Ye Xuan did not move.

"Neither of you can whisper," Mrs. Tang continued. "You talk like people hiding from an army. Sit down."

They sat.

She opened the box.

Inside lay a wedding photograph, a yellowed train ticket, and a watch with a cracked face. The man in the picture had a kind smile and an awkward posture, as if he did not know where to put his hands.

"My husband," she said. "Wei."

Ye Xuan had heard her mention him before. He had not known his name.

"He was not a hero. He carried sacks of flour, fixed the ovens, argued about football, and forgot our anniversary twice." Her mouth twitched. "Then the sky turned white."

She touched the watch.

"After the Glow, he could make things warm. Bread, tea, a cold room in winter. Small things. Nothing like the people on television. For a while it made him happy. He could warm a child's hands without making a fire."

Her voice stayed steady.

"Then people started asking him for more."

* * *

At first, the requests had been harmless. Warm a sick neighbor's room. Keep the bakery oven working during a blackout. Help the elderly woman upstairs through a freezing night.

Wei had liked being useful. He had liked the gratitude. He had not known how to say no.

As the years passed, his gift became harder to use. It left him shaking, exhausted, cold despite the heat he made. Doctors told him to rest. Some people told him to train. A man from a research company offered him a program that could make his gift stronger.

"He refused that man," Mrs. Tang said. "But he did not refuse the people who needed him. He was afraid that if he stopped helping, he would become less than he had been."

She closed the box.

"One winter he used the gift too long. I found him on the kitchen floor. He was breathing, but he could not get warm again."

Juno reached for her hand.

Mrs. Tang let her.

"We did everything the doctors told us. It wasn't enough." She looked at Ye Xuan. "I am not telling you this so that you pity me. I'm telling you because I recognize the way you count other people's troubles as if they are all yours."

He opened his mouth.

She raised a finger.

"Do not deny it. I am old, not blind."

* * *

The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, a customer rang the bell at the front counter, but Tommy was there to help.

Mrs. Tang studied Ye Xuan for a long moment.

"I know you are not an ordinary student," she said.

His hand went instinctively toward the copper box.

"I don't know what you do. I don't want to know everything. But I know how people look at you. The federal agent who comes here pretending he wants buns. The strangers who walk past three times without buying anything. The girl who arrives with bruises and watches the door."

Juno looked at the table.

Mrs. Tang continued, quieter now.

"If you have something dangerous, do not bring danger into this house without telling me. If you are hurt, don't hide it until the floor has to tell me. And if you can help someone, do not believe that means you must destroy yourself to do it."

Ye Xuan thought about Wei, a man he had never met, and the cracked watch in the box.

He had written so many rules in his notebook. Rules about the stone. Rules about the Bureau. Rules about food.

None of them covered this.

"I'll try," he said.

Mrs. Tang narrowed her eyes.

"Try harder."

He smiled despite himself.

"Yes, Mrs. Tang."

* * *

Later, with the kitchen clean and the last guests preparing to leave, Mateo fell asleep in an armchair. Tommy tucked a blanket around him with a seriousness he never showed toward homework.

Lou counted the remaining buns. Wren found two unclaimed dumplings and claimed them. The grannies went home complaining that young people did not know how to stand straight.

Ye Xuan stood at the back door with Juno.

"She likes you," Juno said.

"She tells me to wash dishes."

"Exactly."

He looked toward the sleeping boy and the photograph Mrs. Tang had left beside the wooden box.

For a little while, the world seemed no larger than the room behind them.

It was enough.

Then the bell at the front door rang.

Not the one beneath the world. An ordinary brass bell, cheerful and completely unaware of the history it was announcing.

A delivery rider handed Tommy an envelope and left without waiting for a signature.

The envelope carried a gold sunburst.

Mrs. Tang took it from Tommy before he could open it. She read the printed name.

"Ye Xuan," she said.

He crossed the room.

Inside lay a single white card.

HALCYON DYNAMICS.
PRIVATE RESEARCH OPPORTUNITY.

On the reverse, beneath a date and a Midtown address, someone had written a sentence by hand:

We believe you can hear what the others cannot.

Ye Xuan felt the copper box warm in his pocket.

For the first time, he understood that hiding Seed Zero had not ended the search.

It had merely changed the question his pursuers were asking.
