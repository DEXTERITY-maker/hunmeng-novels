The case arrived at Hudson Polytechnic on a gray Tuesday morning, accompanied by two couriers who carried themselves like security officers.

It was not large. A black hard-shell container, perhaps thirty centimeters wide, secured by two steel latches and an electronic seal. The gold sunburst on its lid was barely visible beneath a white laboratory sticker.

Professor Ward signed for it without inviting the couriers inside.

"Temperature logs?" he asked.

The taller one presented a tablet.

"Chain of custody?"

Another signature field appeared.

Ward inspected the data, signed only after reading each line, and waited until the pair had left the corridor before turning to Ye Xuan.

"You can prepare the standard optical instruments," he said. "The actual sample handling is mine."

Ye Xuan nodded.

He was grateful for the division of work. He had spent all weekend thinking about Priya's warnings, and the copper box in his bag was warm against his side.

* * *

They began with an empty reference holder. Ward checked the spectrometer Ye Xuan had repaired, then calibrated a second instrument against a control standard.

Every step was recorded.

The professor did not behave like a man eager to hide evidence. He behaved like a scientist trying very hard not to make a mistake, which was different.

"The material has unusual behavior under illumination," Ward said. "Our contract requires composition estimates, thermal response, and stability observations. We will not make assumptions beyond the data."

"Do we know where it came from?"

"A licensed supplier. That's what the paperwork says."

"Does it say where the supplier found it?"

Ward looked at him over his glasses.

"That's a question I asked, too."

He unlocked the first latch. Inside the case was another container, transparent and sealed, with a warning about particulate exposure. In its center lay a few dark grains suspended in a gel-like medium.

They did not glow.

To ordinary sight, the contents resembled black sand caught in clear resin.

Ye Xuan felt a bell strike somewhere behind his ribs.

The copper plate answered with a brief warmth.

He did not open his sight yet.

* * *

Ward inserted the sample holder into the instrument chamber and dimmed the laboratory lights. The spectrometer traced a sequence of measurements across its monitor.

At first the data looked ordinary: noisy peaks, a weak absorption band, minor drift. Then a line appeared that the machine refused to fit.

Ward ran the calibration again.

The line returned in the same place.

"An instrumental artifact," Ward said.

"Could be."

"It should not be."

He moved to the second device and repeated the test with a different sensor. The line was still there, though its measured shape changed.

Ye Xuan leaned closer.

"May I see the raw output?"

Ward clicked through the files.

The anomaly was not in one wavelength or one temperature range. It appeared in the relationships between measurements, in the timing of the small deviations.

The machine had been asked to describe the material as if it were a passive object.

The material did not appear to be passive.

Ye Xuan looked.

* * *

The world shifted.

The black grains became splinters of impossible geometry. Each contained threads of pale blue and white, twisted so tightly that the lines seemed to pass through themselves. They were not whole patterns. Their ends had been severed and then forced to continue along paths that no longer existed.

He heard sound without hearing it: short, broken notes, each one searching for a missing answer.

Screaming.

His first instinct was to cover his ears. He knew it would do nothing. The noise was in the structure of the qi itself, like a song cut into pieces that kept trying to reach its next measure.

Pain struck behind his eyes.

He shut his sight at once.

"Ye?" Ward asked. "Are you unwell?"

"Just a headache."

"You look pale."

"Didn't sleep enough."

It was not a good answer. It was also not entirely false.

Ward gestured toward the sink. "Water. Sit down if you need to."

Ye Xuan obeyed.

He held the paper cup with both hands until they stopped shaking.

* * *

He knew something now that he had not understood at Pier 17.

Seed Zero was whole. Its pattern folded back into itself without loose ends, an intricate closed structure with layers beyond his reach. The fragments on Ward's table were not smaller whole stones. They were pieces torn from some larger arrangement.

Every severed line pointed outward, toward geometry that should have continued elsewhere.

The difference mattered.

One stone could be an object. Many fragments arranged by the same absent pattern could be evidence of a mechanism.

He thought of the Glow, the seven minutes of white sky, and the warning in Silas Crane's echo.

Don't let them plant it.

What would happen if someone tried to reconnect the fragments inside living tissue?

The thought made his stomach tighten.

* * *

Ward returned to the instrument and studied the unresolved peaks. He printed the output, placed two sheets side by side, and drew a faint line between corresponding anomalies.

"There's a repeating structure," he said.

"Yes."

"You saw it too?"

"In the data."

Ward watched him for an uncomfortable second.

"You keep saying things like that, Ye. More confidently than your experience suggests you should."

"I could be wrong."

"Good. Remember that."

He tapped the chart. "The supplier calls this a stability assessment. But the questions in their protocol are not the questions I would ask about a stable material."

"What would you ask?"

"Whether the sample is safe to handle, whether it changes in storage, whether the measurements can be repeated. They want to know whether the material responds to biological tissue analogues."

Ye Xuan looked toward the closed case.

"Why?"

"I intend to ask."

"Will they answer?"

Ward gave a tired smile. "Industry rarely pays for questions it doesn't already like."

He locked the samples away and sealed the day's log.

* * *

At the end of his shift, Ye Xuan stood outside Ward's office with his backpack over one shoulder. He wanted to tell the professor about the screams, the stone, the fragments reaching toward missing geometry.

He could not explain any of it without revealing what had happened at Pier 17.

And if Ward reported that he had access to an intact heartstone, the next visitor might not be a polite scout with a phone.

"Professor," he said instead, "if the sample is used in a medical study, would the university review the safety data?"

Ward's expression hardened.

"What medical study?"

"I heard Halcyon has one."

"Halcyon has several. That's not an answer."

"Respira."

Ward went still.

He took off his glasses, folded them, and placed them on the desk.

"I have not been given any evidence that my research samples are part of a clinical study," he said carefully. "If someone is claiming otherwise, I would like to know."

"That's why I'm asking."

Ward looked toward the locked cabinet.

"So would I."

* * *

Ye Xuan went home through a light drizzle. The streets reflected the traffic lights in long colored streaks, and every passing bus left a wake of dirty water.

He counted the lessons of the day.

The fragments were broken. Their patterns had matching missing edges. Ordinary instruments registered only shadows of the structure. Ward was frightened by a connection he could not yet prove.

And the copper box had warmed when the samples arrived.

That last fact disturbed him most.

If Seed Zero recognized the fragments, perhaps the fragments recognized it.

Halfway down Mott Street, he felt a second pulse through his bag.

The first had been a warning. This one felt like an answer.

He stopped beneath a shop awning and pressed the copper box between both palms.

Inside, beyond the shield, the bell sounded a single note.

For the first time, another note replied.

It came from somewhere far away, faint enough that he could not tell whether it was a sound, a memory, or an invitation.

It ended before he could follow it.

He went upstairs to the room above the bakery and wrote one sentence beneath his sketches of the fragments:

A broken thing is still trying to become whole.
