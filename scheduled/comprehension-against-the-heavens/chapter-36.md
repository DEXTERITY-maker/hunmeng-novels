Lou had a rule about courier work: if a customer asked you to deliver a package to an address that did not exist, you called dispatch instead of getting creative.

Ye Xuan broke the rule on Monday, but only after calling Wren.

"The address exists," she said. "I can hear the elevator."

"There's no name on the building."

"It's a warehouse."

"With an elevator."

"Do you want the job or do you want to argue about architecture?"

The delivery slip said CANAL STREET, SOUTH ENTRANCE. No company name. The package was small enough to hold with one hand and had a blank cardboard sleeve instead of a shipping label.

Ye Xuan stood beneath the awning and looked at it. He could see, without opening comprehension, the faint pressure marks left by a removed sticker.

A gold sunburst.

"Tell Lou I'm bringing it back," he said.

"You're not even going in?"

"No."

A moment of silence passed over Wren's line. "Is it dangerous?"

"I don't know. That's why I'm not going in."

She said nothing for a second, then answered, "Finally. A sensible decision. I'll mark it undeliverable."

He turned away from the warehouse and walked past a row of delivery trucks, careful not to hurry.

The last truck had an unfamiliar logo on the door: a white flame inside a gray square.

He looked at it twice. Then he made himself keep moving.

* * *

Back at Skyline, Lou held the package between two fingers as though it smelled bad.

"Anonymous pickup?" he asked.

"From a customer account created yesterday," Wren said. "Paid with a card. Nothing illegal about that."

"Nothing useful about it, either." Lou set it in a metal tray. "We don't play spy games. Send it back through the dispatcher, with a picture of the address and the time."

Tank came down the stairs carrying a bicycle wheel. He listened to the story and frowned.

"Maybe somebody just put the wrong label on it."

"Maybe," Ye Xuan said.

He hoped Tank was right.

Before leaving he asked Wren if there had been any more unfamiliar vehicles near the garage. Her expression changed immediately.

"Dark SUV, tinted windows? Twice. One of them circled the block yesterday. I heard the tires crunch the same patch of broken glass."

"How do you know it was the same car?"

"Because one of its wheels has a flat spot. Very annoying."

That was the sort of detail people forgot Wren could hear. She could not read license plates through walls, but ordinary machines told their stories without knowing they were talking.

"Did it stop?"

"Not here."

"Where?"

"Three blocks west. At the lights. Then it went away." She tilted her head. "Why?"

Ye Xuan did not answer immediately.

He thought about the green fleece jacket at the Pit, the woman with the phone, and the copper box whose presence Wren had learned to hear as absence.

"I think somebody's looking for a thing they can't find," he said. "And they're watching the people who might have it."

* * *

Priya had given him a list of precautions, most of them ordinary and none of them comforting: preserve messages, don't follow strangers, don't meet alone, and don't treat a suspicious delivery as permission to investigate private property.

He obeyed the last instruction.

Instead he checked public information about the warehouse. The property company had been registered six months after the Glow. A shell company owned the property company. The director of the shell company was a lawyer who also represented a Halcyon supplier.

Not proof. Just another line on the map.

His school assignment waited on the table. Ward had asked for a comparison of three ceramic composites under heat. He should have been studying coefficients and thermal tolerances. Instead he was studying corporations.

*If my second life turns into accounting,* he thought, *I will demand a refund.*

He closed the browser and returned to the assignment.

His notes were good. His grades were improving. The professor had confirmed the assistant position in writing, and the first payroll date was next week.

Progress came in small amounts. Three hundred sixty dollars a week. Five hundred for a fight. A repaired spectrometer. A plate that kept a signal from traveling.

He was beginning to believe he could build a life here.

Then the phone rang.

* * *

"Mr. Ye?" a polite male voice asked. "Daniel Avery. Talent Partnerships, Halcyon Dynamics."

Ye Xuan stopped writing.

"We met in spirit at an event in Red Hook," the voice continued. "I wanted to congratulate you on a remarkable performance."

"How did you get my number?"

A pause, no longer than a breath. "Our team works with several community programs. I hope the call isn't inconvenient."

"You didn't answer my question."

"Fair. Your contact information came from a commercial list associated with athletic registrations. If it isn't appropriate to call, I'll remove it."

Ye Xuan knew what that sentence sounded like: a rehearsed explanation built to be difficult to dispute.

"Please remove it."

"Of course. But may I ask one question?"

"You just did."

A short laugh. "Are you interested in earning more than five hundred dollars for an evening's work?"

Ye Xuan looked at the stack of tuition notices beside his notebook.

"Not if the job involves giving you my blood."

The silence on the line was suddenly very precise.

"I wasn't going to ask for that," Avery said.

"No?"

"Not on the first call."

It was meant to be a joke. That made it worse.

Ye Xuan ended the conversation.

He saved the number and wrote down the time, exactly as Priya had instructed. Then he spent five minutes sitting still, letting his heartbeat return to normal.

* * *

On Tuesday morning, he left a message for Priya. He did not send the package story or the call recording through anything she had told him was unsafe. Instead he wrote: SAME SCOUT. DIRECT CONTACT. CAN MEET IN PUBLIC.

Her answer arrived two hours later.

DO NOT GO TO ANY MEETING HE ARRANGES.

A second message followed.

ALSO FINISH YOUR COURSEWORK.

He stared at it.

Somehow, even while suspended, Priya had found a way to be more annoying than a professor.

He opened his lab notebook.

* * *

Across town, in a hospital room with a view of a wall, Rocco Vitale sat with his injured arm in a brace.

The man who visited him wore a black coat despite the warm day. He brought no flowers, no fruit, and no get-well card.

Rocco knew what kind of visitor did that.

"You work for Halcyon?" he asked.

The man pulled up a chair. "People call me Ash."

Rocco tried to laugh. It came out badly.

"What do you want?"

"A boy who lives above a bakery."

"I don't know him."

Ash regarded the brace, then the half-healed bruising beneath Rocco's collar.

"I didn't ask if you knew him."

Rocco licked his lips. "He looks at you. That's all. He looks, and suddenly he knows what to break."

"Does he carry anything?"

"Maybe. A little tin box. He kept touching his pocket."

For the first time, the visitor's expression changed.

"A box."

"Yeah."

Ash stood. The air around him grew a degree colder, and the cup of water beside Rocco's bed filmed with condensation.

"Thank you," he said.

At the door he paused.

"What's the bakery called?"

Rocco closed his eyes.

"The Golden Lotus," he whispered.
