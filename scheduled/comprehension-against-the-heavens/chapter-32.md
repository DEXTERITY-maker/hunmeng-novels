Ye Xuan had survived the Pit, federal questioning, and a man who could bend sound into pain.

At eight thirty on Monday morning, he discovered that an engineering lecture in English was a different kind of disaster.

The professor spoke quickly, wrote more quickly, and had the distressing habit of erasing an equation at the precise moment Ye Xuan began to understand the words around it.

He understood the equation immediately. Differential equations did not care which language you spoke.

The joke the professor made while erasing it defeated him completely.

Forty students laughed.

Ye Xuan looked at the empty board and added another rule to his notebook.

Rule six: a joke is information without a user manual.

His neighbor, a girl with a stack of color-coded folders, leaned closer. "You okay, Ye?"

"Yes."

"You've been gone a while."

"Personal problems."

"Hospital?"

"Something like that."

She nodded in a way that suggested his answer explained more than it did. Her name was Nora; she reminded him that a lab assessment was due next week and that Professor Ward had posted a list of open assistant positions.

"Nobody wants the materials lab," she said. "The equipment's ancient, and Ward grades like he's being paid by the disappointment."

"How ancient?"

"Ancient enough to have opinions."

Ye Xuan sat a little straighter.

* * *

The materials laboratory smelled of warm plastic, alcohol wipes, and dust trapped inside expensive machines. Most equipment was perfectly ordinary. Two benches were marked OUT OF SERVICE, and one of those held a spectrometer whose protective casing had been opened and closed so often that the screws were polished bright.

A notice taped to its monitor read:

DO NOT ATTEMPT TO RESTART. CALIBRATION FAILED.

Ye Xuan read the notice. Then he read the repair log left beside it.

The log described replacement sensors, updated firmware, a new light source, and three visits by technicians who had found no fault with the hardware. He looked through the open casing without activating his sight, tracing the path from source to sample to detector.

The optical path was misaligned by a fraction.

"Can I help you?"

The voice came from behind him. It belonged to a tall man in a faded cardigan, carrying a paper cup of coffee and looking as if he had not slept since the last semester.

"Professor Ward?"

"That depends. Are you here to submit late coursework or break my last working machine?"

"Neither, I hope."

Ward put down his coffee. "That's a rare answer in this building."

"My name is Ye Xuan. I'm enrolled in your materials section. There was a note about a lab assistant."

Ward glanced at the machine. "Experience?"

"Some."

"With optical spectroscopy?"

"I can learn."

"That is what they all say. The machine has been broken for six weeks. Do you know why?"

Ye Xuan almost said yes. He had not opened his sight yet. He knew only the surface of the problem.

"I think the reference mirror is shifting with temperature," he said instead. "The calibration routine assumes the arm stays fixed."

Ward's eyebrows moved.

"Show me."

* * *

There was a narrow window of time in which a professor's curiosity outweighed his caution. Ye Xuan recognized it and used it carefully.

He did not touch a live circuit. He unplugged the housing, waited for the warning light to die, and asked where the service diagram was stored. Ward produced one from a cabinet full of notebooks.

With the diagram spread between them, Ye Xuan found the mirror mount.

It looked perfect to ordinary sight. The bolt was seated, the adhesive intact, the optical axis straight.

He opened comprehension just enough to see how heat traveled across the assembly.

A strip of greenish light pulsed beneath the mount. Someone had replaced the original insulating spacer with a cheaper material. The new spacer expanded at a different rate from the steel bracket. A tiny movement accumulated as the lamp warmed.

"You see the spacer?" Ye Xuan asked.

Ward bent closer. "I replaced that myself."

"With polymer?"

"A common substitute. Same dimensions."

"Different thermal expansion."

Ward stared at him for two seconds. Then he said, "Hand me the parts catalogue."

They located the proper ceramic spacer in a drawer of discarded service components. Ye Xuan fitted it with a torque wrench under Ward's supervision, restored the housing, and waited while the spectrometer warmed to operating temperature.

Calibration completed.

The monitor displayed a clean baseline.

Ward set his cup down very slowly. "I dislike being surprised by students."

"Should I apologize?"

"Not for that." Ward pulled a chair over and sat. "Do you know what a graduate assistant earns here?"

"No."

"Eighteen dollars an hour. Twenty hours a week during term, if payroll can approve the paperwork. The work isn't glamorous."

Ye Xuan calculated: three hundred sixty dollars a week before taxes. It did not solve nine thousand four hundred dollars in thirty days, but it was money earned without being followed through Red Hook by a dark SUV.

"I can live without glamorous."

"Good. Most of my students cannot." Ward studied him. "Your attendance record is terrible. Your previous lab marks were adequate, not remarkable. What changed?"

There was the question again. You look different. You know different things. What changed?

"I had time to think," Ye Xuan said.

Ward considered that. "Perhaps you should do more of it."

* * *

During lunch, Ye Xuan found a quiet staircase and called Lou.

"I can take evening shifts," he said. "Not mornings. I got a position at the university."

"A university position?" Lou said. "Does it pay?"

"Eighteen an hour."

"Then don't be stupid. Take it. We have enough idiots riding bicycles for me." A pause. "You staying out of trouble?"

"Mostly."

"I'll take mostly. Be here Thursday."

Juno texted three minutes later.

WREN SAYS YOU MADE A BOX THAT HURTS HER EARS LESS.

Ye Xuan typed: THAT IS THE IDEA.

She replied: WHY DIDN'T YOU TELL ME?

Because if I tell you everything, you'll have to carry it, he wrote, then erased the sentence.

Instead: I WANTED TO BE SURE IT WORKED.

A long pause. Then: YOU'RE STILL AN IDIOT.

He smiled at the screen.

* * *

Ward asked him to return after his afternoon class to prepare sample trays. Ye Xuan worked at the bench while the professor sorted research papers and spoke to somebody on the phone behind the glass door of his office.

"That shipment is not supposed to arrive until Friday," Ward said. "If your people want an expedited analysis, they can explain the chain of custody. Yes, I know who signed it. No, that doesn't change the protocol."

He hung up, emerged, and found Ye Xuan labeling the trays in neat, careful script.

"Anything else, Professor?"

Ward hesitated. His hand rested on a black case file bearing a small golden sunburst.

"No," he said. "Not today."

He slid the file under a pile of journals.

Ye Xuan kept his eyes on the trays.

The professor had offered him a job. That did not make the professor trustworthy. It also did not make him an enemy.

He had learned something in the Pit: you could lose by assuming a person was only the gift you saw.

He gathered his notes, checked the copper box in his bag, and prepared to leave.

"Ye," Ward said.

"Yes?"

"Never use a machine just because it gives you a result. Ask what the machine thinks it has measured."

Ye Xuan looked at the spectrometer, whose green status light had remained steady for two hours.

It was excellent advice.

Unfortunately, the black file under the journals was marked with the same sunburst as the card from Mama Rocha.

And Ye Xuan had the growing suspicion that Professor Ward's next shipment would be measuring something no spectrometer ought to see.
