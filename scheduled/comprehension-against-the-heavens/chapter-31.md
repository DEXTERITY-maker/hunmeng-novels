The trouble with hiding a stone that could whisper through steel was that the stone never appeared to be doing anything.

Ye Xuan discovered this at one seventeen in the morning, sitting beneath a flickering fluorescent tube in Hudson Polytechnic's materials laboratory. On his left lay three lengths of copper wire, two cracked ceramic tiles, a soldering iron, and the tin box that had failed to keep Seed Zero quiet. On his right lay a sheet of copper the size of a paperback, purchased with money he should have been saving for tuition.

He had written KROLL on one page of his notebook. Underneath it he had drawn a chain.

It was not an accurate drawing. He was not ready to put what he had seen inside the federal agent's spine into metal. But the dark-gold links had taught him something about containment. Every link took a portion of the force and passed the rest to the next. A chain could distribute a burden.

A box, Ye Xuan thought, should distribute a signal.

"Rule five," he murmured. "If you don't know how to turn something off, teach the world not to hear it."

The soldering iron hissed against the copper. A bead of silver gathered, ran, and cooled in precisely the wrong place.

"Excellent," he told the empty room. "I have invented expensive trash."

He scraped it away and tried again.

* * *

The campus was almost deserted on Sunday night. His student card had opened the engineering building; it had also reminded him that his name belonged to someone who had once understood the door codes and the faces in these halls. He had walked past a photograph of a graduating robotics team and felt nothing when he recognized the same face wearing his name in the corner.

He could learn a machine's history from its scratches. Not a dead boy's.

That fact still bothered him more than the death itself.

He opened his sight. The copper plate became a woven plain of pale orange tracks, conducting heat and current along paths that science could describe. Behind them lay finer paths, paths that did not belong to electricity at all. The copper accepted qi reluctantly, like a stranger accepting a package without a return address.

He pressed one thread into the first line he had etched.

Pain tightened behind his eyes.

The line lit, then broke. The energy leaked into the soldering iron and made its indicator blink three times. Across the laboratory, a clock reset itself.

Ye Xuan turned the power off.

"A measurable failure," he said. "Those are the best kind."

He adjusted the pattern, not the voltage. The mistake was in the angles. He had arranged the copper like a cage when he needed it to be a current divider. Instead of forcing Seed Zero's pulse to stop, the plate should redirect the pulse into the metal and let it fade through a broad surface.

Three failed plates and two hours later, his fingers were black with oxide. The fourth looked almost embarrassingly plain: concentric broken circles, each gap facing a different direction, with a thin line crossing the center.

He set the stone's tin beneath it.

The bell came at once.

Not from the stone. From beneath the world, as always. A low note with no beginning, which he felt through his teeth and ribs before his ears could decide what to do with it.

He pushed qi into the center line.

The copper drank the pulse. Orange became blue, blue became a color that had no name in the visible spectrum, and for a single breath the pattern seemed to hang in the air above the plate.

Then the bell stopped.

Ye Xuan sat perfectly still.

The silence was different from ordinary silence. Ordinary silence contained the motor in the ventilation duct, a distant elevator, a truck on the street. This was a gap in attention, a small place the universe had forgotten to listen to.

He lifted the plate. The bell returned, painfully clear. He lowered it. Nothing.

He laughed once, so sharply that the empty room gave the laugh back to him.

"Finally."

* * *

He should have gone home. Instead he spent forty minutes testing limits, because a solution that worked only while nobody touched it was not a solution.

The plate had to sit close to the tin. Turned sideways, it failed. Moved more than a handspan away, it failed. When he tried to strengthen it with extra qi, a hairline fracture spread from the central line.

He stopped before it broke.

A shield that depended on his constant attention would fail the first time he fainted, and he had already developed an unfortunate habit of fainting in public.

He soldered the plate into a shallow holder, wrapped the edges in cloth to keep them from touching the lid, and marked the safe orientation with a scratched arrow. The qi pattern settled into the copper and stayed there after he withdrew his hand.

He could still see the stone. He could still feel a faint pressure when he looked directly at it. He could not hear the bell.

That was enough.

On his way out, he passed a security camera above the lab door. He stopped under it and considered the glowing circuits inside its plastic shell.

He could disable it. Easily.

He didn't. The previous Ye Xuan was a student with overdue tuition, not a burglar. A camera record of him entering a classroom was a boring thing. A missing record, discovered by somebody already watching him, could become interesting.

He walked out beneath its steady red light.

* * *

At six, Skyline smelled of wet jackets, engine grease, and Lou's coffee. Wren sat cross-legged on a chair with her headphones crooked over one ear, dispatching three couriers simultaneously while playing a puzzle game on her tablet.

Ye Xuan placed the tin on the counter.

Wren looked up. "What's that?"

"Something I want you to listen to."

"I listen to things for money."

"Then this is a professional consultation. My budget is one pastry."

"Two pastries."

"One and a half."

"Deal."

She took off her headphones and closed her eyes.

He waited.

Wren could hear a heartbeat through an apartment wall, the click of a key three floors below, the shift of gears from a bicycle around the corner. Her gift spread through the neighborhood like transparent threads touching everything that moved.

She frowned.

"That's weird."

"What is?"

"Everything's there." She pointed at the street. "The truck. Lou yelling at the espresso machine. Tank walking up the stairs." She pointed at the tin. "Except that. It isn't quiet. It's like my ears go around it."

Ye Xuan felt cold despite the heat of the garage.

"Could you hear it yesterday?"

"Yesterday I could hear something in your pocket that made my teeth ache. I thought I was getting sick." Wren opened one eye. "Did you fix it?"

"For now."

She reached toward the tin. He moved it away.

"Not safe to touch."

"Well, that's comforting." She leaned back. "You know what's worse? Until you put it there, I didn't notice I couldn't hear it."

Ye Xuan looked at the little box, copper hidden beneath cloth and solder.

A shield could stop a listener. It could also teach a listener where to look, if the listener already knew what absence meant.

* * *

When he left Skyline, he did not go straight to Mott Street. He took the stairs instead of the elevator, crossed Canal twice, and stopped beside a shuttered flower shop to watch the traffic.

No gray SUV. No man in a green fleece.

He touched the arrow scratched into the copper holder, feeling its sharp little ridge through the cloth.

For the first time since Pier 17, he had something he had made that held its shape against Seed Zero.

He should have felt safe.

Instead he remembered Kroll standing up from a chair that hurt him, and Wren saying she could hear everything except the place his box occupied.

Some machines were built to make signals.

Others were built to find the places where signals should have been.

Across the street, a delivery van turned a corner and vanished. Ye Xuan closed his notebook before the wind could lift the page marked KROLL.

Tomorrow he would go back to class. He had a broken education to repair, nine thousand four hundred dollars to find, and a copper plate that might keep the wrong people from knocking on Mrs. Tang's door.

It was a remarkably ordinary list of problems, considering the stone in his pocket.

Then the plate warmed once beneath his fingers.

Not enough to sing. Just enough to remind him that something on the other side had tried.
