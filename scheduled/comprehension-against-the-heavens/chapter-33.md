Juno's message arrived while Ye Xuan was scraping solder from his fingernails.

MATEO'S HAVING TROUBLE BREATHING. CAN YOU COME?

There was no joke, no insult, not even the usual punctuation she used when she wanted him to understand she was angry.

He called immediately.

She answered on the second ring. He could hear a child coughing in the background, a harsh sound that ended in a thin whistle.

"Carmen's with him," Juno said. Her voice was tight. "We've given him the medicine the doctor prescribed. He's still struggling. I called for help."

"Then stay with him. I'm coming."

"Ye, don't—"

"I'm coming."

He ran.

* * *

The train to the Bronx was delayed. On the platform, Ye Xuan measured the distance across the city, the energy in his dantian, and the limits of his legs.

None of the numbers made him faster than a train.

He waited four agonizing minutes, rode twenty-three more, and took the stairs three at a time when he reached Juno's stop. His chest hurt by the time he found the brick apartment building, but he kept the qi he had left carefully gathered. It would do no good to arrive exhausted and useless.

Juno met him at the door of the third-floor apartment.

Without her courier jacket she looked younger. Her hair was pulled into a loose braid; she had a wet patch on one sleeve where someone had clung to her.

"Paramedics are on the way," she said. "Carmen's keeping him upright. We have a plan for attacks. This one's worse."

He followed her into the living room.

A boy of nine sat on the sofa, leaning forward with his elbows on his knees. His skin was pale, his breathing fast and uneven. A woman with tired eyes held one hand against his back and spoke to him quietly. The woman's other hand rested near the prescribed inhaler and spacer on the table.

"Mateo," Juno said. "This is my friend Ye."

The boy tried to speak. Only a breath came out.

"Don't worry about talking," Ye Xuan said, kneeling where the boy could see him. "I'm not a doctor. Your aunt and the paramedics are taking care of the important part."

Carmen gave him one sharp look, judged his honesty, and returned her attention to Mateo.

Ye Xuan opened his sight.

* * *

Living patterns were harder than metal. A body was not one system but millions of systems arguing, cooperating, compensating. The threads in Mateo's chest flickered in a hurried, tangled rhythm. His airways were narrowing, and the muscles around them worked too hard. Ye Xuan saw old scars, places that had been inflamed many times.

He could not fix asthma by wishing a pattern into obedience. If he pushed qi through tissue he did not fully understand, the energy might make the inflammation worse. The knowledge hit him with such force that he almost closed his sight.

Then he noticed something stranger.

A faint cold imbalance lay beneath the ordinary human patterns, too regular to be an illness and too weak to be an Awakened gift. It shimmered around the scarred places without belonging to them, like dust caught in the wrong gears.

There was no time to study it.

"Juno," he said. "Talk to him. Let him hear you."

She knelt beside the sofa. "Hey, rat. Remember the roof on our old building? The one with the painted stars?"

Mateo nodded weakly.

"You said we'd count them together when the city lights went out. You remember?"

Another nod.

Ye Xuan kept a tiny thread of qi in his own chest, using it only to steady his attention and avoid collapsing from comprehension. He did not put it into Mateo's lungs. He followed the boy's breathing instead, speaking gently between the efforts.

"You're not alone. Listen to your sister. Help is coming."

For the first time, he understood that power could be useful not because it gave you an answer, but because it showed you exactly how much you didn't know.

* * *

The paramedics arrived carrying oxygen equipment and a monitor. Carmen handed them the medication information and described the attack, its onset, and what had already been given. Ye Xuan stood back.

Juno's grip found his wrist and stayed there as the crew assessed Mateo and began treatment.

The minutes crawled.

Eventually Mateo's breathing eased enough for him to look at Ye Xuan properly. He stared at the copper-wrapped box visible in Ye Xuan's jacket pocket.

"Are you a hero?" he whispered.

"No."

"Juno says you're weird."

"That part is true."

The boy attempted a smile.

One of the paramedics spoke to Carmen about further evaluation. She agreed at once. There would be no argument about whether the child needed medical care; not tonight.

Juno went with Mateo. Carmen followed after gathering his things. Ye Xuan remained in the apartment for a minute, standing among the little signs of a life interrupted: homework on the table, a half-built model spaceship, a cereal bowl with a spoon in it.

On the refrigerator was a photograph of Juno at perhaps fourteen, arms around Mateo when he was still a toddler. Beside it hung an envelope stamped with a golden sunburst.

HALCYON DYNAMICS.

He didn't touch it.

* * *

Carmen returned after arranging a ride and found him still looking at the refrigerator.

"They mailed that last week," she said. "Some program. Free treatment for kids with serious asthma. Juno says not to trust them."

"What program?"

"Respira." Carmen pronounced it carefully. "They say they're testing a new therapy. Transportation covered. All the appointments covered." She folded her arms. "When you can't afford to miss work, free starts to sound like a miracle."

Ye Xuan thought about the cold, alien shimmer in Mateo's airways.

"Has he been in any studies before?"

"No. We haven't signed anything." She looked tired, and angry at herself for being tired. "A woman from Halcyon keeps calling. Very nice voice. Very understanding. She knows which insurance forms we've been fighting."

"That's a lot to know about a family you've never met."

"Exactly."

He took a photograph of the letter only after Carmen asked him to help check what it meant. The consent form mentioned an inhaled compound, long-term observation, and proprietary research materials. It named several oversight committees but did not give a plain-language description of the compound.

He could not prove anything from that alone.

He knew enough to be afraid.

* * *

Outside the hospital later, Juno sat on a bench beneath a bus shelter with her elbows on her knees. It was almost midnight. Her braid had come loose.

"He's stable," she said before Ye Xuan could ask. "They're keeping him for observation."

"Good."

She stared at the road. "I hate this part."

"What part?"

"Waiting while somebody else decides whether he's going to be okay." She swallowed. "I can jump across an avenue. I can catch a falling kid. I can't make my brother take a breath."

Ye Xuan sat beside her.

He did not tell her he could cure Mateo. He could not.

"I saw something when I looked at him," he said. "Not a normal gift. I don't know what it is."

She turned sharply. "What?"

"Something faint in his lungs. I need information before I even think about touching it. And I want to understand this Respira program."

Juno looked down at her hands. "They called again this morning. They said the trial might be his best chance."

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd think about it."

He let the words settle between them. He had no right to demand she refuse a treatment he could not replace. But he had seen the gold sunburst on the letter, the case in Ward's office, the scout recording him in the Pit.

There were too many lines pointing toward the same place.

"I'll find out what I can," he said.

Juno nodded, still watching the hospital doors.

From his pocket, the copper box gave a single, sharp pulse of warmth.

Ye Xuan closed his hand around it. He had built the plate to hide a stone from people hunting it.

Now he needed to know why a company that hunted stones wanted to put something into the lungs of a nine-year-old boy.
